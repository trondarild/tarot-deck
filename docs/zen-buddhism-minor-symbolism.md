# Zen deck: symbolism of the minor cards

The minor series has 75 cards in five suits, one for each of the Japanese five elements (*godai*): Earth (地), Water (水), Fire (火), Wind (風) and Void (空). The structure is set out in the proposal in `zen-buddhism-research.md`, and the major cards it refers to are described in `zen-buddhism-symbolism.md`.

Each suit has ten pip cards and five rank cards. Pip *n* is ox-herding stage *n*, major card *n*, played out in the suit's domain: Earth is the body, sitting and daily work; Water is feeling, relationship and the tea way; Fire is effort, doubt and koan work; Wind is breath, movement and speech; Void is awareness itself. In a reading, a pip card points back to the major card with its number.

The rank cards take the place of court cards. Each shows a person in the suit's element at one of Dongshan Liangjie's Five Ranks (五位), which describe how the absolute and the relative stand to each other: the relative within the absolute (正中偏), the absolute within the relative (偏中正), coming from within the absolute (正中來), arriving at mutual integration (兼中至) and unity attained (兼中到). They are degrees of realisation, not social rank, and their use as court cards is a design choice of this deck.

Pip entries give the stage, a suggested image, the meaning, a reading, a pitfall and links. Rank entries give the rank, the figure, the meaning, a reading, a pitfall and links. Image suggestions are notes for future artwork in the deck's ink-wash style.

## One of Earth

**Stage.** This is the first ox-herding stage in the domain of the body: the search for a place to sit. The seeker tries chairs, cushions, benches and postures, and expects one of them to bring the stillness he has read about.

**Image.** A round black cushion on a bare wooden floor in early grey light. A pair of straw sandals lies at the threshold, and a single sliding door stands open on a garden of wet moss.

**Meaning.** Restlessness in the body, and the first decision to give it a place.

**In a reading.** A search for stability that has not yet found its ground. The card marks a person who wants to settle and has not decided where. It suggests choosing one place and one hour.

**Pitfall.** Treating the search as research: comparing cushions, retreats and teachers while the sitting itself is postponed. Dōgen's *Fukanzazengi* asks for a quiet room and a seat.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the same restlessness, shown here as the body that will not stay still. Seven of Water: the settled feeling that this search is looking for.

## Two of Earth

**Stage.** The seeker finds the traces of sitting. He reads an account of posture, notices a stiff knee after the first attempt, and sees a hollow in a cushion that someone else has used. These are signs that a practice exists and that bodies have settled before his.

**Image.** A well-worn cushion with a deep impression at its centre, and beside it an open manual with a drawing of crossed legs.

**Meaning.** Evidence from outside and from the body that sitting works. The card names instruction, imitation and the first aches.

**In a reading.** Early signs that a discipline is within reach. The card suggests following the instructions as written and noting what the body reports.

**Pitfall.** Mistaking knowledge of posture for the posture. A person can describe the angle of the spine, the position of the hands and the line of the gaze, and never sit for more than a minute.

**Links.** II — Seeing the Traces: the footprints here are a hollow in a cushion and a page of instructions. Two of Wind: the breath, which the manual mentions next.

## Three of Earth

**Stage.** In the third stage the ox is seen directly. Here a sitting arrives in which the body settles of its own accord: the weight drops into the hips, the spine rises without effort, and the floor feels like a support. It lasts a few minutes.

**Image.** A figure seated in profile beside a window at dusk, shoulders level, hands resting in the lap. A square of last light lies across the knees.

**Meaning.** A first experience of stability that did not need to be made. The card names the moment when sitting stops being a task.

**In a reading.** A brief, real steadiness has appeared and should be kept without being inflated. The card suggests returning to the cushion the next day as before.

**Pitfall.** Setting out to repeat the sitting, which makes the body tense in search of the memory. The settled posture arrives while attention is elsewhere.

**Links.** III — Seeing the Ox: a glimpse, here felt in the hips and spine. Four of Fire: the effort that must now hold what has been seen.

## Four of Earth

**Stage.** The ox is caught by the discipline of keeping to the schedule. The seeker sits at the same hour each day, through cold mornings, sleepiness and the pain of the legs. Posture is held when the mind wants to shift, and the alarm is obeyed.

**Image.** A man seated before dawn in an unheated hall, breath visible, a lit stick of incense burned halfway down. His knees are bound by a cloth belt, and a lantern hangs at the door.

**Meaning.** Firm commitment to the body's discipline. The card names regularity enforced against inclination.

**In a reading.** A resolve is being tested by routine. The card calls for a fixed schedule kept without bargaining, in training, in work or in the care of the body. It favours endurance over variety.

**Pitfall.** Severity. Sitting through pain as a contest of will turns the cushion into a place of endurance, and the injured knee becomes a trophy. Dōgen describes zazen as the dharma gate of repose and bliss, which excludes grim effort.

**Links.** IV — Catching the Ox: the rope is a fixed hour and a fixed posture. Five of Earth: the same discipline once it has become routine.

## Five of Earth

**Stage.** The ox is herded by routine. Sitting, meals, cleaning and sleep follow one another in a fixed order, and the days take on a shape. The posture needs less force, but it still needs tending, and lapses are corrected when noticed.

**Image.** A temple corridor with a bucket, a rag and a row of shoes set neatly on a step. A monk kneels at one end, wiping the boards in long strokes, and the grain of the wood shows through the water.

**Meaning.** Practice sustained by order. The card names the unglamorous middle of any work.

**In a reading.** Steady, ordinary progress made by regular habit. The card favours maintenance, repetition and small repairs over any new start, and it suggests that the schedule is the achievement.

**Pitfall.** Rigidity. A routine can become the thing guarded, with the person anxious when it breaks and proud when it holds. The tether is meant to become unnecessary.

**Links.** V — Herding the Ox: the string and the whip are the daily schedule. Eight of Water: a looser form of the same attention, in the tea room.

## Six of Earth

**Stage.** The seeker rides the ox home. Sitting no longer feels like work against the body, and the body returns the favour: sleep is sound, the back is straight and the legs have stopped complaining. The cushion is a familiar place rather than an assignment.

**Image.** A broad-shouldered monk seated under a pine in the evening, eyes half open, a flute lying across his lap. A path behind him leads down to a village with lit windows.

**Meaning.** Ease in the body after a long discipline. The card names the comfort of a practice that has become habitual.

**In a reading.** Effort is rewarded with comfort, and the foundations are sound. The card suggests enjoying the stability without examining it too closely. It can name a settled home, a healthy period or a skill that has become easy.

**Pitfall.** Contentment. The pleasant sitting becomes the purpose, and the cushion is visited for its comfort. The ox is still under the rider.

**Links.** VI — Riding the Ox Home: the ride is the body at ease in its posture. Three of Wind: the walk that follows a long sit.

## Seven of Earth

**Stage.** The ox is forgotten and the man remains. The seeker puts down the rope: he stops counting breaths, stops checking his posture and stops measuring his sittings. The practice goes on in the body without supervision, in daily work as much as on the cushion.

**Image.** A thatched hut in morning light. A coil of rope hangs unused on a nail, and a man sits on the veranda with his back to the room, sipping from a bowl.

**Meaning.** A method has done its work and can be set down. The card names the end of self-monitoring.

**In a reading.** A discipline has become part of the person and no longer needs watching. The card suggests that rules once kept by effort can be relaxed. It asks what remains when the scaffolding comes down.

**Pitfall.** Pride in having let go. The one who no longer checks still thinks of himself as the one who sits, and that image remains.

**Links.** VII — The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone: the rope is dropped and the sitter stays. Six of Void: the awareness that does not need a posture.

## Eight of Earth

**Stage.** Man and ox are both gone. In sitting, there is no longer a sitter with a body to be placed. Dōgen's phrase *shinjin datsuraku*, the dropping off of body and mind, belongs to this stage, as the tradition tells it of his awakening under Rujing. The hall remains, and no one occupies it.

**Image.** An empty zendo at dawn with a single cushion in the centre, undented, and a wide circle of pale light on the floor where the sun enters. No figure appears.

**Meaning.** Sitting without a subject. The card names the dissolving of the body as a possession.

**In a reading.** A phase in which roles and ground alike seem to give way. The card can mean relief, loss of footing or a pause with no content.

**Pitfall.** Treating the quiet as the goal. Hakuin warned of the dead stillness, the cave of the black mountain, in which sitting goes on with no change in life.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the empty circle, found here in the floor of the hall. One of Void: the awareness that remains in the absence.

## Nine of Earth

**Stage.** The seeker returns to the source and finds the ground as it was. The floor is boards, the cushion is cloth, the body is flesh, and none of them needs to be made into anything. Sitting, sweeping and eating are seen as they are.

**Image.** A wooden floor, a window, a broom leaning on the wall, and a bowl on the sill. Beyond the glass a river runs through red maples. No person appears.

**Meaning.** The plain world, seen after its dissolution. The card names things restored to themselves.

**In a reading.** Clear sight of an ordinary situation, with nothing to add. The card suggests that a home, a body or a task already holds what was looked for elsewhere. It marks the return of confidence in what is near.

**Pitfall.** Quietism. The clear room is enjoyed and the world outside is left to itself. The sitter who has returned to the source is still asked to open the door.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: the river and the maples are the water and flowers of that card. Nine of Wind: the speech that can now describe things as they are.

## Ten of Earth

**Stage.** The seeker goes out among people with the settled body as his only tool. He cooks, carries, mends and digs. The sitting he does at home shows in the way he works: unhurried, attentive and fit for the task.

**Image.** A barefoot cook at a market stall, sleeves tied back, handing a bowl of rice to a labourer. A basket of radishes sits at his feet, and a child watches from under the awning.

**Meaning.** Daily work done well, among others, with open hands. The card names usefulness without display.

**In a reading.** Practical service in an ordinary setting. The card points to work in the household, a trade or a community, and to labour that needs no recognition. It also closes a round, and the next card begins the search again.

**Pitfall.** Ostentatious humility, or the holiness of someone who cleans in front of an audience. Dōgen's *Tenzo kyōkun* asks the cook to handle the rice and vegetables as his own eyes, which leaves no room for performance.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the market here is a kitchen and a street. Ten of Water: the same giving, in the tea room.

## First Rank of Earth

**Rank.** *Shō chū hen* (正中偏), the relative within the absolute. In the element of Earth it is the first sight of solid things as empty: the body, the floor and the mountain are seen to be without a fixed nature, and the mind lies down in that.

**Figure.** A recluse seated in a mountain hollow, eyes closed, wrapped in a grey robe. Moss has crept over his knees, and rock and sitter share one outline.

**Meaning.** Emptiness realised first, in the stillness of the body. The card names a person for whom the world has gone quiet.

**In a reading.** A withdrawn, composed person, or a period of retreat from ordinary affairs. The card gives credit for clarity and watches for the point where clarity stops.

**Pitfall.** Hakuin warned that this rank can become a trap, the dead sitting in the cave of the black mountain. The seat is comfortable, and the comfort keeps the sitter there.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the emptiness that this figure has reached and settled in. IV — Catching the Ox: the discipline that brought him to the cushion and that he may no longer need to keep.

## Second Rank of Earth

**Rank.** *Hen chū shō* (偏中正), the absolute within the relative. In Earth it is the recognition of emptiness in solid particulars: the weight of a bowl, the grain of a table, the warmth of a floor in the sun.

**Figure.** A potter in a workshop, hands wet with clay, looking at a bowl on the wheel. Shelves of finished and unfinished work stand behind, each piece slightly different. The expression is attentive and calm.

**Meaning.** The ultimate found in what is nearest. The card names a person who sees the whole in a single object.

**In a reading.** Careful attention to particulars, and the sense that each carries more than it seems. The card favours craft, study of detail and respect for materials. It can name a person who teaches by the way things are made.

**Pitfall.** Aestheticism. Beauty found in a bowl becomes a taste to be cultivated, and the potter prizes his own perception above the clay.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: the world seen as it is, in its particulars. II — Seeing the Traces: here the traces are the objects themselves.

## Third Rank of Earth

**Rank.** *Shō chū rai* (正中來), coming from within the absolute. In Earth, action arises from an emptiness that holds the body steady: the hands do what the work asks, and no self stands between the task and the doing.

**Figure.** A builder on a half-finished roof, fitting a beam, with scaffolding and tools laid in order around him. Below, villagers hold the ropes. He looks at the joint and not at the onlookers.

**Meaning.** Work that comes out of stillness. The card names a person who acts without hesitation from a settled ground.

**In a reading.** Action taken from a stable position, with steadiness rather than force. The card suggests that the moment for building has come and that the foundation is sufficient. It can name a competent organiser or a calm presence in a crisis.

**Pitfall.** Tireless doing. The builder who cannot stop has mistaken productivity for the ground it came from, and the stillness is used up.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: action for others that costs the actor nothing. V — Herding the Ox: the discipline now so well absorbed that it is invisible.

## Fourth Rank of Earth

**Rank.** *Ken chū shi* (兼中至), arriving at mutual integration. In Earth the sitter and the worker are one: the cushion and the field, the stillness and the labour are two sides of a single practice and each supports the other.

**Figure.** A farmer with a hoe on his shoulder, walking home from the field at dusk, sitting for a moment on a stump at the edge of the path. Behind him the rows run straight to the horizon.

**Meaning.** Engagement with the world on both sides of the practice. The card names a person for whom sitting and working inform one another.

**In a reading.** A balanced life in which the foundation and the daily tasks agree. The card suggests that nothing needs to be given up in order to keep the other.

**Pitfall.** Self-satisfaction with the balance. A well-arranged life can be held up as a model, and the arrangement then becomes one more thing to protect.

**Links.** XV — Ordinary Mind: the plain manner in which the two sides cooperate. VI — Riding the Ox Home: the earlier ease, now shared with the world.

## Fifth Rank of Earth

**Rank.** *Ken chū tō* (兼中到), unity attained. In Earth it is a person who leaves no trace of sitting or of work. The body is ordinary, the day is ordinary, and nothing in the manner suggests practice or its result.

**Figure.** An old woman sweeping a yard in the early morning, with a straw broom and a bent back. Fallen leaves lie in small heaps.

**Meaning.** Completion without display. The card names a person whose attainment cannot be told from daily life.

**In a reading.** Maturity that has no need to be noticed. The card suggests that the best course is the plain one already under way, and that a quiet person may be the most settled in the room.

**Pitfall.** Pursuing the absence of a trace as a style. A cultivated plainness is itself a mark, and the tradition's phrase for it is the stench of Zen.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the sweeper and the figure in the market are one. 0 — Mu: the question that began the practice and has no further need to be asked.

## One of Water

**Stage.** Searching for the Ox, played out in feeling. A restlessness with no named object: a thirst that tea, company and conversation each fail to settle. The person moves from one relationship or mood to the next, sure that the missing thing is in the next one.

**Image.** A tea bowl stands empty on a wooden veranda in light rain. Beyond it a path runs down through mist toward a river, and no figure is visible.

**Meaning.** Longing before it knows its object. The card names the dissatisfaction that sends a person out, and the habit of taking each new feeling for an answer.

**In a reading.** An unnamed longing is moving things along. The card marks a time when company and comfort fail to satisfy, and it asks what is actually being looked for. The feeling is the beginning of a path, and it needs no immediate cure.

**Pitfall.** Chasing sensation: a new friend, a new grief, a new intensity each season, each treated as the thing itself. The opposite error is to numb the thirst so that no search starts.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the same restless seeker, here in the medium of feeling. 0 — Mu: the question under the longing, which no person or mood answers.

## Two of Water

**Stage.** Seeing the Traces, in the domain of relationship. Evidence appears that something real lies under the moods: a letter kept for years, a friend whose manner steadies a room, a ring left by a cup on the table. The person reads these signs but has not yet felt the thing they point to.

**Image.** Wet footprints cross a dry stone step and lead into a tea room. A single ripple is spreading across a stone basin in the garden outside.

**Meaning.** Early signs of connection. The card names what is learned about feeling from accounts, examples and the traces left by other people.

**In a reading.** Signs of a real bond are appearing before the bond is felt. The card suggests following them, and it warns that a description of intimacy is not intimacy. A period of reading, listening and observation is indicated.

**Pitfall.** Treating knowledge about feeling as feeling: fluent talk about empathy, attachment and boundaries from someone who has not yet sat across from another person without a script.

**Links.** II — Seeing the Traces: the footprints in the stream, here left by other people. VI — Riding the Ox Home: the ease that these first signs will eventually lead to.

## Three of Water

**Stage.** Seeing the Ox, in the stream of feeling. For a moment contact is direct: a glance across a room, a bowl of tea drunk in silence with a stranger, grief that for once carries no story. The separation between the one who feels and what is felt thins.

**Image.** Moonlight lies in a bowl of still water on a low table. A hand hovers above it, and the surface is unbroken.

**Meaning.** Immediacy in feeling. The card names the first time a feeling is met as it is, with no commentary running beside it.

**In a reading.** A moment of unguarded contact has shown what is possible. The card says the moment was real and gives no promise that it will return. It is best held loosely and not retold.

**Pitfall.** Grasping the moment: trying to reproduce the exact conditions, or announcing the experience to friends so that it hardens into an identity. Water taken up in a closed hand runs out.

**Links.** III — Seeing the Ox: the first direct sight, here arriving through the heart. IV — Catching the Ox: the effort that follows once the glimpse has gone.

## Four of Water

**Stage.** Catching the Ox, as the work of holding a strong feeling. Anger, grief or desire arrives with force, and the person must stay with it without being thrown. Attention is the rope, and the feeling pulls hard against it.

**Image.** A figure carries a full bowl across a wooden bridge in wind, both hands under it, knees bent. Water trembles at the rim and does not spill.

**Meaning.** The effort of staying present with strong emotion. The card names the stage at which feeling is neither indulged nor pushed away but held.

**In a reading.** A strong feeling is demanding steady attention. The card calls for some firmness and for staying in the room. It also notes that old reactions return quickly, and that patience with them is part of the work.

**Pitfall.** Gripping too hard. Suppression passes for discipline, and the bowl is clutched so tightly that nothing can move through it. The other error is to let the bowl drop at the first spill.

**Links.** IV — Catching the Ox: the same struggle with a wild animal, here fought in the heart. XVI — The Wild Fox: the feeling that claims to be free of consequences.

## Five of Water

**Stage.** Herding the Ox, as the daily care of relationship. The crisis has passed and what remains is upkeep: the call made on schedule, the apology offered early, the tea prepared with the same attention on an ordinary Tuesday. Rikyū's rules for tea begin with making a good bowl and laying the charcoal so that it heats the water, which is the same kind of unglamorous order.

**Image.** A channel of water runs through a garden between two lines of stones. A figure kneels and clears a fallen leaf from the gate where it enters.

**Meaning.** Steady attention that keeps a bond flowing. The card names regularity as the form care takes between people.

**In a reading.** Regular, quiet attention is what the situation needs. The card favours routine over gesture, and it counsels patience while habits settle into a pattern.

**Pitfall.** Making the routine the purpose. Rituals of courtesy become a performance of care, and the channel is cleared so often that no one remembers where the water goes.

**Links.** V — Herding the Ox: the nose-string and the whip, here shown as small daily courtesies. XV — Ordinary Mind: the point at which such care no longer feels like effort.

## Six of Water

**Stage.** Riding the Ox Home, in ease with others. Feeling and practice agree. Company no longer needs managing, and a long conversation with an old friend can wander without any one trying to steer it. Gain and loss in the relationship have stopped being the measure.

**Image.** Two people drift in a small boat on a wide river at dusk. One plays a bamboo flute and the other trails a hand in the water. The far bank is a faint line.

**Meaning.** Harmony after effort. The card names the easy exchange between people who have done the work of understanding one another.

**In a reading.** A relationship or a way of feeling has settled into ease. The card suggests that the work has been absorbed, and it invites enjoying the company without examining it.

**Pitfall.** Taking comfort for completion. The pleasant current carries the boat, and the passengers come to prefer it to any further question. Agreeableness can shelter a good deal of avoidance.

**Links.** VI — Riding the Ox Home: the flute, the evening and the lowered whip, here shared by two. XIV — No Mirror, No Stand: the ease of a mind that has stopped polishing itself.

## Seven of Water

**Stage.** The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone, in the tea way. The forms that once held a person steady, the ordered movements of preparing tea and the rules of etiquette, are no longer attended to. Tea is made without thought of procedure. The host remains, and with him the pleasure of being a good host.

**Image.** A tea room late in the afternoon. The utensils lie at rest on the tatami, and a single figure sits looking at the kettle. The door stands open to the garden.

**Meaning.** The method has done its work. The card names the stage at which a form is released while the one who practised it stays.

**In a reading.** A discipline, role or way of relating has become unnecessary and can be put down. The card asks what remains of the person once the scaffolding goes, and it advises resting before asking.

**Pitfall.** Pride in having let go. The host who no longer thinks of procedure comes to admire his own naturalness, and the self-regard takes the place of the old rules.

**Links.** VII — The Ox Forgotten: the means set aside. VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the host who will be dropped next.

## Eight of Water

**Stage.** The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight, in feeling and relationship. Host and guest, giver and receiver, and the one who feels and what is felt, drop away together. A moment of grief or love arises with no owner to claim it.

**Image.** A plain bowl sits in the centre of an empty tatami room. A circular ring of damp, left by the bowl, is the only mark on the floor. The paper screens are closed and the light is even.

**Meaning.** Feeling without a subject. The card names a clearing in which the roles through which relationships are held loosen.

**In a reading.** Roles and attachments have lost their grip, and the situation has no clear form yet. The card can mean relief, numbness or a pause before a new arrangement. It asks that the quiet be allowed to stand for a time.

**Pitfall.** Settling into the cold. Detachment passes for peace, the heart is left like a dry well, and nothing in daily life changes. Hakuin's warning about the cave of the black mountain applies to the feelings as much as to the mind.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the empty circle, here as an empty bowl. 0 — Mu: the blank under all the relationships.

## Nine of Water

**Stage.** Returning to the Source, in perception. Water is seen as water. The kettle's hiss, the steam, the steadiness of the bowl are met without the commentary that once accompanied them. Feeling goes on in the same ordinary way that rivers run.

**Image.** An iron kettle simmers on a charcoal hearth in an empty tea room. Through the open shoji a stream runs past red maples. Neither the room nor the garden holds a figure.

**Meaning.** Clear perception of the ordinary. The card names what the emptiness of the preceding stage gives back: a world of cups, kettles and friends, seen as they are.

**In a reading.** A situation can be seen as it is, with nothing to add. The card suggests that the confusion has cleared and invites attention to what is already present. It also marks that nothing needs doing yet.

**Pitfall.** Quietism. The observer sits by the stream admiring the clarity and lets others pour their own tea. The verse of the ninth picture mocks the idea that returning is an achievement, and the same applies to admiring it.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: the river flowing of its own accord. XV — Ordinary Mind: the plain world recovered in a master's words.

## Ten of Water

**Stage.** Entering the Market with Open Hands, in service. The practised steadiness of feeling is offered to strangers without announcement. A bowl of tea is handed to a traveller. A grief is shared without advice. The hands hang open, as the Chinese title of the picture suggests.

**Image.** A roadside stall under a straw awning. A barefoot man ladles water from a wooden bucket into a bowl for a weary porter, and others wait their turn. Children play in the puddle at the stall's edge.

**Meaning.** Care for others that leaves no mark of distinction. The card names the stage at which feeling is put to use among people.

**In a reading.** Generosity is called for, in the form of hospitality, listening or simple presence. The card also closes a cycle, and the next to be drawn is a beginning. Whatever was gained is for use.

**Pitfall.** Performing humility, or pouring from above. The helper who keeps count of what he gives has made a market of the gift. Rikyū's last rule, to treat everyone present with every consideration, is a check on this.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the same open-handed figure, here with a bucket of water. I — Searching for the Ox: the cycle begins again, with the old thirst met more lightly.

## First Rank of Water

**Rank.** 正中偏 (shōchūhen), the relative within the absolute. In Water it is the emptiness of feeling seen first: all moods and attachments are recognised as passing, and the water is still.

**Figure.** A hermit of middle age who has seen through the surface of his own sorrows and joys. He sits beside a mountain pool in late autumn. The water is clear and no leaf moves on it.

**Meaning.** Stillness reached by seeing that feelings have no fixed ground. The card shows a real insight that has not yet entered the life of the person.

**In a reading.** Emotional detachment, which is real but incomplete. The card describes someone who has stopped being carried away and may be mistaking that for the end of the matter. It asks whether the stillness has a use.

**Pitfall.** Hakuin's warning about the first rank: stagnant water. The pool never moves, and the person comes to prefer its calm to the company of anyone whose feelings are not yet seen through. The detachment becomes a quiet form of coldness.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the emptiness that the figure has tasted and holds as home. 0 — Mu: the blank he rests in, without yet asking it anything.

## Second Rank of Water

**Rank.** 偏中正 (henchūshō), the absolute within the relative. In Water it means recognising the whole in the particular: a single bowl of tea contains the emptiness that the hermit saw in the mountains.

**Figure.** A tea host in middle life. He cleans a worn bowl with a white cloth in a small room, and the light from the window falls on the steam. His attention rests wholly on the bowl.

**Meaning.** Emptiness found in ordinary things. The card shows a person for whom the insight of the first rank has come down into the objects of daily use.

**In a reading.** The extraordinary is being met in the ordinary. The card points to a person or time in which small tasks, meetings and exchanges carry unusual weight, with no need for a separate retreat.

**Pitfall.** Aestheticism. The bowl becomes the object of devotion and the host a connoisseur of his own refinement. Attention to particulars slides into collecting them.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: mountains are mountains again, and tea is tea. XV — Ordinary Mind: the plain manner in which the particular carries the whole.

## Third Rank of Water

**Rank.** 正中來 (shōchūrai), coming from within the absolute. In Water, action and compassion arise from emptiness without a prior plan. Response to another person is quick and unrehearsed.

**Figure.** A ferryman at a river crossing. A traveller arrives at dusk in a hurry, and the ferryman is already holding the pole. His face shows no calculation. The boat is small and the current is strong.

**Meaning.** Spontaneous help rooted in stillness. The card names the person whose adaptation to others has no residue of self-interest to slow it down.

**In a reading.** A response is being asked for, and it will be right if it comes without deliberation. The card favours yielding to circumstance, as water takes the shape of the vessel. The Daodejing compares the highest good to water for this reason.

**Pitfall.** Mistaking reactivity for compassion. A person who yields to every pressure may be following habit and calling it flow, and the ferryman's calm is a result of long training.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: action coming from emptiness. XVII — The Shout: another form of spontaneous response, in which the answer is given with force.

## Fourth Rank of Water

**Rank.** 兼中至 (kenchūshi), arriving at mutual integration. In Water the two sides, emptiness and form, stillness and feeling, work together. Each supports the other in the manner of host and guest.

**Figure.** A tea master and a guest kneel across a hearth, and it is difficult to say which is serving. Steam rises between them from a single bowl. The room is plain and warm.

**Meaning.** Full engagement with others and with one's own feeling, while the ground of emptiness stays present. The card names the maturity of relationship.

**In a reading.** The situation holds both depth and ease, and two parties are well matched. The card suggests that nothing needs to be resolved between them. It favours conversation, collaboration and shared work.

**Pitfall.** Fixing the arrangement. Harmony between host and guest hardens into a ritual that excludes the unexpected visitor, and the integration becomes a style.

**Links.** XV — Ordinary Mind: everyday conduct as the way itself. XI — The Flower and the Smile: a meeting in which the whole is passed without explanation.

## Fifth Rank of Water

**Rank.** 兼中到 (kenchūtō), unity attained. In Water the wholeness is mature and carries no trace of attainment. The person is indistinguishable from the common run of people drawing water from a well.

**Figure.** An old woman at a village well in the early morning, lowering a wooden bucket. She nods to a neighbour and goes on. Nothing in her manner or dress marks her out.

**Meaning.** Ordinary life with the work of practice finished and unremarked. The card names a person in whom feeling, stillness and service are one thing.

**In a reading.** The matter resolves in plain conduct, without a sign of resolution. The card suggests that the question may be one that has stopped being asked. It is the quietest card in the suit.

**Pitfall.** Looking for the attainment. Seekers who expect a sage will walk past the well, and a reader who expects a sign will find the card empty. Imitating the plainness is a further form of display.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the unmarked figure among ordinary people. VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the emptiness that is now carried without being mentioned.

## One of Fire

**Stage.** Searching for the Ox in the domain of effort. A restless energy has no direction yet: a monk or lay student feels a need to strive and tries one discipline after another, from fasting to long retreats to a heap of manuals.

**Image.** A lone figure in a dark hall holds a single lit taper and walks between pillars, the flame bending in the draught. The pillars fade into grey wash, and only the flame is tinted.

**Meaning.** Will before its object. The heat is real, and what it should be spent on is undecided.

**In a reading.** Energy is present and has not yet found its question. The card marks the start of a project or practice driven by restlessness more than by a plan. It asks what the effort is for before more of it is spent.

**Pitfall.** Treating intensity as progress. The student who adds harder methods each month is still searching, with more fuel.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the same seeker, now with a flame in hand instead of a road under foot. 0 — Mu: the question that will eventually give the fire something to burn.

## Two of Fire

**Stage.** Seeing the Traces in the domain of koan work. The student reads the recorded cases and the commentaries on them, and finds that the old masters describe the same difficulty he is having.

**Image.** Two small braziers on a wooden floor, one burning and one holding grey ash with a faint line of smoke still rising. Beside them lies an open scroll, with no characters drawn.

**Meaning.** Evidence that others have burned here before. The ash shows that the fire has been tended and that it can be read.

**In a reading.** Someone else's recorded struggle sheds light on the present one. The card points to study, a teacher's remark or a case that clarifies the doubt. It advises reading with attention and without taking the commentary for the fire.

**Pitfall.** Becoming a connoisseur of koans: knowing the cases, their verses and their lineages while the question remains a topic and has not become a doubt.

**Links.** II — Seeing the Traces: the footprints are the cases, which point to the ox without being it. XI — The Flower and the Smile: the teaching that passes outside the scriptures, which the study here cannot supply.

## Three of Fire

**Stage.** Seeing the Ox in the domain of the great doubt. After weeks on a single head phrase, the question stops being something held and becomes something that holds. For a moment the doubt is whole.

**Image.** A red-hot iron ball rests on an anvil in a smithy, its glow lighting a bare forge and the hands of a smith. The room is otherwise grey ink, with the ball as the single warm shape.

**Meaning.** The doubt at full heat. Mumon's image of the hot iron ball that cannot be swallowed or spat out describes it.

**In a reading.** A question has become impossible to put down. The card marks a moment of concentrated attention in which the usual defences fall away. It asks that the heat be kept and not explained.

**Pitfall.** Mistaking the intensity for the answer, or announcing it. The glow passes, and the work of keeping the doubt alive remains.

**Links.** III — Seeing the Ox: a first direct sight, here reached through doubt. 0 — Mu: the head phrase on which this heat is raised.

## Four of Fire

**Stage.** Catching the Ox in the domain of will. The student has to hold the doubt through sleepiness, pain and boredom, with the sitting schedule as the rope and the keisaku as the whip.

**Image.** A figure kneels on a cushion, gripping a long stick across the lap while a flame burns steadily in a lamp ahead. Behind, a second shadow lifts the stick as if to strike a shoulder.

**Meaning.** Discipline applied against the pull of old habit. Effort is the working part of the stage.

**In a reading.** A commitment that must be kept up when it has stopped being exciting. The card recommends structure, regular hours and a companion who can notice drift. It reads as a time for steady effort and little invention.

**Pitfall.** Willing the practice as a fight with the self. Gripping harder tires the one who grips, and the ox pulls the other way.

**Links.** IV — Catching the Ox: the rope and the whip, here as schedule and stick. XVII — The Shout: a sharp interruption that can break the drowse.

## Five of Fire

**Stage.** Herding the Ox in the domain of daily effort. The doubt is no longer a crisis, and it has to be carried through ordinary tasks: sweeping, cooking, answering letters. The flame is lowered to a level that can last.

**Image.** A kitchen hearth at dusk, a small controlled fire under a kettle, and a monk crouching to lay a split log with care. A string of dried radishes hangs above in grey wash.

**Meaning.** Fire kept alive without being fed more than it needs. Steady attention replaces the earlier bursts.

**In a reading.** The work has settled into a rhythm that needs tending more than inspiring. The card suggests that consistency now matters more than intensity. It points to the unglamorous middle of a long undertaking.

**Pitfall.** Letting the fire go out through neglect, or building it up again in search of the old drama. Either error ends the habit that was forming.

**Links.** V — Herding the Ox: the ox follows when the rope is slack, and the hearth burns when the log is laid. XII — Facing the Wall: long uneventful sitting.

## Six of Fire

**Stage.** Riding the Ox Home in the domain of koan work. The case that took years has come through, and the student carries it with ease, answering the teacher in the room without strain.

**Image.** A figure walks a lantern-lit path at dusk, carrying a lit paper lantern at the end of a pole. The flame is steady and the walker looks at the ground ahead, relaxed.

**Meaning.** Effort that has become easy. The fire gives light and little struggle.

**In a reading.** Hard work has paid off and the effort now feels natural. The card marks competence that no longer needs to be proved. It advises enjoying the ease without turning it into a position.

**Pitfall.** Believing the work finished. Hakuin held that passing a koan is the start of deeper practice, and the student who rests here is easily drawn into complacency.

**Links.** VI — Riding the Ox Home: the same ease, the flute replaced by a lantern. III — Seeing the Ox: the sight that this steadiness grew from.

## Seven of Fire

**Stage.** The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone, in the domain of effort. The device that carried the practice, the head phrase or the counting of will, drops away, and only the person remains, still working.

**Image.** An empty tripod stands on a cold hearth in a bare room, with a few embers glowing beneath the ash. Through the open door, a figure sits in sunlight on the veranda.

**Meaning.** Will without a tool. The method has served and been set down.

**In a reading.** A technique, goal or ambition that once drove the work has lost its hold. The card says this loss is a stage and not a failure. It invites a pause before any new device is picked up.

**Pitfall.** Taking the quiet for laziness and reaching for a new method, or taking it for attainment and settling there.

**Links.** VII — The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone: the rope and ox are gone, the person remains. XV — Ordinary Mind: the effort that dissolves into the plain day.

## Eight of Fire

**Stage.** The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight, in the domain of the great doubt. Both the question and the questioner fall away, and the doubt, which seemed the engine of the practice, has burned itself out.

**Image.** A bare hearth with a ring of white ash and no flame, drawn as an empty circle on blank paper. A thin grey thread of smoke leaves the centre and dissolves.

**Meaning.** Fire after its fuel is spent. What remains is a clearing, and the heat has gone with the one who felt it.

**In a reading.** The drive that carried a long effort has ended, and nothing has replaced it yet. The card names the gap and recommends not filling it quickly. It can mark the end of an obsession.

**Pitfall.** Mistaking the clearing for a void to be defended, or for depression to be cured. Both readings add a story to what has been left bare.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the empty circle. 0 — Mu: the question that could not be answered has ceased to need an answer.

## Nine of Fire

**Stage.** Returning to the Source in the domain of effort. Fire is seen as a natural process, like a pine in the wind or the sun on a ridge. The student finds that the work was always part of the world's own burning.

**Image.** A mountain stream under a rising sun, with fresh red maples on the bank in a faint wash of colour. A single burnt stump in the foreground sprouts a green shoot.

**Meaning.** Warmth that needs no effort to maintain. The sun and the leaves do the work that the student once thought was theirs.

**In a reading.** The result arrives without being forced, and the work looks different in retrospect. The card marks a time of simple warmth and clear seeing. It suggests letting things ripen on their own schedule.

**Pitfall.** Concluding that effort was unnecessary. The ease of this stage rests on the earlier labour, and denying that is a way of misreading it.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: the stream and the flowers go on as they were. XIII — Pacifying the Mind: the unrest that has stopped being a problem.

## Ten of Fire

**Stage.** Entering the Market with Open Hands in the domain of will. The seasoned practitioner uses the energy of the work in the service of others: cooking, building, teaching, mending, with no display.

**Image.** A village street at night, with a charcoal seller's brazier glowing at a corner. A stranger in a worn robe warms both open palms over it beside a labourer and a child.

**Meaning.** Warmth shared. The fire that once consumed its owner now heats the street.

**In a reading.** Skill and drive are being put to ordinary use among other people. The card marks a time when the work is for the benefit of others and is done lightly. It also marks the return to a new beginning.

**Pitfall.** Burning out in service, or taking pride in giving. Either keeps the account open.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the open palms of this card, here held to a brazier. I — Searching for the Ox: the cycle returns, and the new search starts with a steadier flame.

## First Rank of Fire

**Rank.** 正中偏 (*shō chū hen*), the relative within the absolute. In Fire, this is the student who has been burned through by the great doubt and sees that no self stood behind the effort. All that was striven for is empty, and the person tends to stop there.

**Figure.** A seated monk beside a dead fire, hands in the sleeves of a robe, face turned from the flame. The ashes are grey, and the single touch of colour is a cooled ember at the foot.

**Meaning.** Clarity about emptiness, with a draw toward rest. The fire has been seen through and has gone cold.

**In a reading.** A person who has seen that striving has no owner and may therefore stop striving. The card advises respect for the insight and caution about the stillness that follows it. It asks whether withdrawal is wisdom or fatigue.

**Pitfall.** Hakuin warned that this is where many are trapped. He called such quiet the demon cave of the Black Mountain, in which the insight is held and nothing further is asked of it.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the clearing that this person has reached and may not leave. XII — Facing the Wall: a stillness that can deepen or harden.

## Second Rank of Fire

**Rank.** 偏中正 (*hen chū shō*), the absolute within the relative. In Fire, this is the person who finds the whole matter present in an ordinary flame: a match struck, a stove lit, charcoal glowing in a brazier.

**Figure.** A cook in an apron, crouched at a stove, with his face lit by a small fire. Behind, utensils hang on the wall in loose ink. The flame alone carries colour.

**Meaning.** The empty and the particular seen together. Heat in the kettle is also heat in the sun.

**In a reading.** Meaning is found in a concrete task and not in a theory about it. The card points to craft, to attention given to small jobs and to the value of familiar acts. It suggests that the matter being looked for is already at hand.

**Pitfall.** Turning the observation into a sentiment. A person who says that everything is sacred and then leaves the dishes has missed the point of the stove.

**Links.** III — Seeing the Ox: the ox appears in the particular scene. XV — Ordinary Mind: the plain way of lighting a fire is the way.

## Third Rank of Fire

**Rank.** 正中來 (*shō chū rai*), coming from within the absolute. In Fire, action arises from emptiness: vigorous, timely and without a plan. This is the teacher or worker whose energy comes without a motive attached.

**Figure.** A blacksmith or a teacher at the moment of striking, with sparks leaving the anvil in a clean arc. The face is calm and the arm is loose. The sparks are the only coloured marks.

**Meaning.** Effort that begins from nowhere in particular and meets the situation as it is. The heat is an expression and not a store.

**In a reading.** Decisive action that is not driven by fear or ambition. The card suggests that the moment for intervening has come and that the response will fit if it is not rehearsed. It can name a teacher who acts directly.

**Pitfall.** Confusing spontaneity with impulse. Action from emptiness has been prepared by long training, and an untrained burst is only temper.

**Links.** XVII — The Shout: a response that leaves no room for a second thought. XIV — No Mirror, No Stand: nothing is fixed in advance to guide it.

## Fourth Rank of Fire

**Rank.** 兼中至 (*ken chū shi*), arriving at mutual integration. In Fire, will and release work together: the person is fully engaged in demanding work and equally free of it, and neither side leads.

**Figure.** A woman at a pottery kiln, tending the firebox with a long iron rake while a child hands her wood. Smoke rises into a pale sky. A thin line of red marks the firebox glow.

**Meaning.** Hard work and ease at once. The heat is used, and nobody is consumed by it.

**In a reading.** A person fully engaged in a difficult task without strain. The card marks a period of competence and shared labour where both determination and flexibility are called for. It suggests leaving the hands busy and the mind open.

**Pitfall.** Overwork under the heading of integration. Engagement that has no rest in it has dropped one of the two sides.

**Links.** V — Herding the Ox: care for the fire over a long time. X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the work done among others.

## Fifth Rank of Fire

**Rank.** 兼中到 (*ken chū tō*), unity attained. In Fire, the effort of a lifetime shows as ordinary warmth. The person burns nothing and makes no mention of having done any of it.

**Figure.** An old woman sweeping a temple yard in the evening, a small bonfire of fallen leaves glowing beside her. The smoke drifts up in a thin grey line. Only the fire is in colour.

**Meaning.** Maturity in which the work and the worker cannot be separated. The fire is lit when it is needed and left when it is not.

**In a reading.** Long effort that has become indistinguishable from life. The card marks a person whose presence is useful without being noticed. It advises ordinary work and no announcement of results.

**Pitfall.** Trying to look the part. A show of simplicity is another form of display, and the fifth rank leaves no trace to show.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: warmth as part of the landscape. XV — Ordinary Mind: the unremarkable way in which it is done.

## One of Wind

**Stage.** Searching for the Ox, played out in the breath. A person sits down to count breaths, and the mind leaves the count within a few exhalations. Each return to the count is followed by another departure, and the seeker has no sense yet of what is being sought.

**Image.** A figure sits on a cushion at dusk beside an open door. A few strokes of loose brush show the breath rising as a thin vapour, which frays and drifts toward the trees outside. No face is visible.

**Meaning.** Restlessness at the first attempt to attend to something as plain as breathing. The card names the discovery that the mind does not stay where it is put.

**In a reading.** A mind that will not settle on a single thing. The card describes scattered attention at the start of a task, a conversation or a practice, and it asks for a return to the count each time without keeping score. Failure at this stage is the material of the practice.

**Pitfall.** Treating the wandering as proof of unsuitability, or changing method at every lapse in the hope that a better technique will stop it.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the same restless seeker, here at the scale of one breath. 0 — Mu: the question that remains when counting fails.

## Two of Wind

**Stage.** Seeing the Traces, in the breath. The sitter begins to notice small signs: the cool of air at the nostrils, the slight rise of the belly, a pause at the end of the exhalation. These are reports about the breath and are not yet the breath itself.

**Image.** A hand held open before a paper window where a lantern flame leans and recovers. Beyond the paper, faint shadows of bamboo sway. The picture shows evidence of moving air and nothing of the air.

**Meaning.** The first reliable indications that something can be attended to. The card names instruction, description and early observation.

**In a reading.** Signs have appeared, and they are worth following. The card suggests reading what a teacher, a manual or a first experience has said and testing it against direct observation. A description of the breath is useful for as long as it sends the sitter back to breathing.

**Pitfall.** Collecting accounts of breathing, its physiology and its schools, and mistaking fluency about the subject for acquaintance with it.

**Links.** II — Seeing the Traces: the signs point to the ox without being the ox. XI — The Flower and the Smile: a transmission that does not go through description.

## Three of Wind

**Stage.** Seeing the Ox, in the breath. For the length of a single inhalation and exhalation the sitter is wholly present, and the breath is seen as it is. The experience is brief, and it is clear that it was always available.

**Image.** A bare branch on a winter morning, a single leaf turning on its stalk in a gust. Mountains behind it are left as blank paper. The leaf is drawn in precise detail and the rest in pale wash.

**Meaning.** One complete breath, with nothing added. The card names the first direct taste of attention that is neither effort nor drift.

**In a reading.** One moment of unmistakable presence has occurred. The card says the moment was real and that its occurrence does not depend on a particular technique. It advises keeping the memory light and not building a story about it.

**Pitfall.** Chasing the moment: trying to reproduce the exact quality of that breath, which ensures that it does not return. A second error is reporting it to others as an achievement.

**Links.** III — Seeing the Ox: the glimpse of what was never absent. IV — Catching the Ox: the breath must now be held across many cycles.

## Four of Wind

**Stage.** Catching the Ox, in the breath. The sitter counts from one to ten and begins again, and each lapse sends the count back to one. The effort is considerable. Attention is held by will, and the breath resists being observed without being managed.

**Image.** A figure walks into a hard crosswind along a ridge, robe pressed flat against the body, one hand clutching a hat. Grasses lie almost horizontal. The walker leans forward and does not look up.

**Meaning.** Deliberate effort to keep attention on the breath against the pull of habit.

**In a reading.** A demanding stretch in which something must be held against resistance. The card advises persistence in a plain, countable task, and it accepts that some force is needed. Progress shows in the number of unbroken counts, which at first is small.

**Pitfall.** Forcing the breath. Controlled breathing, strained and shallow, produces tension in the chest and shoulders. Hakuin described the Zen sickness (*zenbyō*) that follows forced concentration, and in *Yasen kanna* he prescribed settling the breath in the lower abdomen.

**Links.** IV — Catching the Ox: the same struggle, in the plainest material. V — Herding the Ox: the effort softens into routine.

## Five of Wind

**Stage.** Herding the Ox, in the breath and in movement. Counting gives way to following the breath from start to finish. In kinhin, the short walk between periods of sitting, the sitter learns to take each step with an exhalation and to keep the attention in the soles of the feet.

**Image.** A line of monks in a long corridor, hands folded at the chest, each a half-step behind the last. Light falls through slatted windows onto the floorboards. A single sandal has been left at the end of the corridor.

**Meaning.** Steady practice carried from sitting into walking, and from walking into the rest of the day.

**In a reading.** Regular, unremarkable practice that holds a day together. The card commends repetition, a fixed pace and the keeping of an agreed form. It names the slow part of the work, when little seems to happen.

**Pitfall.** Rigid posture and performed seriousness, which turn the form into a display. Walking meditation done for an audience, with a solemn face, has stopped being walking.

**Links.** V — Herding the Ox: the tether is the pace of the step. XV — Ordinary Mind: the later form of discipline that no longer looks like one.

## Six of Wind

**Stage.** Riding the Ox Home, in breath and speech. Breath and step agree. Talk comes easily and without self-watching, and the sitter walks home in the evening with no thought about the walk.

**Image.** A traveller on a country road at evening, a flute held loosely in one hand and a breeze lifting the sleeves. Reeds along the verge bend in the same direction as the walker. The road is straight and empty.

**Meaning.** Ease between practice and life, with the breath no longer a task.

**In a reading.** Effort has settled into fluency. The card suggests that speech, movement and work now agree with each other and that the arrangement can be enjoyed. It recommends not examining the ease too closely.

**Pitfall.** Taking pleasure in the good state of the breath as an end in itself. The music of the flute becomes the thing to be kept, and the walker begins to guard a mood.

**Links.** VI — Riding the Ox Home: the same ease, in air and gait. VII — The Ox Forgotten: the method is the next thing to be dropped.

## Seven of Wind

**Stage.** The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone, in breath. The sitter no longer counts or follows and finds that the breath continues without supervision. The method has done its work and lies unused, like a fishing basket on the bank. One person remains who is sitting, aware of having stopped.

**Image.** A cushion on a wooden veranda with a rosary or counting beads set aside on the floor. A gentle wind moves the paper screens. The seated figure has eyes half-open and faces the garden.

**Meaning.** The aid that is no longer needed and can be set down.

**In a reading.** A method has become unnecessary. The card suggests putting down a routine, a script or a technique that has served its purpose, and asking what remains afterwards. A sense of quiet satisfaction goes with it.

**Pitfall.** Pride in having let go of the method, and a refusal to go further. The sitter who has dropped counting can come to think of himself as one who needs no counting.

**Links.** VII — The Ox Forgotten: the device is left behind while the person stays. VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the one who dropped it is the next to go.

## Eight of Wind

**Stage.** The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight, in breath and speech. There is no one breathing and nothing breathed. The pause between the exhalation and the next inhalation, which the sitter once noticed, is no longer seen as a pause. Talk, if it comes, comes without a speaker.

**Image.** A wide empty sky above a pale horizon. A single bird-shaped gap in a layer of wash, or no bird at all. A faint ring of brush, drawn in one movement, at the centre of the paper.

**Meaning.** The cessation of the distinction between breather and breath.

**In a reading.** A clearing in which the usual voice of the self has gone quiet. The card names silence that is neither an effort nor a mood, and a pause before any new shape appears. It may be a relief, or it may be an emptiness without content.

**Pitfall.** Settling into the stillness. Hakuin warned against the cave of the black mountain, where the sitter rests in a silence that changes nothing in daily life.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: subject and object fall away together. 0 — Mu: the blank that began the sequence.

## Nine of Wind

**Stage.** Returning to the Source, in the sounds of the world. Wind in pines is heard as wind in pines. A cough in the hall is a cough, a bell is a bell, and each is complete without a listener adding to it. Speech also returns, with words taken as sounds before they are meanings.

**Image.** A grove of old pines on a hillside, needles drawn in a few hundred fine strokes, mist threading the lower trunks. A small stone lantern stands at the edge, unlit. No figure appears.

**Meaning.** Hearing and breathing as parts of the world and not as operations performed on it.

**In a reading.** A situation is plain and nothing needs to be added to it. The card suggests listening before speaking and registering what is said without preparing a reply. It names a return to ordinary perception after a period of confusion.

**Pitfall.** Withdrawal into the grove. A listener who attends to every sound and says nothing leaves the household to run on without him.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: the world appears again, unadorned. X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: what has been heard now has to be answered.

## Ten of Wind

**Stage.** Entering the Market with Open Hands, in speech and walking. The practitioner walks into a crowd and talks to whoever is there. The words are ordinary, are spoken for the sake of the listener, and carry no trace of the training behind them. The breath is the same as anyone's.

**Image.** A narrow market street under a stretch of cloth awnings that billow in a gust. A barefoot traveller with a gourd on a staff speaks to a fishmonger, who laughs. Paper lanterns and baskets crowd the edges.

**Meaning.** Plain speech and easy movement among other people, with no sign of distinction.

**In a reading.** A readiness to be useful in conversation, correspondence or a shared task. The card suggests saying the helpful thing in ordinary words and then walking on. It also closes a cycle, and the next card is the first.

**Pitfall.** Preaching. A word offered from above, however kind, tells the listener that the speaker has something he lacks.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the open hand has a voice. I — Searching for the Ox: the cycle returns, and the next search begins from a different footing.

## First Rank of Wind

**Rank.** 正中偏 (*shōchūhen*), the relative within the absolute. In the element of wind, speech and movement are seen through as emptiness first. The person has understood that breath, words and steps arise and fall without anyone owning them.

**Figure.** A monk stands in a courtyard on a day without a breath of air. Hands are folded in front of the chest, the robe hangs straight and the gravel is unmarked. Smoke from an incense stick rises in a column. The face is calm and turned slightly away.

**Meaning.** The stillness that follows the first clear seeing of emptiness, and the wind that has dropped.

**In a reading.** A person or phase in which silence is the principal fact. The card describes a refusal to speak or to move that has the dignity of insight and some of the quality of retreat. It asks what the stillness is for.

**Pitfall.** Resting in the calm. Hakuin named this the trap of the first rank, the quiet that is taken for the end of the way, and he ranked it with the dead sitting of the black mountain cave.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the emptiness in which the person lingers. IX — Returning to the Source: the wind that has to rise again.

## Second Rank of Wind

**Rank.** 偏中正 (*henchūshō*), the absolute within the relative. In wind, the plain particulars of weather, sound and walking are met as the whole. A leaf turning over counts as the teaching.

**Figure.** A woman sweeps fallen leaves from a temple path in a light breeze. Some leaves lift as she sweeps and settle again behind the broom. She looks at the ground a few steps ahead and nowhere else.

**Meaning.** The recognition of the unconditioned in an ordinary event of air and motion.

**In a reading.** The significance of a small thing is clear. The card describes a person who attends to ordinary detail so closely that it becomes illuminating, and a situation in which the particular is enough. It advises attention to the task nearest at hand.

**Pitfall.** Cherishing the particular for its beauty and making a devotion of it. Sweeping becomes a ritual of significance and not a way of clearing a path.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: the world seen as it is. XV — Ordinary Mind: the plain thing at hand is the way.

## Third Rank of Wind

**Rank.** 正中來 (*shōchūrai*), coming from within the absolute. In wind, speech and action arise out of emptiness without a prior plan, in the way a gust arises from a clear sky.

**Figure.** A wandering monk stops at a ford and calls across the water to a ferryman, one arm raised and the sleeve full of wind. The words are unprepared and the ferryman has turned to listen. The far bank is a pale outline.

**Meaning.** A spontaneous word or movement that answers what the occasion requires.

**In a reading.** A response arrives without deliberation and proves to be the right one. The card describes a person who acts from clarity, and a period in which compassion shows itself in a timely remark or a quick step. It advises trusting the response while the occasion is still present.

**Pitfall.** Mistaking impulse for spontaneity. A person who says whatever comes may do harm and call it freedom from conditioning.

**Links.** XVII — The Shout: speech that cuts through the listener's thinking. XVI — The Wild Fox: spontaneity that ignores cause and effect.

## Fourth Rank of Wind

**Rank.** 兼中至 (*kenchūshi*), arriving at mutual integration. In wind, emptiness and form meet in conversation, in which speaker and listener exchange breath and neither dominates.

**Figure.** Two people stand talking at a crossroads where several roads meet. Their sleeves blow toward each other and a child runs between them with a kite. The conversation has the loose attention of people who are also watching the street.

**Meaning.** Full engagement with others, with the two sides of the Rank working together.

**In a reading.** A conversation or negotiation that goes well because both parties are fully present. The card describes the ability to listen and to speak in turn, and to adjust without losing a sense of one's own position. It suggests that the exchange itself is the work.

**Pitfall.** Fluency that turns into skill at managing others. The person who reads every room well can begin to steer it.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: engaged without separation. XIII — Pacifying the Mind: the mind that no longer needs to be quieted before it can meet another.

## Fifth Rank of Wind

**Rank.** 兼中到 (*kenchūtō*), unity attained. In wind, breath, step and word are one and leave no mark. The person is in the air as air is in the room.

**Figure.** An old man hangs washing on a line in a steady wind. The sheets fill and snap, and he reaches for the next peg without looking up. A hand fan of the kind used in summer sits in a basket at his feet.

**Meaning.** Mature wholeness, with no trace of attainment. Dōgen closes the *Genjōkōan* with a fan: a monk asks Baoche of Mount Mayu why he fans himself when the nature of wind is constant and reaches everywhere, and Baoche, saying the monk knows only that the wind is constant, goes on fanning.

**In a reading.** Ordinary competence with nothing left over. The card describes a person who is not noticed for what he has achieved and who does the day's work without remark. It advises doing what is in front of one.

**Pitfall.** Being made into an example. Others may treat such a person as a model, and the person may begin to play the part.

**Links.** XV — Ordinary Mind: the way is the daily way. X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the same figure, with the gourd put down.

## One of Void

**Stage.** Searching for the Ox, in the domain of awareness. A person sits down to find out what is aware and goes looking for it among thoughts, sensations and sounds. Each item found is something that was noticed, so none of them is the noticing.

**Image.** A figure sits in a bare room with a lamp held at arm's length, shining it into the corners. The lamp throws its light across the floor and walls, and the figure's own shadow falls behind. No text.

**Meaning.** The first attempt to look for the one who looks. The card names the sense that something is missing and that attention is the place to find it.

**In a reading.** A search is under way for something that is already doing the searching. The card marks the start of inquiry and suggests that the questions asked of experience are being asked from within it. Frustration at this point is ordinary.

**Pitfall.** Making the search a technique and measuring each sitting by whether awareness was located. Equally, concluding from the paradox that inquiry is pointless.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the same restlessness, here turned inward on the seeker. 0 — Mu: a question that cannot be answered in the terms in which it is asked.

## Two of Void

**Stage.** Seeing the Traces, in the domain of awareness. Descriptions of awareness begin to match something in experience. A thought is noticed as it arises, and a gap appears after it before the next one comes.

**Image.** Fresh footprints cross an expanse of smooth sand and stop at the edge of a still pool. The sky is reflected in the pool, and no one stands nearby. No text.

**Meaning.** Evidence that awareness is present: the noticing of thoughts, the brief spaces between them, a sentence in a book that describes what happens during sitting.

**In a reading.** Early signs that the question has an answer, found in a remark, a pause or an unexpected stillness. The card suggests following the sign and keeping in mind that a description of awareness is a set of marks left behind it.

**Pitfall.** Mistaking the vocabulary for the experience: learning terms such as witness, ground and presence, and speaking fluently of what has not been seen.

**Links.** II — Seeing the Traces: texts and teachers pointing at what they are not. XI — The Flower and the Smile: recognition that passes without a description.

## Three of Void

**Stage.** Seeing the Ox, in the domain of awareness. A sound is heard, and for a moment there is hearing with no one added to it. The usual commentary falls silent, and awareness is evident as the plain fact of the room.

**Image.** A temple bell has just been struck. Its ring is drawn as a set of widening circles over a quiet courtyard, and a monk on the veranda has turned his head. His face is calm and unreadable. No text.

**Meaning.** A first direct sight of awareness. It shows itself as the condition of everything noticed and not as a further thing to notice.

**In a reading.** A moment of clarity that arrives without effort and cannot be repeated on demand. The card advises letting the experience be what it was and refraining from labelling it at once.

**Pitfall.** Stepping back to watch oneself having the experience, which produces an observer standing behind the awareness and a new search for the observer.

**Links.** III — Seeing the Ox: the sight that comes after the traces. XIII — Pacifying the Mind: looking directly and finding nothing to grasp.

## Four of Void

**Stage.** Catching the Ox, in the domain of awareness. Having glimpsed awareness, the sitter tries to hold it. Attention narrows, the jaw tightens, and the openness that was seen contracts into a fixed gaze.

**Image.** A hand closes around a shaft of light coming through a window. The light spills through the fingers, and the knuckles are white. The dust in the beam moves unevenly. No text.

**Meaning.** The effort to keep an open state in place. Awareness resists being kept, and the attempt produces tension where there had been spaciousness.

**In a reading.** A good experience is being gripped and is going stale as a result. The card suggests relaxing the hand and recognising that the openness did not depend on the grip. Some struggle at this stage is expected.

**Pitfall.** Concluding that the original experience was an illusion because holding it failed, or doubling the effort and sitting with ever greater rigidity.

**Links.** IV — Catching the Ox: the wild mind that resists the rope. XVI — The Wild Fox: the cost of treating insight as something to be possessed.

## Five of Void

**Stage.** Herding the Ox, in the domain of awareness. Sitting becomes a repeated cycle of wandering off, noticing and coming back. Open awareness is practised with less force, and the noticing itself grows quicker and gentler.

**Image.** A herdsman walks a path through tall grass with a loose cord trailing from his hand. The ox, drawn in a few strokes, wanders a little way off and returns without being pulled. The sky above them is left empty. No text.

**Meaning.** The long, ordinary work of returning. The card names the sitting that is done day after day with little to show for it, and the gradual softening of the effort.

**In a reading.** Steady practice, or a commitment that needs only to be kept. The card suggests that progress here is not visible from one day to the next and that attention is being trained by the returning itself.

**Pitfall.** Vigilance that turns into policing, in which every lapse is an offence and the sitter becomes the warden of his own mind.

**Links.** V — Herding the Ox: the rope and whip that are held more lightly as the work continues. XV — Ordinary Mind: trying to direct oneself toward the Way turns away from it.

## Six of Void

**Stage.** Riding the Ox Home, in the domain of awareness. Open awareness holds without effort, and the sitter rests in it. Thoughts pass through like weather, and the struggle of the earlier stages has lapsed.

**Image.** A boy rides sideways on the back of an ox along a river at dusk, playing a flute. The ox walks on without guidance. Behind them the far bank is a pale line, with no mountains drawn. No text.

**Meaning.** Ease in awareness, with the sense of having arrived. The card names the pleasure of sitting that no longer needs defending.

**In a reading.** A phase of calm and competence, in which things that once took labour now go of themselves. The card suggests enjoying it and noticing that the ease is still being treated as a possession.

**Pitfall.** Savouring the peace and sitting in order to return to it, so that a pleasant state replaces the earlier object of the search.

**Links.** VI — Riding the Ox Home: the tune played on the way, in no hurry to arrive. IX — Returning to the Source: the ease of this card, when it no longer depends on a state.

## Seven of Void

**Stage.** The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone, in the domain of awareness. Awareness is no longer an object that is kept or lost. Sitting continues with no one checking whether it is going well, and the sitter is left as the last item of the practice.

**Image.** A man sits on the veranda of a thatched hut in the afternoon, with an empty stall behind him and a rope coiled in the dust. The sky is wide and plain. His eyes are closed, and one hand rests on his knee. No text.

**Meaning.** The disappearance of awareness as something to be managed. What remains is a person who is sitting, with a faint sense of himself as the one who has let go.

**In a reading.** A technique or a project has done its work and can be set down. The card suggests asking what stays when the method goes, and who is left to ask it.

**Pitfall.** Pride in effortlessness. The sitter may take rest in his own non-striving for the goal and decline the last step.

**Links.** VII — The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone: the tool dropped and the user still present. VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the person who drops in turn.

## Eight of Void

**Stage.** The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight, in the domain of awareness. Shikantaza, just sitting, in which Dōgen's instruction is to sit with no aim of becoming a buddha. There is neither a sitter nor something that is aware of sitting, and the stage cannot be reported while it occurs.

**Image.** A single large circle in one brushstroke on pale paper, open at the lower edge where the brush left the surface. Inside it is empty paper, and outside it is empty paper too. No text.

**Meaning.** The point at which awareness, its object and its owner all drop away together. The card names the suit's centre and, in this suit, the stage that is closest to the element itself.

**In a reading.** A clearing in which roles and goals have lost their hold. The card can mean relief, a pause before a new form, or a blankness that is not yet understanding.

**Pitfall.** Settling into the blank. Hakuin called this quiet the cave of the black mountain, in which a person sits as if awakened and does not change.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the circle itself. 0 — Mu: the same blank, met at the start of the sequence.

## Nine of Void

**Stage.** Returning to the Source, in the domain of awareness. The sounds and sights of the room are back, and they arise inside an awareness that has nothing added to it. Rain on the roof is heard as rain on the roof.

**Image.** An open window in an empty room looks out on a wet garden. A bowl of water on the sill holds the grey sky, and a maple leaf drifts across the garden. No one is drawn. No text.

**Meaning.** Awareness as the field in which things appear, with nothing to separate the field from its contents. The paradox of finding awareness has dissolved without being solved.

**In a reading.** Clear perception after confusion, in which a situation is seen as it is. The card suggests that nothing needs adding to what is present, and that the view comes without a method.

**Pitfall.** Quietism: staying at the window and watching the garden. The verse of the ninth picture also warns that returning to the source is already a step too many.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: streams flowing and flowers red, for no one in particular. XV — Ordinary Mind: the plain world recovered in a master's words.

## Ten of Void

**Stage.** Entering the Market with Open Hands, in the domain of awareness. Awareness goes out into conversation, errands and other people's trouble. It does not call itself awareness, and the person who carries it is hard to tell from anyone else in the queue.

**Image.** A barefoot man in a crowded street stops to hold a door for a woman with a heavy basket. A child looks up at him. Around them the market stalls are drawn in dense detail, and the man alone is painted with few lines. No text.

**Meaning.** Awareness used for others and shown only in conduct. The card names attention that is available to whoever happens to be present.

**In a reading.** Being useful in ordinary company with whatever has been learned, and without comment. The card marks the end of a cycle, and the next card is the first.

**Pitfall.** Displaying presence: speaking slowly, gazing meaningfully, offering the calm as a performance. Awareness that announces itself has become an object again.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the closing gesture of the whole path. I — Searching for the Ox: the cycle begins again, with the seeker now able to search without distress.

## First Rank of Void

**Rank.** 正中偏 (*shō chū hen*), the relative within the absolute. In this suit emptiness comes first: awareness is seen to be without content or owner, and the particulars of life appear as thin and passing.

**Figure.** A sitter in a bare room before a blank wall, hands in the cosmic mudra, with the window shuttered. The room is tidy and unlived in. Dust gathers on a closed letter on the floor beside him.

**Meaning.** The clarity of someone who has seen through things. The world is emptied of weight, and nothing in it needs a response.

**In a reading.** A phase of detachment that feels like wisdom. The card suggests it is real insight, and that insight of this kind is not yet the whole of the matter.

**Pitfall.** Resting here. Hakuin warned against the first rank as a trap, a quiet in which realisation is mistaken for withdrawal from the world, and Void is the suit where that trap is the most comfortable.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the emptiness that the figure has met. 0 — Mu: the unanswerable question, which this person has stopped hearing as a question.

## Second Rank of Void

**Rank.** 偏中正 (*hen chū shō*), the absolute within the relative. Awareness is recognised in ordinary particulars: a sound, a cup, the grain of a table. The emptiness found earlier is met again in what is in front of the eyes.

**Figure.** A woman at a kitchen table has stopped in the middle of peeling an orange. Light from the window falls on the peel in a long spiral, and she is looking at it with an expression of mild surprise.

**Meaning.** Recognition of the open ground in a small thing. The world is back in view, and each part of it shows what the first rank had set aside.

**In a reading.** Noticing that what was sought elsewhere was present in a routine object or hour. The card suggests keeping attention on the particular and not hurrying to name what it shows.

**Pitfall.** Treating the ordinary object as a sign of something more, so that the orange becomes a symbol and the attention moves past it.

**Links.** IX — Returning to the Source: the world seen with nothing added. III — Seeing the Ox: a sight that is quiet and complete.

## Third Rank of Void

**Rank.** 正中來 (*shō chū rai*), coming from within the absolute. Action arises out of emptiness. In this suit it is the person who listens with open attention and responds to what is said, with no script prepared.

**Figure.** A nurse sits beside a bed at night with a shaded lamp, one hand lightly on the patient's wrist. Her gaze is on the patient's face, and the window behind her is a dark rectangle.

**Meaning.** Compassion that comes from an open mind. The card names attention that has no agenda of its own and is therefore available to what is in front of it.

**In a reading.** An opportunity to act, speak or stay silent from a clear place. The card suggests that the right response is likely to be seen once the self-regarding ones are set aside.

**Pitfall.** Posing as empty while acting from habit, and calling a reflex spontaneity. The person may also leave their own needs unattended and call it selflessness.

**Links.** X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: help that carries no mark of the helper. XVII — The Shout: a response that comes out before a reply is composed.

## Fourth Rank of Void

**Rank.** 兼中至 (*ken chū shi*), arriving at mutual integration. Emptiness and form work as two sides of one activity. In this suit the person is fully engaged in affairs and fully open at the same time, and neither side is given up for the other.

**Figure.** A ferryman poles a crowded boat across a river in morning mist. His passengers talk, argue and doze, and he answers each one while he keeps the boat on its line. The far shore is not yet visible.

**Meaning.** Competent, wholehearted engagement that rests on open awareness. The work is done well, and the worker is not caught in it.

**In a reading.** A period in which responsibility and clarity coexist. The card suggests that the two need no longer be traded against each other, and that the demands of the present are best met from where one already stands.

**Pitfall.** Becoming a model of balance, so that the integration is carried as a reputation. The person who has to look integrated has lost the thing.

**Links.** XV — Ordinary Mind: the Way in daily affairs, with no special state. IX — Returning to the Source: the world and the open ground seen as one.

## Fifth Rank of Void

**Rank.** 兼中到 (*ken chū tō*), unity attained. Wholeness with no trace of attainment. In this suit the paradox of finding awareness has gone, and the person has no view about it and nothing left to say.

**Figure.** An old man sweeps the steps of a small shrine at dawn, in a plain robe. A few leaves lie on the stone. Nothing about him draws the eye, and a visitor passing through the gate would not look at him twice.

**Meaning.** Maturity without display. The practice is not separate from life, and no one, including the person himself, can point to where it is.

**In a reading.** Quiet completion. The card suggests that the matter at hand is done, or nearly so, and that no announcement is needed.

**Pitfall.** Turning the absence of a trace into a style, a studied ordinariness that is another kind of showing. The card can also be misread as the end of the way, when it is the point at which the way is walked without notice.

**Links.** 0 — Mu: the beginning and end of the sequence, here seen as one. XV — Ordinary Mind: the Way that is nothing special.
