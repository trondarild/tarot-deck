# Pessimistic deck: symbolism of the major cards

The major series has sixteen cards in three movements, laid out in the proposal section of `pessimistic-philosophy-research.md`. The first movement (I–IV) describes an awakening: consciousness grows beyond its use (I), and then three places where happiness was sought, in this life, in an afterlife and in the future of the world, are tried and given up (II–IV). The second (V–X) describes the world once those hopes are gone, in the voices of Ecclesiastes, Schopenhauer, Leopardi, Mainländer and Cioran. The third (XI–XV) sets out five responses. Card XV, the call to let the earth fall silent, leads back to card 0, The Unborn, which has no number and stands before the sequence in the way the Fool does in the tarot. The cards can be read as stages on either side of it.

The responses are plural because the tradition disagrees about what follows from pessimism. Camus rebels, Schopenhauer counsels compassion and then renunciation, Leopardi asks for solidarity, Zapffe ends with extinction, and the deck does not choose among them. The framing follows Joshua Foa Dienstag's *Pessimism: Philosophy, Ethic, Spirit* (2006), which treats pessimism as a tradition with an ethic, one that aims to fortify the reader for a disenchanted world and not to depress him. Each entry below gives the source, the argument, the symbols, a reading, a counterweight (the strongest objection, which replaces a reversed meaning) and links to other cards. Several authors discussed here wrote about suicide. The entries report their positions, including the arguments of Schopenhauer and Hartmann against it, and the deck does not recommend it. Translations are credited where quoted. The entries mark details that could not be confirmed.

## 0 — The Unborn

**Source.** The wisdom of Silenus, which has several witnesses: Aristotle's lost dialogue *Eudemus*, preserved in Plutarch's *Consolatio ad Apollonium*; Theognis, *Elegies*, lines 425–428, where the saying appears as a proverb (Stobaeus attributes a version to Alcidamas, and the numbering was confirmed only through a secondary page); Sophocles, *Oedipus at Colonus*, the chorus near lines 1224–1227 (the translation consulted shows markers at 1220 and 1230 around it, so the range is plausible but was not checked line by line); and Nietzsche, *Die Geburt der Tragödie* (*The Birth of Tragedy*, 1872), section 3. A modern philosophical form is David Benatar, *Better Never to Have Been* (Oxford, 2006).

**Argument.** Midas asks the captive Silenus what is best for human beings. In Nietzsche's telling the answer is: "What is best of all is for ever beyond your reach: not to be born, not to be, to be nothing" (Haussmann translation). Second best is to die soon. Benatar gives the saying an argument. The absence of pain is good even when no one enjoys that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is bad only if someone is deprived of it. Since the unborn lack both and nobody is deprived, non-existence has the advantage, and coming into existence is a harm.

**Symbols.** Silenus, old and drunken, laughs as he answers, and Midas recoils from what he hears. The laughter stands for a wisdom older than the gods of Olympus, given reluctantly and without pity. The spring and the rose garden, where Silenus was caught in one version of the story, mark the ordinary pleasures from which the question is asked. The blank margin of the etching, the paper itself, is the unborn.

**In a reading.** A question asked before there is anything to lose. The card names the thought, which most people meet at some point, that existence was not a favour. It marks a starting premise and does not predict an outcome.

**Counterweight.** Critics of Benatar, among them David DeGrazia and Aaron Smuts, dispute the asymmetry, and a common reply is that it is stipulated and not derived. Another reply is that goods and bads need a subject, so that "better for the unborn" has no one to whom it applies. Nietzsche reports the saying and then argues that the Greeks answered it with the Olympian gods, who made life worth living.

**Links.** XV — The Last Messiah: the card that returns to this one. I — The Giant Deer: the biological form of the complaint. XI — The Stone: the one response that accepts being born.

## I — The Giant Deer

**Source.** Peter Wessel Zapffe, "Den sidste Messias" ("The Last Messiah"), *Janus*, 1933, a short version of the book *Om det tragiske* (1941); the dates are taken from the research note and were not rechecked. The English text consulted is the Philosophy Now translation (issue 45).

**Argument.** Zapffe describes human consciousness as a biological paradox: an organ developed beyond the needs of survival, which turns against its bearer. He compares it to the antlers of a deer that grew too heavy to carry. A creature that sees its own mortality, the indifference of the world and the suffering in it lives in what he calls cosmic panic. The essay opens with a parable of the first man who wakes to this understanding at a waterhole.

**Symbols.** A single deer stands in a bare winter forest, its head bowed under antlers wider than its body. The antlers stand for an endowment that has outgrown its use and become a burden. The empty forest shows a creature alone with its excess. The species was extinct long before Zapffe wrote, which suits an image of a line that ends.

**In a reading.** An awareness greater than the situation requires: a person who thinks more than the day demands, or who finds ordinary life harder because he sees too much of it. The card names the cost of insight and invites a question about what to do with it.

**Counterweight.** The antler story is an analogy and not an argument, and the zoology was not checked here. A more serious objection is that consciousness is also the source of the values by which suffering is judged bad; a verdict delivered by the faculty the argument condemns carries the same doubt. Camus, who did not read Zapffe, accepts a similar diagnosis of the gap between human demands and the silence of the world and declines the conclusion.

**Links.** XV — The Last Messiah: the essay's own ending and the card that completes this one. X — The Insomniac: lucidity as the same excess felt nightly. 0 — The Unborn: the state before the burden.

## II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here

**Source.** Eduard von Hartmann, *Philosophie des Unbewussten* (*Philosophy of the Unconscious*, 1869), as summarised in the excerpt on the Utah *Ethics of Suicide* site, which uses William Chatterton Coupland's 1893 translation. The research note records that Britannica describes a different triad, about the Unconscious itself; that was not examined here.

**Argument.** Hartmann holds that humanity passes through three stages of illusion, each ending in disappointment. In the first, happiness is sought in earthly life in the present. He associates the stage with antiquity and with childhood. Experience then shows that the goods of this life do not deliver the happiness they promise: pleasures are brief and mixed with pain, and desire renews itself as it is satisfied.

**Symbols.** Children play in a classical garden, the age and the era Hartmann pairs with the first stage. The hoop and the wreath stand for pleasure in the present. A soap bubble drifts above them, the *homo bulla* of vanitas painting, and a hairline crack runs through the fountain basin: the signs of fragility are small and easy to miss, as they are in the stage itself.

**In a reading.** Satisfaction pursued in the immediate: money, pleasure, comfort, reputation. The card describes the point at which the pursuit has been achieved and the contentment has not arrived, or has arrived and gone. It does not say that pleasure is worthless.

**Counterweight.** Epicurus and Aristotle held that a moderate happiness in this life is available and sufficient, so that the failure of excess does not show the failure of the whole. Hartmann's claim that experience proves the illusion is itself an empirical claim, and it is contested by the evidence that most people report being satisfied with their lives.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the next stage, once this world is given up. V — Vanity: the same observation made by Ecclesiastes two millennia earlier. VI — The Pendulum: Schopenhauer's account of why satisfaction fails.

## III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter

**Source.** Eduard von Hartmann, *Philosophie des Unbewussten* (1869), stage two of the three illusions, via the Utah excerpt (Coupland translation, 1893).

**Argument.** After the first illusion fails, happiness is relocated to a transcendent life after death. Hartmann associates this stage with the Middle Ages and with youth. The stage meets its own disappointment: the hope of a heaven devalues the present world, and with the decline of belief the hope loses its force. In Hartmann's account it is exhausted historically, as faith recedes, and in the individual, as the believer ages.

**Symbols.** Pilgrims with staffs and scallop shells look up at a heavenly city resting on cloud. The pilgrimage stands for a life organised around a destination elsewhere. The lower walls of the city are thinning into cloud, so that sky shows through the stone: the hope remains in view while its support gives way.

**In a reading.** Deferred fulfilment: the belief that effort, suffering or patience now will be repaid elsewhere, in a religion, a promised retirement or a future life. The card asks what is being postponed and for whom.

**Counterweight.** The religious traditions reply that the afterlife is the ground of meaning and that its loss is the problem, which would make the pessimist's argument a symptom and not a finding. Nietzsche made the reverse charge, that the other-worldly hope slanders the present life. A narrower objection is that Hartmann treats the stage as a historical fact about what people believe, which says nothing on whether the belief is true.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the stage that this one replaces. IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the hope transferred to the future of the world. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: Schopenhauer's renunciation, which Hartmann judged a private escape.

## IV — The Third Illusion: Progress

**Source.** Eduard von Hartmann, *Philosophie des Unbewussten* (1869), stage three, via the Utah excerpt (Coupland translation, 1893).

**Argument.** In the third stage happiness is placed in the future development of the world: in science, politics and the improvement of the species. Hartmann associates it with modern times and with adulthood. He expects it to fail in turn, because additional means to satisfaction create additional wants. Having dismissed all three, he draws a conclusion that differs from Schopenhauer's. He rejects individual suicide as selfish and pointless, since the world-will would continue in other forms. He proposes instead that the world-process could end only if humanity as a whole came to will its cessation, a "cosmic-universal negation of will as the act that forms the end of the process" (Coupland).

**Symbols.** A procession in 19th-century dress walks toward a horizon of chimneys and railway lines, led by a man with a blank pennant. The road and the rails converge on a destination that grows fainter as it nears. The blank pennant is a cause with no stated content.

**In a reading.** Faith in a future that will justify the present: the plan, the cause, the career that ends in a better life. The card questions whether a goal that always lies ahead can supply the happiness that is missing now.

**Counterweight.** Measures of health, literacy and material welfare have improved over the long run, and a piecemeal improvement does not require a final state. There is also an internal objection: Hartmann's own endpoint, a collective act that ends the world-process, is a progress narrative with a teleology of its own. Dienstag, who studies the pessimists as a tradition, argues that progress is not a lasting feature of history, which supports Hartmann on the point.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the hope that progress replaced. XV — The Last Messiah: a different collective ending, by refusal and not by willing. XIII — The Broom: a response that gives up progress and keeps solidarity.

## V — Vanity

**Source.** Ecclesiastes (*Qohelet*), chapters 1–3. The book was composed between about 450 and 180 BCE, and scholars divide between the Persian and the Hellenistic period. The Vulgate's *vanitas vanitatum* (1:2) gave the vanitas still life its name.

**Argument.** The Hebrew *hevel* means vapour or breath and is rendered "vanity", "futility" or "meaningless". The Preacher examines wisdom, wealth, pleasure and labour "under the sun", a phrase that recurs twenty-nine times, and finds each transient. In the King James translation: "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity." He asks what profit a man has from his toil (1:3). Chapter 3 pairs a time to be born with a time to die, and a time to weep with a time to laugh, in a cycle that adds up to no gain.

**Symbols.** A skull, an hourglass and a snuffed candle lie among tools of labour: spade, hammer, compasses and a closed ledger. The skull and hourglass mark mortality and time, the candle a life put out, and the tools the toil from which no profit remains. An open window shows a bright sun, the "under the sun" of the Preacher, which shines on all of it alike.

**In a reading.** Labour whose point has gone, or a success that arrives without satisfaction. The card names the afternoon when the work still has to be done and the question of its worth has been asked.

**Counterweight.** The book contradicts its own gloom. The Preacher also advises enjoyment of food, work and companionship as gifts, and the epilogue (12:13) counsels fear of God and keeping the commandments, though many modern commentators think the epilogue was added to make the book orthodox. Whether Ecclesiastes is a pessimist text or a text about how to live with transience is disputed.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the same finding stated as a stage in an argument. VI — The Pendulum: Schopenhauer's explanation of the cycle. XI — The Stone: a later image of labour without gain, answered differently.

## VI — The Pendulum

**Source.** Arthur Schopenhauer, *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung* (*The World as Will and Representation*, 1818, enlarged 1844), volume I, §57. The section number was confirmed in the Haldane–Kemp translation on Project Gutenberg.

**Argument.** Schopenhauer holds that willing arises from want, and that want is suffering. When a desire is satisfied the pleasure is only the removal of a lack. With the lack gone, emptiness and boredom follow, and a new want soon appears. In the Haldane–Kemp translation: "Thus its life swings like a pendulum backwards and forwards between pain and ennui." The theory treats pleasure as negative, a relief from pain, and pain as positive.

**Symbols.** A great pendulum hangs over a bare plain. At one end of its swing a figure is doubled over in pain; at the other a man yawns on a stone with a closed book at his feet. The pendulum stands for the will's motion between want and its satisfaction, and the two figures for the two states it passes through. The symmetry of the scene shows that neither end is a resting place.

**In a reading.** The oscillation between pressure and flatness: a busy period followed by a restless one, a goal reached followed by a drift. The card does not condemn either state. It names the pattern and suggests that changing the circumstances will not stop the swing.

**Counterweight.** Nietzsche rejected the premise that life is basically a matter of suffering, and the claim that pleasure is merely negative is the weakest point of the argument. Experiences of absorbed activity do not fit the picture, since they involve neither a prior want nor a subsequent emptiness. Schopenhauer's own exceptions, aesthetic contemplation and compassion, concede that the pendulum can be stopped.

**Links.** XIV — The Stilled Wheel: Schopenhauer's answer, the moment when the swing stops. II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the hope the pendulum defeats. X — The Insomniac: boredom as a waking state.

## VII — Nature and the Icelander

**Source.** Giacomo Leopardi, "Dialogo della Natura e di un Islandese" (Dialogue between Nature and an Icelander), in the *Operette morali*, written in 1824. The English text consulted is Charles Edwardes, *Essays and Dialogues of Giacomo Leopardi* (1882), on Project Gutenberg.

**Argument.** An Icelander who has fled Nature across the world meets her in inner Africa, a giantess seated against a mountain. He complains that she has caused his suffering. She replies that the world was not made for human beings: "What I do is in no sense done for your enjoyment or benefit, as you seem to think" (Edwardes). She adds that creation and destruction are one process and that she would not notice the loss of the human race. Critics read the dialogue as Leopardi's turn from a historical pessimism, which blames civilisation, to a cosmic one, which blames nature.

**Symbols.** A small traveller in a heavy northern coat stands before a colossal woman seated with her back against a mountain, her face between beautiful and terrible, as Leopardi describes her. The scale is the argument: the traveller's grievance is addressed to something that does not register him. The barren plain is the interior of Africa where he finds her, at the far end of his flight.

**In a reading.** A complaint addressed to a world that does not listen. The card names the moment of finding that illness, accident or loss has no author who could answer for it, and that the grievance has no one to receive it.

**Counterweight.** A nature that is indifferent cannot be accused of cruelty, and the Icelander's protest presumes the purposes it denies. A theist would say the dialogue personifies a mechanism and then blames the person it has invented. The dialogue anticipates this by making Nature a character who is amused by the question.

**Links.** XIII — The Broom: Leopardi's later poem, which keeps this nature and adds a human answer. I — The Giant Deer: the same indifference seen from the biology of the mind. IX — The Shattered God: a metaphysics that gives the indifference an origin.

## VIII — The Porcupines

**Source.** Arthur Schopenhauer, *Parerga und Paralipomena* (1851), volume II. One secondary source gives chapter XXXI, §396; another places the passage in a chapter of psychological observations, and the German text was not consulted, so the section number is unconfirmed. The English text used is the parable "A Few Parables" in T. Bailey Saunders's *Studies in Pessimism* (Project Gutenberg), where the translator is not named in the part of the page that was fetched.

**Argument.** On a cold day porcupines huddle together for warmth and prick one another with their quills, so they move apart, and the cold drives them back. They repeat this until they find a distance at which they can bear each other. In the Saunders translation, "the need of society drives the human porcupines together, only to be mutually repelled." Schopenhauer concludes that good manners and courtesy are the moderate distance found by experience. Freud quoted the parable in a footnote in 1921.

**Symbols.** Porcupines sit on snow at a settled distance from one another, and one pair, still too close, is flinching apart. The cold stands for the need of company, the quills for the faults that make company painful, and the gaps for the courtesy that Schopenhauer says is learned from both.

**In a reading.** A question of closeness: how near to stand to a friend, a family, a colleague. The card does not advise withdrawal. It describes a relationship at which the warmth is real and the distance has been worked out.

**Counterweight.** The parable ends in an equilibrium, which is a less pessimistic result than the rest of the tradition would predict. Attachment research and Aristotle's account of friendship suggest that intimacy changes the quills: people who know each other well hurt each other less, not more. The image also assumes a fixed physical nature in both parties.

**Links.** XII — Compassion: the capacity that, in Schopenhauer, allows closeness without injury. X — The Insomniac: the isolated end of the range. XIII — The Broom: solidarity as the case in which the cold forces cooperation.

## IX — The Shattered God

**Source.** Philipp Mainländer, *Die Philosophie der Erlösung* (*The Philosophy of Redemption*), volume I, 1876; volume II, 1886. Mainländer was born in 1841 and died in 1876, the day after the first volume was published. No scholarly encyclopedia entry was found, and the summary below rests on Wikipedia and on a recent journal article by A. V. Popkov, "The Will to Die as a Basic Metaphysical Principle", which was seen only as a search result. No public-domain English translation was located, so the card carries no quotation.

**Argument.** Mainländer begins from an original unity, God, who wished for non-existence and could reach it only by dissolving into a world of many things. The world is the remains of that act: matter, life and the stars are God's fragments, driven by a will to die that underlies the apparent will to live. History tends to nothingness. Unlike Schopenhauer, Mainländer takes the will as plural, with many finite wills and not one, and holds that death brings absolute nothingness. His ethics follows from the metaphysics, and he argued that enlightened self-interest leads toward asceticism and, as a social aim, toward the gradual decline of the species.

**Symbols.** A vast pale sphere breaks along fine cracks, and its fragments drift outward, becoming stars, stones, a tree, a fish, a bird and a small walking figure. The sphere is featureless because Mainländer's original unity cannot be pictured. The things of the world appear as its remains, and their dispersal as the slow course of its dying.

**In a reading.** A sense that the parts of one's world are remains of something that has ended: a family, an institution, a belief. The card names the condition of living among fragments and invites the question of what the whole was.

**Counterweight.** The system is a speculative cosmology without independent evidence, and it asserts a divine act that has no witness. Schopenhauer's single will was criticised for its remoteness from experience, and Mainländer's plurality does not escape the charge. Nietzsche, in *The Gay Science* §357, dismissed Mainländer in a single scornful phrase.

**Links.** VII — Nature and the Icelander: an indifferent nature, here given a history. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: Schopenhauer's denial of the will, which Mainländer reinterprets. XV — The Last Messiah: another proposal for an orderly end.

## X — The Insomniac

**Source.** Emil Cioran, *Pe culmile disperării* (*On the Heights of Despair*, 1934, written in Romanian); *Précis de décomposition* (*A Short History of Decay*, Gallimard, 1949); *De l'inconvénient d'être né* (*The Trouble with Being Born*, Gallimard, 1973). Cioran suffered from insomnia from the age of twenty for the rest of his life and wrote that his first book grew out of a sleepless night. He died in Paris in 1995. These details come from a single encyclopedia page. No public-domain translation exists, and the card has no quotation.

**Argument.** Cioran writes in aphorisms and does not build an argument. His theme is lucidity: the wakeful mind sees what sleep and habit hide, and cannot stop seeing it. Being born is an inconvenience, a condition imposed on a person that he spends his life accommodating. On suicide, Cioran was drawn to the idea and regarded it as a consolation that helped him to keep living; he did not recommend the act. The card draws on the insomnia and the lucidity, and not on that thought.

**Symbols.** A man sits awake at an open window at night with a lamp beside him, above a city that sleeps. The lamp stands for lucidity, which keeps him company and keeps him awake. The image inverts Goya's Capricho 43, in which reason sleeps and the creatures of the night are awake; here the world sleeps and one mind does not.

**In a reading.** A period of wakefulness in which one sees clearly and without relief: a night of worry, a long illness, an obsession with a question. The card names lucidity as a condition that has to be endured and that the sufferer can learn to use.

**Counterweight.** A critic can ask whether wakefulness gives accurate sight or a distortion of its own, because the exhausted mind is a poor judge of the value of life. Aphorism also leaves its claims unargued, and Dienstag counts Cioran among the existential pessimists who write for the edification of the reader. Camus offered the opposite resolution: lucidity as the ground of a lived happiness.

**Links.** I — The Giant Deer: the same excess of mind, now in the night. VI — The Pendulum: the boredom that fills the hours. XI — The Stone: the response of a lucid person who decides to carry on.

## XI — The Stone

**Source.** Albert Camus, *Le Mythe de Sisyphe* (*The Myth of Sisyphus*), written in 1941 and published in 1942. The summary rests on the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Camus, and no public-domain translation exists, so the card has no quotation.

**Argument.** The absurd arises from the gap between the human demand for meaning and the silence of the world. Camus opens by calling suicide the one serious philosophical problem, since it asks whether life is worth living. He rejects it, and he rejects the two other exits from the absurd, hope in another world and a great cause. He recommends lucid revolt: to live without consolation and without reconciling oneself. Sisyphus, condemned to push a stone that rolls back, is the model. In the descent, when he knows his situation fully, he is superior to his fate. Camus concludes that one must imagine Sisyphus happy.

**Symbols.** Sisyphus walks down the mountain with a steady step, and the boulder lies at rest at its foot. Camus singles out this descent, when Sisyphus returns to his task knowing it fully. The stone stands for a labour without result, and the empty hands for the pause in which the labour can be faced.

**In a reading.** Persistence in work without a promise of result. The card names the disposition of someone who continues with a task that is neither finished nor justified, and the possibility that the continuing is its own answer.

**Counterweight.** The Stanford entry notes a shift from scepticism to a normative stance, which it calls a sleight of hand. On Zapffe's terms, revolt could be read as one of the anchoring mechanisms, a way of fixing attention on a task so as to avoid the panic, which would make the happy Sisyphus another of the evasions. Schopenhauer would say that the stone is the will itself, and that carrying it gladly misunderstands the problem.

**Links.** 0 — The Unborn: the premise Camus accepts without the conclusion. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: renunciation as the opposite answer. VI — The Pendulum: the same repeated cycle, met with a different attitude.

## XII — Compassion

**Source.** Arthur Schopenhauer, *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung*, volume I, book IV, §§66–69; and *Über die Grundlage der Moral* (*On the Basis of Morality*, 1840). In the Haldane–Kemp translation of the first work, *Mitleid* is rendered "sympathy". The three incentives of the second work, egoism, malice and compassion, were confirmed only through secondary summaries, which cite §16 for the discussion. The Payne translation was not consulted.

**Argument.** Schopenhauer holds that actions arise from three fundamental incentives: egoism, which seeks one's own good; malice, which seeks another's harm; and compassion, which seeks another's good for its own sake. Only compassion gives an action moral worth. It rests on a recognition that the suffering of another is, at the level of the will, one's own. In the Haldane–Kemp translation: "all true and pure love is sympathy, and all love which is not sympathy is selfishness." Schopenhauer argues against suicide on the same ground: in §69 he writes that it is "a phenomenon of strong assertion of will", because the person who ends his life rejects the conditions of his life and not the will to live.

**Symbols.** A woman kneels beside a stranger lying by the road, her hand on his shoulder, while a boy brings water to a dog with a bandaged leg. The stranger is unknown to her, which marks compassion as a response to suffering as such, without a tie of kin. The dog follows Schopenhauer's insistence that compassion extends to animals.

**In a reading.** The pull toward helping someone because they suffer, with no calculation of return. The card names a moral disposition and the cost of keeping it, which is that the helper takes on part of the pain.

**Counterweight.** Nietzsche treated pity as a weakness that multiplies suffering by sharing it. A second objection comes from within the system: if life is on the whole an evil, compassion that preserves and comforts the sufferer prolongs the evil, and Schopenhauer's reply is that it is a step toward renunciation. Hartmann objected that individual renunciation is a private escape that leaves the world-process untouched.

**Links.** VIII — The Porcupines: the distance that compassion crosses. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: the next step in Schopenhauer's sequence. XIII — The Broom: compassion made collective.

## XIII — The Broom

**Source.** Giacomo Leopardi, "La ginestra, o il fiore del deserto" ("The Broom, or the Flower of the Desert", 1836), his last major poem. Only secondary summaries were consulted, from Italian study sites and an article in the journal *Between* (2015). The primary text and the line numbers were not checked, and the reference to the "social chain" at line 149 comes from the *Between* article.

**Argument.** The poem is set on the slope of Vesuvius, where a broom grows on the hardened lava. The poet addresses a century that believes in progress and invites its readers to the mountain, where the power of nature and the smallness of humanity are visible. The broom accepts its situation without denial and without pride. Against a nature that harms all people alike, Leopardi proposes a *social catena*, a social chain: a union of human beings who recognise a common enemy and give each other help. Solidarity is the reasoned response to pessimism.

**Symbols.** Broom flowers on black lava, with Vesuvius smoking above and a ruined Roman town below. The broom stands for a life that grows where it has been placed, without denying the danger or claiming to defeat it. The buried town shows what the mountain has already done, and no people are drawn: the human chain the poem calls for is left to the reader.

**In a reading.** Dignity in a precarious place and the decision to join with others in it. The card names the stance of someone who has seen the situation clearly and works with others on the ground that remains.

**Counterweight.** A pessimist can ask what grounds the solidarity. If existence is on the whole an evil, the reason to sustain a human chain against nature is unclear, and Zapffe would count the appeal as an anchoring mechanism. A different objection is that the poem's tone toward the optimism of its time is sarcastic, which sits oddly with its own constructive proposal. The secondary sources disagree on how far the poem is a political programme.

**Links.** VII — Nature and the Icelander: the indifferent nature that the broom endures. XII — Compassion: the personal disposition from which the chain is made. IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the faith the poem opposes.

## XIV — The Stilled Wheel

**Source.** Arthur Schopenhauer, *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung*, volume I: §38, on aesthetic contemplation, and §68, on asceticism and the denial of the will. Both sections were confirmed in the Haldane–Kemp translation and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Schopenhauer.

**Argument.** In aesthetic contemplation the knower becomes a pure, will-less subject for a time, and the striving that drives the pendulum is suspended. In the Haldane–Kemp translation: "the wheel of Ixion stands still." The state is temporary. The lasting form is the denial of the will in §68: the saint, seeing through the illusion of separate individuals, ceases to will, accepts poverty and chastity, and reaches a calm that Schopenhauer describes as a relative nothing, the absence of the will and not its opposite. He distinguishes this sharply from suicide, which he regards as an affirmation of the will (§69).

**Symbols.** A large spoked wheel stands motionless in long grass, ivy over its rim, and a figure sits beside it facing away, toward a wide valley. The wheel is Ixion's, empty and at rest, as in Schopenhauer's image of aesthetic contemplation. The ivy shows that it has been still for some time. The seated figure looks at the view without wanting anything from it.

**In a reading.** A pause in the cycle of wanting, in music, a landscape or a piece of work. The card names relief that comes from attention and not from acquisition, and notes that it ends.

**Counterweight.** Nietzsche rejected the denial of the will as hostility to life and held that the will to power affirms it. Hartmann objected that the ascetic seeks a private escape and leaves the world-process untouched. There is also a doubt about the argument: if the saint's calm is a relative nothing, the description has little content, and Schopenhauer admits that it cannot be put in words.

**Links.** VI — The Pendulum: the swing that is stilled. XII — Compassion: the step before it in Schopenhauer's sequence. XI — The Stone: the opposite answer, which keeps willing.

## XV — The Last Messiah

**Source.** Peter Wessel Zapffe, "Den sidste Messias", *Janus*, 1933, in the Philosophy Now translation (issue 45). The essay sets out four mechanisms for damping consciousness and closes with the Messiah's message.

**Argument.** People survive, in Zapffe's account, by limiting what enters consciousness. Isolation shuts out disturbing thought. Anchoring fixes the flow of experience to points such as God, the nation or the family. Distraction fills attention. Sublimation turns pain into art, and Zapffe treats it as the rarest. After a series of failed saviours there comes a last Messiah, who sees the human condition fully. The Philosophy Now translation gives his message as: "Know yourselves – be infertile and let the earth be silent after ye." The Messiah descends from the first man at the waterhole. Zapffe presents this as a vision and not as a plan.

**Symbols.** A lone man on a rise speaks to a crowd, most of whom have turned their backs and are walking away. Beyond them lies an empty, quiet landscape under a wide sky: the silent earth of his message. The crowd's turning shows the reception Zapffe predicts; in the essay they then turn on him, which the card leaves undrawn.

**In a reading.** The conclusion of a long argument, and the question of whether one is willing to follow it. The card names the extreme end of the tradition and returns the reader to the first card.

**Counterweight.** Camus holds that the absurd is to be lived and that the correct response to a meaningless world is revolt and not extinction. Antinatalist arguments of this kind depend on contested premises, like Benatar's asymmetry. Hartmann, who also imagined a collective end of the world-process, saw that it would need nearly universal agreement among people who could communicate with one another, which he did not expect soon. Zapffe's own essay, which belongs to the rare mechanism of sublimation, may be read as an instance of what it describes.

**Links.** 0 — The Unborn: the card to which it leads. I — The Giant Deer: where the essay begins. IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: Hartmann's collective alternative, by willing and not by refusal.
