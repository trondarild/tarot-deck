# Pessimist deck: symbolism of the minor cards

The minor series has 56 cards in four suits, one for each of the mechanisms that Peter Wessel Zapffe describes in "The Last Messiah" (1933): Isolation, Anchoring, Distraction and Sublimation. Zapffe argues that human consciousness exceeds what survival needs, and that people live by damping it. The structure is set out in the proposal in `pessimistic-philosophy-research.md`, and the major cards it refers to are described in `pessimistic-symbolism.md`.

Each suit has ten pip cards and four court cards. Pip *n* shows the suit's mechanism applied to the truth of major card *n*: Isolation dismisses it, Anchoring walls it in with a fixed point such as faith, family or a cause, Distraction keeps attention elsewhere, and Sublimation, the rarest and the only productive mechanism, gives it form. In a reading, a pip card points back to the major card with its number. The three evasive suits stay with avoidance; the Sublimation suit leads toward the response cards XI–XV.

The court cards are the four ages of life: the Child, the Youth, the Adult and the Elder. Hartmann tied his three illusions to childhood, youth and adulthood, and Schopenhauer wrote on the periods of life in the *Aphorisms on the Wisdom of Life*; the Elder is the age the three illusions leave out.

Each entry carries a Defence paragraph in place of a pitfall. Zapffe treats the mechanisms as necessary for living, and the Defence states the case for each evasion fairly. Pip entries give the evasion, a suggested image, the meaning, a reading, the defence and links; court entries give the age, the figure, the meaning, a reading, the defence and links. Image suggestions are notes for future artwork in the deck's etching style.

## One of Isolation

**Evasion.** The truth of the Giant Deer is that awareness exceeds what the day requires. Isolation meets it by dismissing the surplus as it arises: a thought at the edge of attention is labelled overthinking and put down. The habit is taught early and is usually kind.

**Image.** A woman at a kitchen table pauses with a cup half raised. Her free hand lays a lid over a glass jar in which a small antlered shadow presses against the glass. The window behind her is dark.

**Meaning.** The first small act of exclusion: a thought judged unhelpful and sent away.

**In a reading.** A thought has been set aside before it was finished. The card asks what it was going to say, and can mark either a sound sense of proportion or the start of a long habit.

**Defence.** A mind that followed every implication would not get through breakfast. Zapffe holds that the surplus of consciousness must be damped for a person to function, and isolation is the cheapest means, since it needs no belief and no occupation.

**Links.** I — The Giant Deer: the excess being set aside. X — The Insomniac: the hour at which the setting aside fails.

## Two of Isolation

**Evasion.** The First Illusion holds that happiness is to be had here and now. When a good moment shows its thinness, a dinner already ending or a holiday already half spent, isolation removes the observation. The guest declines to notice that the pleasure is fading and attends to the next dish.

**Image.** A long banquet table under lanterns, the guests in profile and in high spirits. One diner at the end looks into his glass, where the candle flames are shrinking. A servant behind him lifts a cloth over the dessert.

**Meaning.** The refusal to inspect a pleasure while it lasts.

**In a reading.** An enjoyment is being shielded from scrutiny. The card suggests that scrutiny would have found something real, and that skipping it is a choice.

**Defence.** Pleasure examined too closely seldom survives the examination, and few people regret leaving it for later. A guest who audits every dinner has lost the means of enjoying one, and Zapffe would count the looking away as what the species has always done.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the hope whose weak point is left alone. V — Vanity: the Preacher takes the opposite course and looks.

## Three of Isolation

**Evasion.** The Second Illusion places fulfilment after death. In practice the thought of death is fenced off, and a formula of consolation closes the subject. At the graveside someone says that the dead are at peace, and the talk turns to lunch.

**Image.** Mourners in dark coats stand in rows at a cemetery. A priest raises a hand to the sky, and the people nearest the grave watch his hand and not the hole. A child at the edge has been turned to face the road.

**Meaning.** A hope used as a door shut on a question.

**In a reading.** A consoling phrase is doing the work of silence. The card does not doubt the comfort. It asks whether the comfort was meant to end the thought or to accompany it.

**Defence.** Grief is borne better in company and ritual than in analysis, and the phrases that close a subject also let people stand together at the worst hour. A funeral is where looking is shared out.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the hope that supplies the formula. 0 — The Unborn: the question before life, fenced off as firmly.

## Four of Isolation

**Evasion.** The Third Illusion hopes that the world will improve and justify what it costs. Isolation keeps the hope intact by controlling intake: the report of a failed programme is passed over, and the report of a successful one is read twice.

**Image.** A man in a waistcoat sits among stacks of newspapers and cuts out columns with scissors. The cuttings fall into a basket, and the pages he keeps are pinned in neat rows. Through the open door a railway cutting runs toward a smoking horizon.

**Meaning.** Selective reading: a faith kept by filtering what reaches it.

**In a reading.** Information is being sorted by whether it can be borne. The card asks what has been cut out, and whether the view that remains is sound or only comfortable.

**Defence.** No one can carry all the world's news, and a person who tried would be of use to nobody. The filter protects the energy for work that may do some good. Zapffe describes it as one of the means by which the species continues.

**Links.** IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the hope the filter keeps alive. XIII — The Broom: the poem that looks at the mountain.

## Five of Isolation

**Evasion.** Vanity is the finding that labour yields no lasting profit. Isolation keeps the finding out of working hours by treating the question of purpose as one for another occasion. It is entered in the calendar for a quieter month and not reached.

**Image.** A clerk at a high desk writes steadily in an open ledger. In the margin a small skull has been inked and half scratched out. On a shelf behind him a row of hourglasses is turned to face the wall.

**Meaning.** The postponement of the question of worth.

**In a reading.** A question about the point of the work is being deferred. The card notes that the deferral has a date, and that the date has passed more than once.

**Defence.** Most work is done well by people who have not settled its ultimate point, and some would not be done if they had to. Setting the question aside lets the task go forward. The Preacher himself advises enjoying toil as a gift, and Zapffe treats such suspension as ordinary practice.

**Links.** V — Vanity: the question put off. VI — The Pendulum: the flat hours the question would otherwise fill.

## Six of Isolation

**Evasion.** The Pendulum swings between pain and boredom. The flat end of the swing, a Sunday evening or the hour after a goal is reached, is felt and then renamed. It is called tiredness or a passing mood, so that it is never taken as information about the arrangement of life.

**Image.** A man in an armchair at dusk, his coat still on, watches a clock on the mantel whose pendulum is hidden behind a small curtain. A tray of tea has gone cold at his elbow.

**Meaning.** The renaming of a mood so that it need not be examined.

**In a reading.** A recurring flatness has been given a harmless name. The name may be accurate; the card suggests testing it once.

**Defence.** Moods pass, and many described as emptiness are fatigue that sleep will correct. A person who read each dip as a verdict on existence would be exhausted by his own attention. Zapffe's mechanisms let a creature go on without reading each swing as a message.

**Links.** VI — The Pendulum: the swing whose far end is renamed. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: the pause that comes from attending, where this card flinches.

## Seven of Isolation

**Evasion.** Leopardi's Nature owes human beings nothing and does not notice their loss. Isolation keeps the fact at a distance by reading accident as a story: an illness or storm is given a cause, a lesson or a villain, and where none can be found the account is not asked for.

**Image.** A family sits at a lamplit supper while a storm wrecks the shore beyond the window. The mother draws the curtain with one hand and serves the soup with the other. Outside, a giantess, hardly more than a drift of cloud, turns away.

**Meaning.** The habit of not dwelling on what cannot be addressed.

**In a reading.** A loss has no author, and attention has moved elsewhere. The card asks whether the elsewhere is a rest or an avoidance.

**Defence.** A grievance with no one to receive it can occupy a whole life. Turning from it lets a household eat, sleep and mend what can be mended. Zapffe counts such turning as part of how people stay alive in a world that does not answer.

**Links.** VII — Nature and the Icelander: the silence being turned from. XII — Compassion: the opposite movement, toward another's suffering.

## Eight of Isolation

**Evasion.** The Porcupines learn a bearable distance. Isolation fixes it by agreement not to speak of what pricks: two relatives keep to the weather, and the unspoken quarrel stays in a closed room of the house.

**Image.** Two people sit at either end of a long sofa, each reading, with a low table between them carrying a lamp and a covered dish. A door at the back of the room stands ajar on darkness. Neither looks toward it.

**Meaning.** The silence kept between people for the sake of peace.

**In a reading.** A subject between two people is by tacit consent not raised. The card marks the arrangement as workable and asks how long it has been in force.

**Defence.** Many close relations survive because certain things are not said. Schopenhauer holds that courtesy is the distance found by experience, and a distance that lets people share a table is no small achievement. Zapffe would see the silence as a useful partition.

**Links.** VIII — The Porcupines: the distance being kept. XII — Compassion: what might cross it.

## Nine of Isolation

**Evasion.** Mainländer's God is gone, and the world is what remains. Isolation avoids the thought by treating the broken thing as housekeeping. A family that has come apart after a death sorts the objects, shares them out and does not discuss what the whole was.

**Image.** In a house being cleared, a woman wraps china in newspaper. A cracked tureen has been set aside on a chair, its pieces lying in the shape of a sphere. She wraps the plate in her hand with care and does not look at the chair.

**Meaning.** The managing of remains in place of mourning what they were.

**In a reading.** Something has ended, and its practical aftermath is handled with great efficiency. The efficiency is itself a response, and the card asks when the other one is due.

**Defence.** The practical tasks after a loss are real, and doing them is often how a person stays upright through the first weeks. The packing of china is among the oldest of Zapffe's mechanisms at work. The grief is postponed and not abolished.

**Links.** IX — The Shattered God: the whole of which these are fragments. XI — The Stone: a way of continuing that keeps the loss in view.

## Ten of Isolation

**Evasion.** The Insomniac cannot stop seeing. Isolation answers the lucid hour with dismissals: it is the coffee, the hour, the lack of sleep, and things will look different in the morning. The thought is treated as a symptom of the night.

**Image.** A man sits up in bed with a lamp lit, a glass of water and a closed book on the table. The grey of early morning begins at the window. On the wall the large shadow of his head leans over him, and the edge of the blanket covers it.

**Meaning.** Lucidity treated as a condition of the hour.

**In a reading.** A clear thought arrived at a bad time and has been discounted for that reason. The hour affects the mood and does not decide whether the thought was true.

**Defence.** A tired mind judges badly, and discounting the conclusions of three in the morning is usually sound practice. Zapffe describes isolation as a necessary mechanism, and its use at night is the ordinary way of getting to the day.

**Links.** X — The Insomniac: the lucidity being discounted. I — The Giant Deer: the excess that returns when the lamp is lit.

## Child of Isolation

**Age.** In childhood the mechanism works almost without effort. Hartmann ties the first illusion to the early years, and Schopenhauer describes the child as living in the present, without a view of the whole. A frightening fact is dropped as quickly as it is picked up, and play closes over it.

**Figure.** A girl of seven sits on a garden step beside a dead bird in a shoebox. She has covered it with a leaf and is looking at a snail. Behind her a window shows an adult watching, hand on the glass, not coming out.

**Meaning.** The natural forgetting of the young.

**In a reading.** A matter that frightens or saddens has been put down with ease. The card marks a capacity for recovery and asks whether the thing has been set aside or not yet understood.

**Defence.** Children could not grow if each loss had to be taken in whole, and parents rightly protect the ease with which they turn away. Zapffe's mechanisms start here, as a mercy practised before the creature knows it is practising anything.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the garden where turning away comes easily. 0 — The Unborn: the question not yet asked.

## Youth of Isolation

**Age.** Schopenhauer observes that the young look to the future with expectation, and Hartmann places the second illusion in youth. The mechanism takes the form of confidence: warnings from older people are heard as a description of those people, and thoughts of failure as a lapse of nerve.

**Figure.** A young man in a travelling coat stands on a quay with a trunk, his back to older relatives who are speaking to him. An old woman in the group raises a hand, as if to ask a question she has decided not to put.

**Meaning.** The refusal of counsel by those who feel they have time.

**In a reading.** Advice is being heard and not taken in. The card marks a season of boldness and asks which part of the advice was true.

**Defence.** Plans of any size require some deafness to the odds. A youth who weighed all that might go wrong would not leave the quay. Zapffe treats the damping of foresight as a condition of undertaking anything.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the hope that carries the young. IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the faith that makes deafness feel like vision.

## Adult of Isolation

**Age.** Schopenhauer holds that middle life brings knowledge of how things go and a growing load of cares, and Hartmann ties the third illusion to adulthood. The mechanism becomes routine: the thought is reserved for later, the diagnosis for next month, the difficult conversation for after the holiday.

**Figure.** A woman in her forties stands at a kitchen counter at the end of a workday, a pile of unopened letters beside her. She reads the label of a jar with great attention. A note by the telephone on the wall has been turned face down.

**Meaning.** Administration used as a way of not attending.

**In a reading.** A matter of weight is being handled as logistics. The card asks which of the unopened letters it is.

**Defence.** The adult carries other people, and the ability to put a worry in a drawer is what lets a household function. Zapffe sees isolation as an economy of attention, and most adults practise it competently. The cost falls later, after a good deal has been bought with it.

**Links.** IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the plan that fills the calendar. VI — The Pendulum: the evening flatness the busyness holds back.

## Elder of Isolation

**Age.** Hartmann's three illusions leave out old age, and Schopenhauer, in his essay on the periods of life, compares the first half of life to the right side of an embroidery and the second half to the wrong side, less handsome but more instructive, because it shows how the threads are joined. Isolation takes two forms: the old habit, practised so long that it is invisible, and a release, in which the dismissals lose their force.

**Figure.** An old man sits in a garden chair with a shawl over his knees, his eyes on a shut gate. On the table beside him lie a bundle of letters tied with string and a pair of reading glasses. One end of the string has been untied.

**Meaning.** The end of the long deferral.

**In a reading.** What was put off for years is now within reach. It may be smaller than was feared, or larger, and either way it can be looked at.

**Defence.** A lifetime of managed attention bought a lifetime of work and affection, and there is no reason to count it a loss. If the mechanism relaxes with age, it does so after it has done its service.

**Links.** X — The Insomniac: the lucidity old age may allow to stay. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: the calm when wanting has eased.

## One of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The Giant Deer names a mind larger than daily life needs. Anchoring contains it by giving the mind a single fixed point, usually an occupation or a role, so that attention returns to it whenever it begins to range. A person who answers the question of who he is with the name of his post has found a post to tie the antlers to.

**Image.** A stag stands in a farmyard with ribbons and paper tags wound through its antlers, the other ends knotted to a fence post. A child on tiptoe is tying on one more tag. The sky behind is empty and wide, and the ground has been trodden bare in a circle.

**Meaning.** The card names the first fixing of a point: the title, the trade or the creed that lets a restless mind say what it is for.

**In a reading.** A wide mind has found one thing to be. The card marks a person who answers the large question with a small, definite one, and who is steadier for it. It asks how long the cord is.

**Defence.** Zapffe holds that a consciousness left to its full extent cannot keep going, and that people survive by damping it. A fixed role does this cheaply. It gives the day a shape, the self a name and the excess of thought a place to rest. Most lives that look settled owe that settlement to some such post.

**Links.** I — The Giant Deer: the excess that the post is meant to hold in check. X — The Insomniac: the night, when the title is put down and the excess returns.

## Two of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The First Illusion locates happiness in this life, in the present. Anchoring holds off the failure of that hope by attaching it to a person: a partner, a child, a household. If the goods of the world disappoint, the family remains, and it supplies the contentment that pleasure alone did not. Disappointment is then read as a fault in the arrangements and not in the hope.

**Image.** A small kitchen at evening, in the manner of Goya's domestic scenes. A family sits close around a table lit by one lamp, the figures overlapping so that no gap shows between them. Through the window behind them a crowd of strangers passes in the dark street.

**Meaning.** The card names happiness lodged in other people, and the walls that a household builds around it.

**In a reading.** Contentment is placed in a home and the people in it. The card describes a settled and warm arrangement, and asks whether the warmth would last if one of its members left.

**Defence.** Affection is among the strongest sources of meaning that people report, and Zapffe counts the family among the sturdiest anchors for that reason. It gives purpose to effort that would otherwise seem arbitrary. Few critics of the first illusion would wish a person to lose it before something else was in place.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the hope the household shelters. VIII — The Porcupines: the distance that family life has to work out.

## Three of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The Second Illusion defers happiness to an afterlife. Anchoring takes the hope and builds it into a practice: a church, a calendar of observance, a community that confirms the belief by sharing it. The belief is then supported by habit and company, and it does not have to be re-examined each time doubt arises.

**Image.** A village congregation kneels in a stone church, in the manner of Goya's engravings of crowds. Their heads are bowed in rows, and the vault above them is painted with a sky. A single figure at the back looks up through a broken window pane at the real one.

**Meaning.** The card names faith held in place by structure, and the structure that holds it.

**In a reading.** A hope for a later reward is kept steady by routine and community. The card does not ask whether the belief is true. It asks what the practice does for the person who keeps it.

**Defence.** Zapffe names God first among the anchors, and the choice reflects how much weight the belief has carried. It gives death a meaning, grief a limit and conduct a measure. Communities of faith also supply company, ritual and care, which do not depend on the doctrine being proved.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the hope the practice secures. XII — Compassion: the care that congregations often supply, whatever their doctrine.

## Four of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The Third Illusion places happiness in the progress of the world. Anchoring attaches a person to a cause or an institution that is moving toward a goal: a party, a movement, a nation. Labour for it fills the days, and the distance still to be travelled accounts for any present dissatisfaction.

**Image.** A column of marchers carries banners through a town square, in the manner of Klinger's crowd scenes. Every banner has been painted with a different device, and the leaders at the front hold theirs high. A woman at the roadside holds a child up to see, and the road ahead narrows into haze.

**Meaning.** The card names a loyalty to a collective project, and the energy that the project returns to its members.

**In a reading.** A cause or an organisation gives the work a direction. The card describes the steadiness of belonging to something that is going somewhere, and asks what will remain if it arrives or fails.

**Defence.** Zapffe lists the nation and the cause among the anchors, and they are the ones with the widest use. Collective projects have built schools, drained marshes and ended abuses, and those who joined them were seldom troubled by cosmic panic. A person who works for something larger than himself is generally easier to live with.

**Links.** IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the faith that the cause borrows. XIII — The Broom: a common effort that makes no promise of arrival.

## Five of Anchoring

**Evasion.** Vanity finds that labour under the sun leaves no profit. Anchoring answers by making the labour a calling. Work is given a name, a ladder and a reputation, and a person measures it by position and not by what remains afterwards. The question of what the toil is for is replaced by the question of what comes next.

**Image.** A clerk sits at a tall desk in a room of identical desks, writing in a ledger, and a framed certificate hangs above him. Through the open door a long corridor of other offices recedes. On the windowsill a candle burns down unnoticed in the daylight.

**Meaning.** The card names a career held as a fixed point, and the satisfaction of rank in a hierarchy.

**In a reading.** Work has become the frame of a life. The card describes a person who is absorbed in a career and rewarded by it, and who would find it hard to say what the days would hold without it.

**Defence.** Work is among the oldest sources of structure, skill and standing, and Zapffe includes the career with the anchors for that reason. It spends the hours in a way that other people recognise. The craftsman and the official are often content, and the content is real even when the Preacher would doubt its object.

**Links.** V — Vanity: the question the career answers by distraction from it. XI — The Stone: labour continued with the question left open.

## Six of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The Pendulum swings between pain and boredom. Anchoring tries to hold the swing by a fixed routine: the same hours, the same places, the same company. Routine takes out the extremes of want and of emptiness and brings the two states closer together, so that neither is felt in full.

**Image.** A man walks the same path along a canal at the same hour, in the manner of an etching of a town at dusk. The lamplighter, the baker and a woman at a window all appear in the same places they appeared the day before. A clock on the tower has no hands.

**Meaning.** The card names the steadying effect of habit, and the habits that a life is built on.

**In a reading.** A regular pattern keeps both restlessness and flatness at a distance. The card describes a life that runs on rhythm, and asks whether the rhythm has become the purpose.

**Defence.** Habit relieves the mind of continual decision and spares it the work of finding something to want. Zapffe's mechanisms operate mostly in this way, quietly and without the person's attention. Much of what passes for a tranquil temperament is a well-kept routine, and the benefits are plain in the years that go by without incident.

**Links.** VI — The Pendulum: the swing that the routine flattens. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: a pause reached by attention and not by repetition.

## Seven of Anchoring

**Evasion.** Nature does not care what happens to human beings. Anchoring keeps this out of view by attaching a person to a place and a people: a home town, a homeland, a language, a landscape that is felt to be one's own. A territory with a name and a history looks as if it had been made for those who live in it.

**Image.** A farmer stands at the edge of his field with a boundary stone beside him, and a flag hangs on a pole behind the barn. Beyond the fence the land continues unmarked to a range of mountains. A flock of birds crosses the line without altering course.

**Meaning.** The card names belonging to a place, and the borders that declare the place to be a home.

**In a reading.** Attachment to a homeland or a locality makes a large and indifferent world seem familiar. The card describes the comfort of belonging, and the line beyond which belonging does not extend.

**Defence.** A person without a place has a harder life, and Zapffe puts the nation among the anchors because it gives so many people a definite sense of where they stand. Local loyalty also produces care for land and neighbours that an abstract concern for the world seldom matches.

**Links.** VII — Nature and the Icelander: the indifference that the border does not reach. XIII — The Broom: a bond between people that does not rest on a territory.

## Eight of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The Porcupines find a distance at which company is bearable. Anchoring fixes that distance by institution: marriage, the club, the office, the guild. Roles and rules decide how close people stand, and the pain of misjudging the distance is mostly avoided because the form has settled the question in advance.

**Image.** Guests sit at a long table, each at a marked place, and the cutlery is laid in exact rows. Between each pair of neighbours a small gap has been left, wide enough for a hand. At the head of the table a master of ceremonies holds a staff, in the manner of Goya's satirical gatherings.

**Meaning.** The card names the form that regulates closeness, and the relief it gives to those who follow it.

**In a reading.** Convention and role settle how near people stand to one another. The card describes a relationship that works because its terms are fixed, and asks what the terms leave unsaid.

**Defence.** Schopenhauer found good manners to be the distance learned from experience, and a formal arrangement preserves that knowledge. Rules spare people the injuries of working it out afresh. Many bonds that would not have survived intimacy have lasted well under a form.

**Links.** VIII — The Porcupines: the distance that the form fixes in advance. XII — Compassion: the response that goes beyond the places the form assigns.

## Nine of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The Shattered God shows a world of fragments with no whole behind it. Anchoring answers with a tradition, a lineage or a cause large enough to be treated as the whole: a body of belief, a family name, a heritage handed down. The fragments are read as parts of something that still holds together.

**Image.** An old man stands in a gallery of portraits, each of his ancestors in a gilt frame. The frames at the far end hold only cracked canvas, and he faces the nearer ones. On the floor a child assembles a broken jar from its pieces.

**Meaning.** The card names the effort to treat the fragments as a whole by means of a tradition.

**In a reading.** A heritage or an institution is held up as the thing that makes the pieces cohere. The card describes loyalty to a continuity, and asks who maintains it, and at what cost.

**Defence.** Tradition gives a person a place in a sequence that began before him and will continue after, which softens the thought of ending. Zapffe's anchors work by supplying firm points in the flow, and a lineage is one of the oldest. The piety it asks is often repaid in a sense of being known.

**Links.** IX — The Shattered God: the scattered world that the tradition gathers up. XV — The Last Messiah: the end of the line, which the lineage keeps out of sight.

## Ten of Anchoring

**Evasion.** The Insomniac sees clearly in the night. Anchoring moves the same hours onto something fixed, so that waking thought is already occupied: a prayer, a ledger, a task for tomorrow. Lucidity then runs along a prepared track and reaches only the points that the track allows.

**Image.** A woman sits up in bed at night with a rosary, or a ring of keys, in her hand, and a list is pinned to the wall by the lamp. Beyond the window the sky is dark and without stars. Her face is calm, and the shadow of the bedpost falls across the page like a bar.

**Meaning.** The card names the holding-fast used against the small hours.

**In a reading.** A fixed thought or routine is used to get through sleeplessness. The card describes the means a person has found to keep from the open question, and whether they have been enough.

**Defence.** The small hours are hard, and a settled practice is a decent defence against them. Zapffe does not treat the damping of consciousness as a failing, since without it most people could not sleep, work or care for anyone. Nothing in the card suggests that the prayer or the list should be given up.

**Links.** X — The Insomniac: the lucidity the fixed thought keeps within bounds. XI — The Stone: a way of continuing that does not claim to settle the question.

## Child of Anchoring

**Age.** Childhood. Hartmann assigned the first illusion, happiness in the present life, to the child. The anchors at this age are chosen by other people: parents, home, the school, the faith of the household. The child has no need to hold them in place, because the surrounding adults do it, and the world appears as it is told.

**Figure.** A small child holds the hand of an adult who is larger than the frame, in the manner of Goya's drawings of children. The child looks up and not ahead. Behind the pair a gate stands open on a road that neither of them looks at.

**Meaning.** The card names the anchor given before it could be chosen.

**In a reading.** Security rests on someone else's arrangements. The card describes a person who holds to what was handed over in childhood, and who may not yet know it as a choice.

**Defence.** A child needs fixed points before it can understand why it needs them, and the loss of them in early years is among the hardest things to repair. Zapffe's mechanisms are mostly adopted without reflection, and the child has the best claim to this.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: Hartmann's stage of childhood. III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the faith that is passed down with the household.

## Youth of Anchoring

**Age.** Youth. Hartmann tied the second illusion, happiness hereafter, to this age, when a person goes looking for something to give himself to. The young adopt an anchor with enthusiasm: a creed, a movement, a vocation, a person. The commitment is often total, because there is not yet anything else to weigh it against.

**Figure.** A young man stands before a crowd with a flag in his hands, or a young woman at the door of a seminary, in either case with a bag packed at their feet. Their gaze is fixed on the horizon, and the light falls on the face. Behind them the road they have left runs back to a small house.

**Meaning.** The card names the anchor chosen for the first time and held with conviction.

**In a reading.** A person has found something to give himself to and gives it everything. The card marks the strength of the first commitment, and the quiet that comes if it turns out to be one of several.

**Defence.** Conviction gives the young a direction and a community at the moment they most need both. Many lasting works and friendships begin in a zeal that later looks excessive. The hardest to bear would be a youth without anything to believe in.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: Hartmann's stage of youth. IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the cause that the young often take up next.

## Adult of Anchoring

**Age.** Adulthood. Hartmann assigned the third illusion, progress, to this age, and it is the age at which anchors are many: a career, a household, a mortgage, a standing in the community. Schopenhauer's essay on the periods of life treats the middle years as the time of practical engagement, and the load of obligation protects against reflection.

**Figure.** A man in the middle years carries a child on one shoulder and a satchel on the other, in the manner of an etching of a market town. He is walking fast, and the streets are crowded around him. On the wall behind, the posted notices and tax rolls are numerous.

**Meaning.** The card names the many anchors of full adult life and the obligations that come with them.

**In a reading.** Commitments are many and the days are full. The card describes a person who is held by duties on every side, and who has little occasion to ask where the duties lead.

**Defence.** Responsibility for other people is among the most dependable sources of meaning, and it is largely carried out by adults in their middle years. Zapffe names the family and the career among the anchors because they are at once the commonest and the most useful. The world is kept running by people too occupied to doubt it.

**Links.** IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: Hartmann's stage of adulthood. V — Vanity: the question that the full days leave unasked.

## Elder of Anchoring

**Age.** Old age, which Hartmann's three illusions leave out. The anchors of the earlier ages begin to loosen: the career ends, the children leave and friends die. Some elders return to the faith of childhood or hold more tightly to the family and the home. Others find that the supports have given way, and this is the age at which the mechanism is tested.

**Figure.** An old woman sits in a doorway with a worn prayer book, or a bundle of letters, in her lap, in the manner of Goya's late drawings of the aged. A younger person stands beside her with a hand on the chair. The chair is anchored to the wall with a chain of old keys.

**Meaning.** The card names the anchors that remain, and those that have been lost.

**In a reading.** The fixed points of a life are fewer, and the ones left are held with care. The card asks what has been kept, what has been put down, and what steadies a person at the end.

**Defence.** The last years are often easier for those with a faith, a family or a place to which they are tied. Zapffe's mechanisms are no less needed at the end than at the beginning. An old person who holds to a few things firmly has made a sound use of what remains.

**Links.** XV — The Last Messiah: the end toward which the anchors, in the end, all point. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: the calm that comes when the anchors are loosened and not replaced.

## One of Distraction

**Evasion.** The excess of consciousness in card I is held off by never leaving it idle. A radio runs while the dishes are washed, a podcast while the dog is walked, a screen until the light goes out. Each gap in the day is filled before the mind can turn to what it sees.

**Image.** A woman sits at a kitchen table at night, lit from below by a small bright rectangle in her hands. On the wall behind her hangs a hunting trophy of enormous antlers, its shadow spread across the ceiling. She does not look up.

**Meaning.** The card names the habit of continuous input and the unease it covers.

**In a reading.** Every spare minute has been given a task, and none is left for thought.

**Defence.** Zapffe's starting point is that a mind with spare capacity turns it on its own situation, and panic follows. A steady supply of small impressions is the cheapest remedy. It asks for no belief, and it lets a person work and look after others despite an awareness that would otherwise get in the way.

**Links.** I — The Giant Deer: the excess that the distraction occupies. X — The Insomniac: the night, when the supply runs thin.

## Two of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card II holds that satisfaction in the present does not last. Distraction keeps the question from being asked by supplying the next pleasure before the last has faded: the purchase, the outing, the plans for the weekend.

**Image.** An arcade of lit shop windows at dusk. A woman with parcels on both arms stops at the next window, and behind her a parcel lies open on the pavement, its contents half out of the paper and forgotten.

**Meaning.** The card names pleasure used to keep attention moving.

**In a reading.** The next treat is always already chosen. Much of the enjoyment lies in anticipation, and the thing itself is soon put aside.

**Defence.** Pleasures are real, and a day of small ones is better than a day without. A person who is looking forward to something is spared the larger view for the time being, and for many people the rhythm of wanting and getting is the plainest form of ordinary cheer.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the hope that the next pleasure will be the lasting one. VI — The Pendulum: the pause between purchases, which the arrangement avoids.

## Three of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card III places happiness after death, and a parish can hold the question of its truth at a distance through activity: rotas, bazaars, committees, repairs to the roof. The hereafter is so thoroughly arranged for that no one has leisure to ask whether it is there.

**Image.** A church hall after the service, with trestle tables and cake stalls. Women count coins into a tin, and a man on a ladder hangs a banner. Through the open door a priest stands alone in the nave, looking at the altar.

**Meaning.** The card names a belief kept in motion by the work of maintaining it.

**In a reading.** Busy service has taken the place of the question it was meant to answer. The card asks what the hurry protects.

**Defence.** A congregation's activity does real good, in food, company and care for the sick. What lies beyond death is among the questions a mind cannot settle, and a community that sets it aside for shared work has chosen sensibly.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the hope that the activity surrounds. XII — Compassion: the part of the work that needs no belief to justify it.

## Four of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card IV places happiness in the future of the world. News, launches, updates and forecasts keep attention fixed on what comes next, and each novelty is received as a step on the road. Where the road leads is not asked, since the next announcement has arrived.

**Image.** A crowd waits in the dark outside a shuttered shop, wrapped in coats, some on folding chairs. Inside, a clerk sets a single object on a pedestal under a lamp. A clock in the square stands at midnight.

**Meaning.** The card names novelty as a way of living in the future.

**In a reading.** Attention is held by what is new, and the present is a waiting room. The card asks whether the thing awaited has ever, on arrival, been enough.

**Defence.** Curiosity about new things is a source of energy, and some novelties do improve life. Zapffe's concern is with a consciousness that has nothing to occupy it, and a flow of new matter is among the most dependable occupations.

**Links.** IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the faith in the future that the stream of novelty feeds. V — Vanity: the Preacher's remark that nothing is new under the sun.

## Five of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card V finds labour without lasting profit. Distraction answers by multiplying the labour: the full diary, the overflowing inbox, tasks that generate further tasks. Being busy is taken as evidence that the work matters, and the afternoon on which its worth might be asked never comes.

**Image.** A clerk sits at a desk heaped with papers that spill on to the floor and climb the walls, while a boy carries in another pile. On the sill, half hidden by a ledger, stands a small hourglass.

**Meaning.** The card names busyness as protection against the question of purpose.

**In a reading.** The workload has grown until it leaves no room to ask whether the work is worth doing.

**Defence.** Work provides income, structure and a sense of being needed, and an occupied person is useful to others. Zapffe treats such absorption as one of the ways a mind stays functional. Even the Preacher, who calls labour vanity, advises enjoying its fruit.

**Links.** V — Vanity: the futility that the pile covers. XI — The Stone: labour taken up without the excuse of being busy.

## Six of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card VI describes the swing between want and boredom. Distraction shortens the second phase: at the first sign of an empty moment the hand goes to the phone, the radio, the cupboard. The pendulum turns so quickly that the lower end is hardly reached.

**Image.** A man in a railway waiting room reads a newspaper, glances at his watch, folds the paper, takes out another and unfolds it. A pendulum clock swings behind glass over his head, and the travellers on the benches do much the same.

**Meaning.** The card names the quick filling of every idle moment.

**In a reading.** Boredom is avoided before it can be felt. The card asks what happens in the moment before the hand moves.

**Defence.** Boredom is unpleasant, and for a person with no means of bearing it can be worse. A ready supply of small occupations spares everyone the worst of it. A mind that stops may stop at something it cannot face, and Zapffe counts the capacity to keep moving as a sign of health.

**Links.** VI — The Pendulum: the swing whose slow end the card shortens. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: a pause taken in attention instead of being filled.

## Seven of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card VII shows an indifferent nature that does not listen. Distraction keeps this at the edge of attention by turning nature into scenery or programme: the weather forecast, the documentary with its narrating voice, the resort with its loudspeaker. The storm becomes an item.

**Image.** A family sits on a crowded beach under a striped awning, a radio playing, children shrieking at a ball. On the horizon a bank of black cloud rises over the water. Only the lifeguard, on his high chair, has turned to look.

**Meaning.** The card names nature as entertainment, with the indifference left out.

**In a reading.** The surroundings are enjoyed at a distance that keeps them safe. Met without the screen, they would speak less pleasantly.

**Defence.** The beach is a pleasure and the forecast is useful. Attending to the pleasant surface of the world is a condition of ordinary life, and a family that spent the afternoon contemplating the cosmos would have had no afternoon.

**Links.** VII — Nature and the Icelander: the indifferent world that the radio is turned up against. IX — The Shattered God: the account that gives the indifference an origin.

## Eight of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card VIII describes the right distance between people. Distraction keeps a distance without having to find one, by surrounding every meeting with activity: the party, the group message, the television left on during dinner. People are together, and nobody has to come near.

**Image.** A long table crowded with guests, every mouth open and every glass raised, each turned half toward a neighbour and half toward the next sound. At the far end two porcupines sit still on the tablecloth, eating from the same dish.

**Meaning.** The card names company used as noise.

**In a reading.** Conversation has become a way of not being alone with anyone. The evenings are full, and something is missing from them.

**Defence.** Sociability is a pleasure and a duty, and shared noise is a kind of warmth. Schopenhauer's parable grants that the cold drives people together. The party spares each guest the harder task of attending to the others, and often to himself.

**Links.** VIII — The Porcupines: the distance that the crowd lets people avoid settling. XII — Compassion: attention paid to one person, which the noise prevents.

## Nine of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card IX shows a world made of the fragments of something finished. Distraction supplies fragments in quantity and lets attention move among them: the collection, the feed, the puzzle, the hobby with its endless small parts. The whole is never asked for.

**Image.** A man sits at a long workbench sorting broken china into shallow trays by colour and size. Trays cover every surface. On the wall behind him hangs an empty frame.

**Meaning.** The card names the sorting of small things as a way of not seeing the whole.

**In a reading.** Attention is occupied with details that do not add up to a picture. The card asks what the picture was supposed to be.

**Defence.** Collecting and puzzling are among the oldest pleasures and often the most absorbing. They give hands and eyes a task that can be finished, which the larger questions rarely do. The sorter has found, in a shattered world, a scale on which it is possible to live.

**Links.** IX — The Shattered God: the broken whole whose pieces the card handles. XIII — The Broom: a different way of living among remains, which accepts them.

## Ten of Distraction

**Evasion.** Card X is the mind awake at night with nothing to do but see. Distraction fills the dark as it fills the day: the television left on, the radio under the pillow, the screen held above the face. Lucidity is met with a murmur of other people's voices.

**Image.** A man lies in bed in a small room, lit by the blue flicker of a screen, his eyes open. Across the street another window glows blue, and beyond it a row of others.

**Meaning.** The card names noise used against sleeplessness and its thoughts.

**In a reading.** The nights are filled so that the mind has no chance to turn to itself. The screen makes the night easier and leaves the cause of the wakefulness where it was.

**Defence.** Sleep matters, and a person who has lain awake for hours is entitled to whatever helps. Zapffe never asked anyone to meet his thoughts unaided at three in the morning. A voice on the radio keeps a lonely hour company.

**Links.** X — The Insomniac: the lucidity that the noise is set against. VI — The Pendulum: the boredom of the long night.

## Child of Distraction

**Age.** Schopenhauer describes childhood as a time when the world is seen with fresh eyes and the child is occupied almost wholly by what it perceives. Attention goes where colour, sound or movement is, and moves on, so that nothing is held long enough to oppress.

**Figure.** A child of about five sits on a nursery floor among toys and picture-books, reaching for a coloured top while a drum lies half beaten beside it. Through the window the garden stands in rain, unnoticed.

**Meaning.** The card names attention held without effort by a supply of new things.

**In a reading.** An absorption that has not yet become a defence.

**Defence.** For a child distraction is a method of growth. The mind has not yet acquired the excess that Zapffe describes, and play is how it learns the world. Turning a child from a grief to a toy is a kindness that usually works.

**Links.** II — The First Illusion: Happiness Here: the stage Hartmann assigns to childhood. 0 — The Unborn: the condition before awareness has anything to damp.

## Youth of Distraction

**Age.** Schopenhauer describes youth as a restless age, full of longing, in which the future seems to promise more than it will give. Distraction takes the form of constant stimulation: music, parties, fashion, rivalry, the next person met.

**Figure.** A young man in an evening coat stands in the doorway of a ballroom, one foot on the stair to a second room where music is playing, his head turned toward a third. Behind him masked dancers cross the floor in lines.

**Meaning.** The card names appetite for experience used to keep quiet questions away.

**In a reading.** A time of restless movement among attractions, each held lightly. It may also describe a person of any age who keeps the pace of youth because slowing down would raise questions.

**Defence.** The energy of youth needs somewhere to go, and variety of experience is how it finds a place. Dancing is a good use of an evening.

**Links.** III — The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter: the hope Hartmann ties to youth, kept alive by motion. VI — The Pendulum: the swing that the pace is meant to hide.

## Adult of Distraction

**Age.** Hartmann ties adulthood to the hope of progress, and Schopenhauer treats the middle years as the age of plans and duties. Distraction takes the form of scheduling. The diary, the commute and the household tasks use up the hours and leave no room to ask what they add up to.

**Figure.** A man in his forties in a plain dark coat crosses a station concourse at speed, a briefcase in one hand and a telephone at his ear, a child's coat over his arm. A crowd flows around him in both directions.

**Meaning.** The card names obligation used to fill the hours.

**In a reading.** A life managed efficiently, with little left over. The card asks whether the demands are all real, or whether some are sought so that something else can be left unexamined.

**Defence.** Most of the obligations are real. Children need feeding and bills need paying, and the busy adult is doing what his position requires. Responsibility serves others as well as the self.

**Links.** IV — The Third Illusion: Progress: the hope of a better future that the plans serve. V — Vanity: the afternoon on which the plans are looked at.

## Elder of Distraction

**Age.** Hartmann's three illusions leave out old age. Schopenhauer remarks that in later years life offers less that is new, and the strength to pursue it declines. Distraction narrows to small things: the crossword, the television, the garden, the post, meals at fixed hours.

**Figure.** An old woman sits in an armchair by a window, a knitting basket on her knee and a wireless on the shelf beside her. A newspaper lies folded on the sill with a crossword half done. She watches a blackbird on the lawn in the lamplight of a late afternoon.

**Meaning.** The card names routine as a quiet form of distraction.

**In a reading.** Attention held by small and regular matters.

**Defence.** Habit is a mercy in old age. The rhythm of meals, post and programmes gives each day a form that takes no strength to make. An old person who lives gently on small impressions is managing well.

**Links.** VI — The Pendulum: the swing that routine slows to a gentle sway. XIV — The Stilled Wheel: the calm that routine can approach without reaching.

## One of Sublimation

**Evasion.** The excess of consciousness is given a use. A draughtsman studies the antlers of an extinct deer, and the surplus that Zapffe saw as a biological error becomes attention, patience and a finished drawing. The overgrowth is accepted as a fact and put to work.

**Image.** A figure at a table in a museum store-room, drawing a vast antler from a skull set on a trestle. The paper on the board is already crowded with fine hatching, and the drawn antler is slightly larger than the real one. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** The first use of a faculty that has outgrown its purpose. The card names the moment when too much mind finds an object worth its weight.

**In a reading.** An excess of awareness has found something to hold. The matter is not resolved, but it has acquired a shape. The card asks what the surplus is currently making.

**Defence.** Zapffe held that a consciousness too large for survival must be damped or it becomes unbearable, and he treated sublimation as the rarest of the four mechanisms and the only one that damps by transforming. Pain that becomes a drawing is neither denied nor buried, and the maker keeps sight of the cause. He also thought the mechanism rare, since few people have the gift or the leisure. Whether it works for the maker or only for the audience he left open.

**Links.** I, The Giant Deer: the same overgrowth, here put to use. XIV, The Stilled Wheel: contemplation of a form, which rests the will for a time.

## Two of Sublimation

**Evasion.** The hope of happiness in this life is given form in a still life or a love poem. The brief pleasure, which the card admits will not last, is painted at the height of its ripeness, so that it can be looked at without being owned. The loss is built into the picture.

**Image.** A painter at an easel before a table set with peaches, a half-peeled lemon and a glass. One peach has begun to soften, and the painter has put it at the centre of the canvas. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Happiness here, kept as a record. The card names the pleasure that is enjoyed and then recorded as a form, with its transience visible.

**In a reading.** Joy is being turned into something that outlasts it. The pleasure is real and so is its end, and the card allows both. It suggests making a record of what is good now.

**Defence.** A person who writes down a happy day does not expect it to return. The record lets the day count without the claim that it was permanent. Zapffe would grant that this costs little and gives much. He also noted how seldom the mechanism is open to people, since it needs a talent or a craft, and he would ask whether the painter is happier or only more occupied.

**Links.** II, The First Illusion: Happiness Here, which the still life holds without believing in it. V, Vanity: the genre of vanitas painting, in which pleasure and its end share a canvas.

## Three of Sublimation

**Evasion.** The hope of happiness hereafter is given form as music. A choir rehearses a mass in an unheated church, and the promise of another world becomes a sound that exists in this one. The afterlife is not believed, but the longing behind it is allowed to shape something.

**Image.** A small choir in a stone nave, singing from a single shared score under one lamp. Above them the vault is dark and empty, and the singers look up at it without seeing anything. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** The longing for a world beyond, made into a work. The card names what remains of religious feeling when the doctrine has gone.

**In a reading.** Longing has been turned into craft. The hope of a hereafter is no longer asked to be true, and it is still allowed to be beautiful. The card points to the work that the longing can produce.

**Defence.** Hartmann argued that the second illusion fails, and Zapffe offered no replacement. Sublimation supplies one that asks for no belief. A hymn sung by someone who doubts it still steadies him, and the tradition that built it kept many people going through hard years. Zapffe would add that this is a gift to a few, and that the rest need a firmer anchor.

**Links.** III, The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter, here emptied of doctrine and kept as music. XIV, The Stilled Wheel: the will quiet for the length of a piece.

## Four of Sublimation

**Evasion.** The illusion of progress is given form as a monument to the work of earlier generations. A restorer repairs a fresco knowing that the wall will outlast her and that nothing will be finished. The truth that the future will not redeem the past is accepted, and the effort is spent on the thing in front of her.

**Image.** A scaffold against a cracked wall in a half-ruined chapel, where a restorer in a smock fills in a missing patch of sky with a fine brush. Beside her a bucket of lime and a ladder. Sections of the fresco beyond her reach are already flaking. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Work done without a promise of improvement. The card names effort that does not depend on history arriving anywhere.

**In a reading.** Work is justified by the task and not by the outcome. The expected progress has not arrived, and the work remains worth doing. The card advises attending to the piece at hand.

**Defence.** The belief that history improves has sustained many labourers, and Hartmann treated its loss as the last disillusion. A craft offers a smaller and sturdier ground: the job is good when done well, whatever follows. Zapffe would remind us that the craftsman still needs to believe the work matters, which is an anchor of a kind, and that sublimation seldom stands alone.

**Links.** IV, The Third Illusion: Progress, replaced by care for a single task. XIII, The Broom: a plant that grows on ruined ground and asks for nothing.

## Five of Sublimation

**Evasion.** The vanity of labour under the sun, as Ecclesiastes states it, is given form in the preacher's own book. The writer sets down that all is breath, and the setting down is a labour that he does not exempt from the verdict. Form is the means by which he looks at the emptiness without being flattened by it.

**Image.** An old man at a plain desk, writing on a long roll of paper that falls over the edge and coils on the floor. A candle burns beside him and the shadow of the roll crosses the wall. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** The statement of vanity as a work. The card names the writing that tells the truth about writing.

**In a reading.** An honest account of futility has itself become a task. The labour is acknowledged as breath, and it is carried out regardless. The card suggests that saying plainly what is the case is a use of the time.

**Defence.** Ecclesiastes has lasted longer than most books of comfort, which suggests that a clear statement of vanity can be consoling to a reader. Zapffe would accept this as an example of sublimation at its best, and he might add that the mechanism cannot be known to work from inside. His essay on the last messiah is itself a piece of writing, and he was aware that it might belong here.

**Links.** V, Vanity: the verdict that the book gives and does not escape. X, The Insomniac: a sleepless writer, who at least has something to do with the hours.

## Six of Sublimation

**Evasion.** The swing between pain and boredom is given form as rhythm. A composer sets a restless period in a dull month to a metre, and the oscillation becomes a pattern with a beginning and an end. Pain and boredom are used as material instead of being fled.

**Image.** A woman at a pianoforte in a bare room, with a metronome beside the score. A pendulum shadow moves across the wall behind her, and the score is covered with corrections. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** The oscillation as a measure. The card names the way that a recurring discomfort can be made into time that has a shape.

**In a reading.** The restlessness has a form, and the form can be worked. Neither the pain nor the boredom has gone, but each now serves a pattern. The card suggests giving the swing a metre.

**Defence.** Schopenhauer wrote that aesthetic contemplation suspends the will for a time, and with it the swing. For an artist the periods of lull and torment are both useful, since one supplies material and the other leisure. Zapffe would observe that the swing resumes when the piece ends, and that the relief is therefore partial and renewable only by more work.

**Links.** VI, The Pendulum: the same swing, kept in time. XIV, The Stilled Wheel: the pause in the will that art affords.

## Seven of Sublimation

**Evasion.** Nature's indifference is given form as landscape. A painter sits before a cold shore and puts down what is there: the grey water, the empty sky, the absence of any sign that either regards him. The indifference becomes a subject, and a subject can be studied with care.

**Image.** A painter on a stony beach with a small folding stool, working at a panel in the wind. In front of him is a sea under a heavy sky, and a single small sail in the distance. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Indifference observed with attention. The card names the discipline of looking at a world that does not look back.

**In a reading.** The indifference of the world has become a subject of study. Attention is repaid with detail, if not with comfort. The card suggests looking at the thing without asking it to answer.

**Defence.** In Leopardi's dialogue Nature answers the Icelander only that the world was not made for him, which leaves him with nothing but his own account. Sublimation lets that account be exact. Zapffe thought art was the one mechanism in which the cost of the knowledge is partly turned back into gain. He would also point out that most people cannot paint a shore, and that the sea is easier to bear for those who look at it for an afternoon than for those who are caught in a storm.

**Links.** VII, Nature and the Icelander: the silence of nature, here recorded faithfully. XIII, The Broom: Leopardi's own poem, which belongs to this kind of work.

## Eight of Sublimation

**Evasion.** The problem of the right distance between people is given form as correspondence. Two friends write long letters over many years, and the page supplies the space that sitting together lacks. Warmth is exchanged without the pricks of proximity.

**Image.** Two desks in two rooms of different towns, each with a lamp and a sheet half written. A road drawn faintly through the window of each connects them. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Closeness at a workable distance. The card names the forms, such as letters, art and books, through which people reach one another without crowding.

**In a reading.** Distance has been made into a medium. The relationship can be warm because it is mediated, and the card suggests that a form may carry what direct contact cannot. It invites writing rather than visiting.

**Defence.** Schopenhauer's porcupines found a tolerable distance by trial and wound. A letter or a poem is a distance chosen in advance, and many friendships have survived because of one. Zapffe would grant its value and add that he expected few to practise it, since it demands skill and time. The reader of a book is also a partner who cannot answer, which is part of its comfort.

**Links.** VIII, The Porcupines: the right distance, found through form. XII, Compassion: warmth that can be given without contact.

## Nine of Sublimation

**Evasion.** Mainländer's account of a God who shattered into the world is given form as a mosaic. A mason sets thousands of small pieces into a floor, and the whole is made of fragments that remain visibly separate. The picture does not restore the unity and does not conceal the breaks.

**Image.** A mason kneeling on a floor, setting small tesserae into a wide circular pattern with a trowel. Most of the circle is complete and a few gaps remain, with a heap of loose stone beside him. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Fragmentation made orderly. The card names the arrangement of broken things into a pattern that is not a repair.

**In a reading.** The pieces have been arranged and not mended. The fragmentation is acknowledged, and a design has been found within it. The card suggests that a coherent whole can be made from parts that were never one.

**Defence.** If everything is a fragment of a lost unity, any arrangement of fragments is a kind of orientation. A mosaic gives the viewer a fixed point without a false claim about the origin. Zapffe would see this as the productive form of anchoring, and would note that the mason must keep his eyes on the stones to be content, and the same can be said of anyone who works.

**Links.** IX, The Shattered God: the fragments of Mainländer's metaphysics, set in order. XIV, The Stilled Wheel: the quiet that follows when the pattern is complete.

## Ten of Sublimation

**Evasion.** Lucidity without rest is given form as the night's work. The insomniac does not sleep, and uses the hours to write. Cioran described a life of sleepless nights and wrote a great deal during them, and the card follows that pattern without claiming it as a cure. The wakefulness yields pages.

**Image.** A narrow room at night with a single window, through which a pale sky shows. A man sits at a desk with a pen, and a neat stack of finished pages stands at his elbow. The bed behind him is made. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Wakefulness used. The card names the transformation of a sleepless mind into a body of work.

**In a reading.** Sleeplessness has become productive. The mind is not at rest, and its restlessness now has an outlet. The card suggests that a long night can be given a task.

**Defence.** Cioran's lucidity could be a torment, and the pages he wrote were a way of living with it. Zapffe regarded this as the most dignified response he knew, and the least common. He doubted it could be recommended, because the gift is not given to all, and because writing about wakefulness may deepen it. He allowed that the result might be worth the price.

**Links.** X, The Insomniac: the same lucidity, spent on work. XI, The Stone: a labour repeated each day without hope of an end.

## Child of Sublimation

**Age.** Childhood in Schopenhauer's account is a time when the world is taken in through the senses and has the freshness of the new, and when play is the chief activity. Sublimation here is play: a drawing, a song, a story told to a toy. Hartmann tied the first illusion to childhood, and the child's art is made inside it, with no thought of consolation.

**Figure.** A child sitting on a floor, drawing on a large sheet with a stub of chalk. The drawing shows a house, a sun and a very large animal with antlers. Nobody is watching. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Making before there is anything to make against. The card names the play from which later art grows.

**In a reading.** Making is still free of purpose. The activity is its own reward, and nothing has yet been asked of it. The card points to what the maker did before the work had to justify itself.

**Defence.** A child's drawing protects nothing in particular, since the child has not yet met the thing it might protect against. That is its value: the habit of making is acquired early and is available later, when the need arises. Zapffe saw sublimation as rare, and the capacity may be best preserved by those who are allowed to play. Children also stop drawing at a certain age, as most adults notice.

**Links.** II, The First Illusion: Happiness Here, in its earliest and least troubled form. I, The Giant Deer: a mind with room to grow, using it for pictures.

## Youth of Sublimation

**Age.** Schopenhauer described youth as a period of ardent expectation, and Hartmann tied the second illusion to it. In this suit the feeling of youth, its yearning and its dissatisfaction, is written into verse, music or a first novel. The excess of feeling finds a form because it has nowhere else to go.

**Figure.** A young person in an attic room, writing by lamplight, with loose pages on the floor and a violin on the bed. The window is open, and a town lies below it. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Longing made into work. The card names the apprentice years, in which feeling is large and technique is small.

**In a reading.** Strong feeling is looking for a form. The results may be imperfect, and the effort is the point. The card suggests that the intensity of the present is raw material.

**Defence.** Much of the world's art was written by the young, and for many the habit of writing carried them past years that might have gone worse. The mechanism protects them from their own intensity. Zapffe's reservation applies: the great majority of the young who write poems do not become poets, and some come to rely on the dream of fame, which is a different anchor. Still, the poem is written.

**Links.** III, The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter, the yearning for something beyond, given an earthly outlet. VI, The Pendulum: the swings of youthful mood, set to rhythm.

## Adult of Sublimation

**Age.** Schopenhauer's middle years bring responsibility, repetition and a measured view of what is possible. Hartmann tied the third illusion, progress, to adulthood. Sublimation in this age is craft: a trade, a body of work built up in the evenings, a practice kept up beside other duties. The pain of adult life is turned into steady output.

**Figure.** A middle-aged woman at a workbench after the day's labour, carving a small figure from wood. A coat hangs on a nail, supper dishes stand on a shelf, and shavings lie on the floor. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** Craft sustained beside duty. The card names the art that grows in the margin of an ordinary life.

**In a reading.** A regular practice is giving structure to a difficult period. It is modest and it holds. The card suggests protecting the hour set aside for it.

**Defence.** For an adult with obligations, the sustained practice is one of the few forms of sublimation available, and it is cheaper than a grand project. Zapffe rated the mechanism rare, and Schopenhauer thought that most people live without such an interest. Work done in the evening may be of modest worth to others and of great worth to the person doing it, which is enough for the card.

**Links.** IV, The Third Illusion: Progress, replaced by the slow accumulation of work. V, Vanity: labour under the sun, here with a visible product.

## Elder of Sublimation

**Age.** Schopenhauer saw old age as a time of calm, in which the passions have lessened and the mind turns more freely to what is lasting. Hartmann's three illusions leave out this age. Sublimation here is the late work: a memoir, a final cycle of drawings, a garden laid out for others. The long view is put into a form for those who come later.

**Figure.** An old man sitting in a garden, with a sketchbook on his knee. A young apprentice stands at his shoulder, holding a roll of paper, and the old man is pointing to a place on the page. No text appears in the image.

**Meaning.** The late work and its handing on. The card names the form given to a lifetime's understanding.

**In a reading.** A long experience has reached the stage of being passed on. The pace is unhurried and the form is settled. The card suggests that what has been learned is ready to be made into something another person can use.

**Defence.** Late work is often the plainest, and it is made when ambition has fallen away and the maker has little to defend. It protects the elder from the sense that a life has left nothing behind. Zapffe doubted that humanity as a whole would use its gift well, and he would see the elder as an exception. He would also ask who inherits the work, and whether it is read.

**Links.** XIV, The Stilled Wheel: the quiet of age, spent on contemplation. XIII, The Broom: a gift made for others on poor ground.
