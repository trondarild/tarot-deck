# Zen Buddhism deck: research and proposed structure

This note covers the first item in `todo.md`. It sets out the Zen structures a deck could draw on, then proposes a card list for the major series and a suit structure. The symbolism and illustration files will build on the card list in the proposal section, so that list is the part to settle first.

Source gathering was delegated to a research agent; its references are listed at the end. Points the sources disagree on, or that could not be confirmed, are flagged in the text.

## Design premise

The European major arcana are a sequence of allegorical figures with no single source text. Zen has several sequences of its own, each with a fixed number of stages and a known author. A Zen deck can therefore take its card count from one or two of these texts instead of fitting Zen material onto 22 trumps. The number of major cards follows from that choice.

## Candidate structures

### Ten Ox-Herding Pictures (十牛圖, jūgyūzu)

Kuoan Shiyuan (廓庵師遠, Jp. Kakuan), a Linji master of 12th-century Song China, wrote the ten-stage version usually reproduced in the West. The paintings commonly shown are attributed to Tenshō Shūbun (1414–1463) at Shōkoku-ji, Kyoto. The ox stands for the practitioner's true nature, or mind; the herdsman is the practitioner.

| # | Chinese | Gloss | Standard reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 尋牛 | Searching for the ox | Longing and seeking |
| 2 | 見跡 | Seeing the traces | First signs, through scripture and teaching |
| 3 | 見牛 | Seeing the ox | First glimpse |
| 4 | 得牛 | Catching the ox | Struggle to hold the experience |
| 5 | 牧牛 | Herding the ox | Disciplined practice |
| 6 | 騎牛歸家 | Riding the ox home | Ease; harmony with the mind |
| 7 | 忘牛存人 | Ox forgotten, self remains | The means is dropped |
| 8 | 人牛俱忘 | Ox and self both forgotten | Empty circle; subject and object fall away |
| 9 | 返本還源 | Returning to the source | The world seen as it is |
| 10 | 入鄽垂手 | Entering the market with open hands | Return to ordinary life to help others; the figure is Budai |

The earlier Puming (普明) version also has ten pictures, but its ox whitens stage by stage and the series ends at the empty circle. Kuoan's series continues past emptiness back into the world. That return is the main reason to prefer Kuoan for a deck meant to be read: it gives a cycle in which the last card leads back to the first.

Other versions have four, five, six, eight and twelve pictures. Their authorship is disputed (Qingju, Qingju Haosheng and Foguo Weibai are each named for the five- and eight-picture sets).

### Dongshan's Five Ranks (五位, go-i)

Attributed to Dongshan Liangjie (洞山良价, Jp. Tōzan, 807–869), founder of the Caodong (Sōtō) line; modern scholars date part of the associated text to the 12th century. The ranks describe the relation between the absolute (正, "lord") and the relative (偏, "vassal"):

1. 正中偏 — the relative within the absolute
2. 偏中正 — the absolute within the relative
3. 正中來 — coming from within the absolute
4. 兼中至 — arriving at mutual integration (some texts have 偏中至)
5. 兼中到 — unity attained

English renderings of ranks 1 and 2 are sometimes swapped; the Chinese order above follows Dumoulin, Cleary and Wikipedia. A distinct set, the Five Ranks of Merit (向, 奉, 功, 共功, 功功: orientation, service, merit, merit in common, merit upon merit), measures depth of realisation.

Hakuin, in the Rinzai line, commented on the Five Ranks, and his school studies them late in koan training. That gives a historical basis for combining the Five Ranks with the Rinzai ox pictures in one deck.

### Linji's fourfold sets

The *Linji lu* contains several fourfold devices: the Four Shouts; the four selections of person and circumstance (奪人不奪境 and its permutations); the four guest–host relations; and the Three Mysteries and Three Essentials. Their names are applied inconsistently across translations, and the Three Essentials could not be defined from a verified source. They suit individual cards or a spread layout better than they suit a backbone.

### Koan collections

The Gateless Gate (無門關, Wumen Huikai, 1228; 48 cases) and the Blue Cliff Record (碧巖錄, Xuedou and Yuanwu, compiled 1125; 100 cases) supply named scenes with a fixed text. Cases that recur in teaching and have a clear image:

- Mumonkan 1, Zhaozhou's Mu
- Mumonkan 2, Baizhang's fox
- Mumonkan 6, the Buddha holds up a flower
- Mumonkan 7, Zhaozhou's "wash your bowl"
- Mumonkan 14 / Blue Cliff 63–64, Nanquan cuts the cat
- Mumonkan 19, ordinary mind is the way
- Mumonkan 23, the original face (Huineng)
- Mumonkan 29, not the wind, not the flag
- Mumonkan 41, Bodhidharma pacifies the mind
- Mumonkan 46, stepping forward from the top of the pole
- Blue Cliff 1, the Emperor questions Bodhidharma
- Blue Cliff 6, every day is a good day

### Figures and symbols

Bodhidharma (Daruma), Huineng and Shenxiu's verse contest in the *Platform Sutra*, Budai (Hotei), Hanshan and Shide, Kannon, Manjushri, the ensō, the begging bowl, the zafu and the keisaku. Several of these already appear inside the ox pictures and the koans, which reduces the need for separate cards.

### Fivefold and fourfold groupings for suits

- **Godai (五大)**, the Japanese Buddhist five elements: earth 地, water 水, fire 火, wind 風, void 空. Each has a fixed shape in the gorintō grave marker: cube, sphere, pyramid, half-moon, jewel. The set derives from the Indian *mahābhūta* through esoteric Japanese Buddhism. It differs from the Chinese wuxing (wood, fire, earth, metal, water).
- The Japanese "way" arts (chadō, shodō, kadō, kyūdō) are a recognised cluster, but no canonical list of four or five was found.
- The Four Arts of the Chinese scholar (qin, qi, shu, hua) are a fixed set, but they are Chinese literati culture rather than Zen.

## Existing decks

The Osho Zen Tarot (1994–95, 79 cards) renames the 22 trumps, adds a card for Osho, and replaces the suits with Fire, Water, Clouds and Rainbows. Its structure is the Rider-Waite structure with new titles. Several Zen-themed oracle decks exist (36–50 cards each, built on words, animals or garden elements). The search found no deck built on the ox pictures, the Five Ranks or a koan sequence. The search was shallow, so this should be checked on Aeclectic Tarot before the deck is described as original.

## Proposal

### Major series: 18 cards in two strands

The major series has two strands. The ox pictures follow one practitioner's path. The transmission cards follow how the teaching passes between people, using the lineage legends.

**0 — Mu (無).** Unnumbered, like the Fool: Zhaozhou's answer and the gateless gate itself, before any path begins (Mumonkan 1).

**The Way (I–X): Kuoan's ten ox pictures**, in Kuoan's order and titles as in the table above. Card X leads back to I, so the strand reads as a cycle.

**The Transmission (XI–XVII): seven lineage scenes**, in traditional chronological order:

| # | Card | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| XI | The Flower and the Smile | Mumonkan 6; Mahākāśyapa |
| XII | Facing the Wall | Bodhidharma's nine years; Blue Cliff 1 |
| XIII | Pacifying the Mind | Mumonkan 41; Huike |
| XIV | No Mirror, No Stand | *Platform Sutra* verse contest; Huineng |
| XV | Ordinary Mind | Mumonkan 19; Nanquan and Zhaozhou |
| XVI | The Wild Fox | Mumonkan 2; Baizhang, on cause and effect |
| XVII | The Shout | *Linji lu*, the Four Shouts |

The legends behind XI and XII are not historical, by scholarly consensus: the Flower Sermon first appears in 1036, and the four-line verse attributed to Bodhidharma is no earlier than 1108. The cards present them as lineage legends, which is how the tradition itself uses them.

### Minor series: five suits by godai, five ranks as court cards

- **Suits:** Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, Void. Each suit is marked by its gorintō shape.
- **Pip cards:** 1–10 per suit.
- **Rank cards:** five per suit, named for Dongshan's Five Ranks. These take the place of the court cards. The ranks' own "lord and vassal" metaphor fits that role, though using the ranks as court cards is a design decision rather than traditional usage.

That gives 5 × 15 = 75 minor cards, and 93 cards in all. A smaller variant would drop the pips and keep 25 rank cards, for 43 cards in all.

### Minor series: how it supports the major cards (proposal, 2026-10-10)

- **Pips as the Way in each element:** Pip n of every suit echoes major card n, Kuoan's ox-herding stage n, played out in that element's domain. Three of Water is seeing the ox in the stream of feeling; Seven of Earth is putting down the rope in daily work. In a reading, a minor card points back to the major card with its number.
- **Element domains:** Drawn from the traditional sense of the godai. Earth is stability, the body and zazen; Water is feeling, adaptation and the tea way; Fire is effort, doubt and koan work; Wind is breath, movement and kinhin; Void is awareness itself and shikantaza.
- **Court cards as ranks of maturity:** The Five Ranks describe how the absolute and the relative stand to each other. As courts they describe a person in one element at one degree of maturity: the pips are stages, and the courts are the people who have reached them. This departs from the social rank of the tarot court. Links to the Transmission cards (XI–XVII) are loose, since seven teachers do not map onto five ranks.
- **Void as a special suit:** Void could keep pips, or have court cards only, echoing card 0 (Mu) and the empty circle of card VIII. Courts only would reduce the minor series from 75 to 65 cards.
- **Cards without a pip counterpart:** Card 0 and the Transmission cards stand outside the numbered run. They are teachers and teachings, while the minor cards are situations.

### Decisions (2026-10-10)

1. The Transmission strand stays: 18 major cards.
2. The Five Ranks are the court cards of the minor series.
3. Symbolism and illustration work covers the major cards only for now; the minor series is deferred.

### Repository notes

- A deck entry would live at `data/decks/zen/`, alongside the existing three decks.
