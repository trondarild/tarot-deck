# Rider-Waite-Smith tarot: symbolism of the cards

The Rider-Waite-Smith deck was designed by Arthur Edward Waite and drawn by Pamela Colman Smith, and published by William Rider & Son in 1909. Waite explained it in *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1911), which is the main source for the entries below. Part II of the *Key* describes the pictures of the major arcana, and Part III describes the minor arcana and gives the divinatory meanings, upright and reversed, for all 78 cards. The book is in the public domain, and short quotations from it are marked "(Waite)".

Waite wrote a good deal about the major arcana and little about most of the minor cards. Many details of Smith's minor-card drawings, such as the ships on the Two of Pentacles or the objects in the cups of the Seven of Cups, are not explained in the *Key*. The entries say where a detail is Smith's and Waite gives no meaning for it, and they label interpretations that came later as later readings.

Major arcana entries give the image, its symbols, Waite's upright and reversed meanings, the Hebrew letter and astrological sign that the deck's metadata assigns to the card, a reading and links to other cards. The Hebrew and astrological attributions are those of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, to which Waite belonged; he did not print them on the cards. Minor arcana entries give the image, symbols, the two meanings and a reading.

The text of the *Key* used here is the copy in `archive/PictorialKey.xml`, with a few corrupted words corrected, and the Wikisource transcription for the divinatory meanings of the major arcana. Picture details were checked against the card images in `assets/img/big/`.

## 0 — The Fool

**Image.** A young man in "gorgeous vestments" pauses at the brink of a precipice among great heights, looking out at the blue distance, "its expanse of sky rather than the prospect below" (Waite). His dog bounds at his heels. In one hand he holds a white rose, in the other a wand from which an embroidered wallet hangs over his right shoulder. The sun shines behind him.

**Symbols.** Waite calls the Fool "a prince of the other world on his travels through this one" and "the spirit in search of experience". The sun behind him knows "whence he came, whither he is going, and how he will return by another path after many days". The unconcern at the edge suggests that angels would uphold him if he leaped. Waite says the card summarises many symbols of the Instituted Mysteries and "reverses, under high warrants, all the confusions that have preceded it". He records the older reading of the Fool as the flesh and the sensitive life. Later readings take the white rose as purity and the dog as instinct or a faithful companion; Waite does not say so.

**Meaning.** Folly, mania, extravagance, intoxication, delirium, frenzy, bewrayment (Waite). He adds that the card may indicate the whole range of mental phases between excitement and madness.

**Reversed.** Negligence, absence, distribution, carelessness, apathy, nullity, vanity.

**Correspondences.** In the Golden Dawn system the Fool carries the letter aleph and the element air. Waite did not print them on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names a beginning taken without knowledge of the ground ahead. It can describe a step made out of trust, inexperience or recklessness, and the reading usually depends on which of these the surrounding cards support.

**Links.** XXI, The World, which the Fool precedes as the end of the same sequence and which Waite treats as the final message of the Major Trumps. I, The Magician, who shares the wand and the flowers but possesses what the Fool has yet to acquire. XVII, The Star, another figure in open country, shown with the sky above.

## I — The Magician

**Image.** A youthful figure in a magician's robe, "having the countenance of divine Apollo, with smile of confidence and shining eyes" (Waite). Above his head floats the horizontal figure 8, and about his waist is a serpent that devours its own tail. His right hand raises a wand towards heaven while the left points to the earth. On the table before him lie the emblems of the four suits. Roses and lilies grow below.

**Symbols.** The horizontal 8 is for Waite the sign of life and of the Holy Spirit; he recalls that Christian Gnosticism speaks of rebirth in Christ as a change "unto the Ogdoad", and that Martinism makes 8 the number of Christ. The serpent signifies "the eternity of attainment in the spirit". The raised wand and lowered hand form a dual sign of the descent of grace, virtue and light "drawn from things above and derived to things below". The suit emblems stand for the elements of natural life, which "lie like counters before the adept". The roses and lilies are the flower of the field and lily of the valley "changed into garden flowers, to shew the culture of aspiration".

**Meaning.** Skill, diplomacy, address, subtlety; sickness, pain, loss, disaster, snares of enemies; self-confidence, will; the Querent, if male.

**Reversed.** Physician, Magus, mental disease, disgrace, disquiet.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn assigns beth and the planet Mercury. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names a person with the means to hand and the will to use them. It points to skill, initiative and the capacity to turn intention into act. Waite's divinatory list shows that the same skill may serve deceit.

**Links.** II, The High Priestess, who holds the hidden counterpart to his open action. VIII, Strength, which carries the same sign of life above the head. XV, The Devil, whose Mercury sign marks a distorted use of will.

## II — The High Priestess

**Image.** A woman sits between the white and black pillars marked J and B of the mystic Temple, with a veil behind her embroidered with palms and pomegranates. A lunar crescent lies at her feet; on her head is a horned diadem with a globe, and on her breast a solar cross. She holds a scroll inscribed *Tora*, partly covered by her mantle.

**Symbols.** The scroll signifies "the Greater Law, the Secret Law and the second sense of the Word"; its being half hidden shows "that some things are implied and some spoken" (Waite). The veil is the veil of the Temple. Waite rejects the title of occult science on the threshold of the Sanctuary of Isis and calls her "the Secret Church, the House which is of God and man", the spiritual Bride and Mother, and the Moon "nourished by the milk of the Supernal Mother". In the Kabbalah her truest name is Shekinah, the indwelling glory, which Waite identifies with Binah above and Malkuth below. He calls the card in some respects "the highest and holiest of the Greater Arcana". She makes no sign, unlike the Hierophant, because her power is withdrawn.

**Meaning.** Secrets, mystery, the future as yet unrevealed; the woman who interests the Querent, if male; the Querent herself, if female; silence, tenacity; mystery, wisdom, science.

**Reversed.** Passion, moral or physical ardour, conceit, surface knowledge.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes gimel and the Moon. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names what is known but not yet said, or what remains to be learned. It counsels waiting and attention to what is implied. It can also describe a person who keeps their own counsel.

**Links.** V, The Hierophant, her counterpart as the public voice of doctrine. I, The Magician, who acts where she holds back. XI, Justice, also seated between pillars, though Waite says those open "into one world" and hers into another.

## III — The Empress

**Image.** A stately woman sits in rich vestments, with "royal aspect, as of a daughter of heaven and earth" (Waite). Her diadem holds twelve stars gathered in a cluster. Beside her rests a shield bearing the symbol of Venus. A field of corn ripens in front of her and beyond it is a fall of water. Her sceptre is topped by the globe of the world.

**Symbols.** Waite calls her "the inferior Garden of Eden, the Earthly Paradise, all that is symbolized by the visible house of man", and "the fruitful mother of thousands". She is "universal fecundity and the outer sense of the Word", as the High Priestess is the inner sense. He allows that she has been described as desire and the wings thereof and as the woman clothed with the sun. In another order of ideas she is the door by which entrance is gained to this life, and the way out of it is the secret known to the High Priestess. The Venus shield and the ripening grain are the plain signs of love and harvest. Waite dismisses most older attributions of the card as wrong on the symbolism.

**Meaning.** Fruitfulness, action, initiative, length of days; the unknown, clandestine; also difficulty, doubt, ignorance.

**Reversed.** Light, truth, the unravelling of involved matters, public rejoicings; according to another reading, vacillation.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes daleth and the planet Venus.

**In a reading.** The card names abundance, growth and care for what has been planted. It describes a generative phase in work, family or the body, and a source that supplies others.

**Links.** IV, The Emperor, the male power to which she responds. II, The High Priestess, whose inward knowledge complements her outward fruitfulness. XIX, The Sun, another card of natural plenty.

## IV — The Emperor

**Image.** A crowned monarch, "commanding, stately", sits on a throne whose arms are fronted by rams' heads. He holds a sceptre in the form of the *crux ansata* and a globe in his left hand (Waite).

**Symbols.** Waite describes him as "executive and realization, the power of this world, here clothed with the highest of its natural attributes". He is the virile power to which the Empress responds, and he is "he who seeks to remove the Veil of Isis", yet she "remains virgo intacta". The card and that of the Empress do not exactly represent married life, though it is implied; on the surface they stand for royalty, and above this for "the higher kingship, occupying the intellectual throne", the lordship of thought rather than of the animal world. Waite adds that the pair are "full of strange experience" but without the wisdom drawn from a higher world. He sets aside two readings of the Emperor, as will in embodied form and as an expression of the Absolute Being, calling the second fantasy. The rams are the animal of Aries, which later authors assign to the card.

**Meaning.** Stability, power, protection, realization; a great person; aid, reason, conviction; also authority and will.

**Reversed.** Benevolence, compassion, credit; also confusion to enemies, obstruction, immaturity.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes heh and the sign Aries, in the arrangement that exchanges the Emperor and the Star. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names order, structure and the exercise of authority. It points to a person or institution that provides stability, or to the need for the same in the querent's own affairs.

**Links.** III, The Empress, his counterpart. V, The Hierophant, the authority of doctrine beside his authority of rule. VII, The Chariot, a second figure of conquest and command.

## V — The Hierophant

**Image.** A figure in the triple crown sits between two pillars, which Waite says are not those of the Temple guarded by the High Priestess. His left hand holds a sceptre ending in the triple cross; his right makes the "sign of esotericism", dividing the manifest from the concealed part of doctrine. At his feet lie the crossed keys, and two priestly ministers in albs kneel before him.

**Symbols.** The card is usually called the Pope, which Waite regards as a particular application of a more general office. The Hierophant is "the ruling power of external religion, as the High Priestess is the prevailing genius of the esoteric, withdrawn power". He is the power of the keys and "exoteric orthodox doctrine", or "the *summa totius theologiæ*, when it has passed into the utmost rigidity of expression". He is also the channel of grace belonging to the world of institution, as distinct from that of Nature, and the head of a hierarchy that reflects a greater one. Waite warns that the pontiff may forget the significance of his symbolic state. The Hierophant is not philosophy, inspiration or religion itself, though he is a mode of its expression.

**Meaning.** Marriage, alliance, captivity, servitude; by another account, mercy and goodness; inspiration; the man to whom the Querent has recourse.

**Reversed.** Society, good understanding, concord, over-kindness, weakness.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes vav and the sign Taurus. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names conventions, institutions and the guidance of an established tradition. It may describe a teacher or adviser, a formal commitment, or the pressure to conform.

**Links.** II, The High Priestess, whose hidden doctrine answers his public one. IV, The Emperor, secular rule beside his sacred rule. VI, The Lovers, which Waite says replaced the older card of marriage.

## VI — The Lovers

**Image.** The sun shines in the zenith and below it a great winged figure with arms extended pours down influences. In the foreground stand a man and a woman, "unveiled before each other, as if Adam and Eve when they first occupied the paradise of the earthly body" (Waite). Behind the man is the Tree of Life bearing twelve fruits; behind the woman is the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil, with the serpent twining round it.

**Symbols.** Waite says the figures suggest "youth, virginity, innocence and love before it is contaminated by gross material desire", and calls this "the card of human love, here exhibited as part of the way, the truth and the life". It replaces the old card of marriage and the later designs that showed a man between vice and virtue. The woman signifies attraction to the sensitive life, which carries the idea of the Fall, but she is "the working of a Secret Law of Providence" rather than a willing temptress; through her lapse man arises, and by her alone can he complete himself. Waite names the winged figure only as an influence from above. Later readers have called it Raphael.

**Meaning.** Attraction, love, beauty, trials overcome.

**Reversed.** Failure, foolish designs. Another account speaks of marriage frustrated and contrarieties of all kinds.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes zayin and the sign Gemini. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names a union or a choice that engages both feeling and value. It may refer to a relationship, or to a decision in which two ways of living are weighed.

**Links.** V, The Hierophant, whose sanction of marriage it replaces. XV, The Devil, which Waite sets as a parallel of the same pair in bondage. XIV, Temperance, another card of an angel and a union of opposites.

## VII — The Chariot

**Image.** An erect and princely figure carries a drawn sword in a chariot (Waite). On the shoulders of the victor are supposed to be the Urim and Thummim. Two sphinxes draw the chariot, following the variation of Éliphas Lévi.

**Symbols.** Waite describes the figure as "conquest on all planes — in the mind, in science, in progress, in certain trials of initiation", and "above all things triumph in the mind". Having answered the sphinx, he has led captivity captive. Waite then qualifies the triumph: the sphinx's question concerned a mystery of Nature and not of the world of Grace; the conquest is of manifest, external planes and not of himself; his liberation may leave him in "the bondage of the logical understanding"; and if he came to the pillars of the Temple he could not open the scroll called *Tora* or answer the High Priestess. He is neither hereditary royalty nor priesthood. The later detail of a starred canopy and black and white sphinxes appears on Smith's card; Waite assigns no meaning to them beyond the general account above.

**Meaning.** Succour, providence; also war, triumph, presumption, vengeance, trouble.

**Reversed.** Riot, quarrel, dispute, litigation, defeat.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes cheth and the sign Cancer. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names a victory won by direction and effort, and the energy needed to hold opposed forces on one course. It also warns that a success of this kind has limits.

**Links.** II, The High Priestess, before whom Waite says he would have no answer. IV, The Emperor, another seated or crowned holder of worldly power. VIII, Strength, which achieves mastery through gentleness.

## VIII — Strength

**Image.** A woman, over whose head broods the sign of life seen in the Magician, is closing the jaws of a lion. Waite notes that her "beneficent fortitude has already subdued the lion, which is being led by a chain of flowers".

**Symbols.** Waite exchanged this card with Justice, which is usually numbered eight, for reasons he says "satisfy myself" and does not explain. He describes the virtue as connected with "the Divine Mystery of Union", with *innocentia inviolata*, and with "the strength which resides in contemplation". He adds that these higher meanings are matters of inference and not transparent on the surface. The chain of flowers signifies among other things "the sweet yoke and the light burden of Divine Law, when it has been taken into the heart of hearts". The card has nothing to do with ordinary self-confidence; it concerns those whose strength is God. The lion signifies the passions and the woman "the higher nature in its liberation". Waite cites the verse on treading the asp, the basilisk, the lion and the dragon.

**Meaning.** Power, energy, action, courage, magnanimity; also complete success and honours. Waite's list calls the card Fortitude.

**Reversed.** Despotism, abuse of power, weakness, discord, sometimes even disgrace.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes teth and the sign Leo. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names composure that governs impulse without breaking it. It describes courage of a quiet kind, patience with a difficult force, and the ability to persuade where compulsion would fail.

**Links.** I, The Magician, who bears the same sign of life above his head. XI, Justice, the card with which it was exchanged. XV, The Devil, in which the animal nature rules instead of being led.

## IX — The Hermit

**Image.** An old figure in a mantle holds a lantern and a staff, standing on an eminence and holding up his beacon. Waite says the variation from conventional designs is "only that the lamp is not enveloped partially in the mantle of its bearer". A star shines in the lantern.

**Symbols.** Waite calls the card one of attainment, and the figure blends "the idea of the Ancient of Days with the Light of the World". The raised beacon intimates "where I am, you also may be". He rejects Court de Gebelin's reading of the Hermit as a wise man in search of truth and justice, and the later view of him as an example of experience. He also rejects the claim of Éliphas Lévi and the Martinists that the mantle protects personal magnetism through occult isolation, calling it a frivolous rendering. In true Martinism the *Philosophe inconnu* refers to the truth that "the Divine Mysteries secure their own protection from those who are unprepared". The snowy height and grey cloak on Smith's card are in the picture; Waite does not interpret them separately.

**Meaning.** Prudence, circumspection; also and especially treason, dissimulation, roguery, corruption.

**Reversed.** Concealment, disguise, policy, fear, unreasoned caution.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes yod and the sign Virgo. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** Waite's divinatory list gives caution and, less happily, concealment. In modern use the card more often names a withdrawal for reflection and the guidance of someone further along the road.

**Links.** II, The High Priestess, another figure of hidden knowledge. XVII, The Star, a second source of light in the dark. X, Wheel of Fortune, the next card, which places that stillness against the turning of fate.

## X — Wheel of Fortune

**Image.** A wheel turns with the transliteration of Taro as *Rota* inscribed on it, counterchanged with the letters of the Divine Name. Waite says he presents Typhon in his serpent form, and a Sphinx is the equilibrium of the whole. The four Living Creatures of Ezekiel occupy the angles of the card.

**Symbols.** Following Éliphas Lévi, the wheel stands "for the perpetual motion of a fluidic universe and for the flux of human life" (Waite). The Sphinx represents "stability amidst movement", which Waite says is lost in those designs that show it couchant on a pedestal above the wheel. The letters show "that Providence is implied through all", and the Living Creatures give the similar intention without. The serpent is Typhon, in Egyptian terms. Behind the general notion lies "the denial of chance and the fatality which is implied therein". Waite adds that from Lévi onward the occult explanations of the card are "of a singularly fatuous kind" and that common fortune-telling does better on its own plane. The jackal-headed figure rising and the winged creatures with books are visible on Smith's card; Waite does not itemise them.

**Meaning.** Destiny, fortune, success, elevation, luck, felicity.

**Reversed.** Increase, abundance, superfluity.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes kaph and the planet Jupiter. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names a turn of circumstance outside the querent's control, and the cycles in which good and bad periods follow one another. It encourages attention to what remains steady while events move.

**Links.** XI, Justice, the moral order beside the apparent randomness of fortune. XXI, The World, which also carries the four creatures at its corners. XVI, The Tower, a more violent reversal.

## XI — Justice

**Image.** A figure is seated between pillars like the High Priestess (Waite). Waite adds little to the traditional description, which he gave in the first part of the book. Smith's drawing shows a crowned figure holding a sword upright and scales, with a veil between the pillars.

**Symbols.** Waite says the card follows traditional symbolism and has little to add. He remarks that, because the figure sits between pillars, he must distinguish "the moral principle which deals unto every man according to his works" from "the spiritual justice which is involved in the idea of election". The first is analogous to higher things; the second belongs to "a mysterious order of Providence" which operates "like the breathing of the Spirit where it wills", as the gifts of the poet are present or absent. The law of Justice is not involved by either alternative. In conclusion, "the pillars of Justice open into one world and the pillars of the High Priestess into another". The sword and scales are the conventional signs of judgement and of weighing, taken over from earlier decks. He notes that the card is numbered eleven in his arrangement, having been interchanged with Strength.

**Meaning.** Equity, rightness, probity, executive; triumph of the deserving side in law.

**Reversed.** Law in all its departments, legal complications, bigotry, bias, excessive severity.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes lamed and the sign Libra. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names consequence and fair dealing. It refers to a decision that must be weighed honestly, to a legal or formal matter, or to a result that follows from earlier acts.

**Links.** II, The High Priestess, the other figure between pillars. VIII, Strength, with which it was interchanged. XX, Judgement, a later reckoning of the whole.

## XII — The Hanged Man

**Image.** A man hangs from a gallows that forms a Tau cross, and the position of his legs forms a fylfot cross. A nimbus surrounds his head. Waite notes that the tree of sacrifice is "living wood, with leaves thereon", that the face "expresses deep entrancement, not suffering", and that the figure suggests "life in suspension, but life and not death".

**Symbols.** Waite writes that this is "a card of profound significance, but all the significance is veiled". He considers it falsely called a card of martyrdom, prudence, the Great Work or duty, and says one may exhaust the published interpretations and find only vanity. He will say "very simply on my own part that it expresses the relation, in one of its aspects, between the Divine and the Universe". Whoever understands that the story of his higher nature is imbedded in the symbolism "will receive intimations concerning a great awakening that is possible", and will know that "after the sacred Mystery of Death there is a glorious Mystery of Resurrection". The Tau and fylfot are the two crosses he names. Interpreters since have connected the inverted pose with Odin and the hanging of a god, but Waite does not.

**Meaning.** Wisdom, circumspection, discernment, trials, sacrifice, intuition, divination, prophecy.

**Reversed.** Selfishness, the crowd, body politic.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes mem and the element water. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names a suspension voluntarily accepted, in which the usual view is inverted and progress waits. It often marks a period of surrender or of seeing a situation from another side.

**Links.** XIII, Death, the next stage of transformation. XX, Judgement, which Waite compares to a resurrection. XVIII, The Moon, another card of entrancement and an unknown light.

## XIII — Death

**Image.** A mysterious horseman moves slowly, bearing a black banner emblazoned with the Mystic Rose, which signifies life. Between two pillars on the verge of the horizon shines the sun of immortality. The horseman carries no visible weapon, but "king and child and maiden fall before him, while a prelate with clasped hands awaits his end" (Waite).

**Symbols.** Waite says the veil or mask of life "is perpetuated in change, transformation and passage from lower to higher", and that this is better represented "by one of the apocalyptic visions than by the crude notion of the reaping skeleton". Behind it lies the whole world of ascent in the spirit. Ordinary death he regards as one form of progress, but the card chiefly concerns the unusual entrance, while still in this life, into "the state of mystical death", which is a change in the form of consciousness. He finds the existing occult explanations of the thirteenth card better than usual: rebirth, creation, renewal. Smith shows the rider as an armoured skeleton on a white horse; Waite prints the sun and pillars, the banner and the falling figures, and treats the rest as the conventional image he is replacing.

**Meaning.** End, mortality, destruction, corruption; also, for a man, the loss of a benefactor; for a woman, many contrarieties; for a maid, failure of marriage projects.

**Reversed.** Inertia, sleep, lethargy, petrifaction, somnambulism; hope destroyed.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes nun and the sign Scorpio. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names the ending of one condition and the passage to another. Waite treats it as transformation, and modern readers rarely take it as a prediction of bodily death.

**Links.** XII, The Hanged Man, the preceding suspension. XX, Judgement, the resurrection the sun hints at. XVI, The Tower, a sudden ending of another kind.

## XIV — Temperance

**Image.** A winged angel has the sign of the sun upon the forehead and on the breast the square and triangle of the septenary. Waite writes of the figure in the masculine sense, but says it "is neither male nor female". It pours the essences of life from chalice to chalice, with one foot on earth and one on waters. A direct path goes up to heights on the verge of the horizon, and above is a great light through which a crown is seen vaguely.

**Symbols.** The card portrays "some part of the Secret of Eternal Life, as it is possible to man in his incarnation". Waite renounces "all the conventional emblems" and the conventional meanings, which refer to changes of season, the perpetual movement of life and the combination of ideas. It is untrue to say the figure symbolises the genius of the sun, though it is "the analogy of solar light, realized in the third part of our human triplicity". It is called Temperance "fantastically", because when its rule obtains in our consciousness "it tempers, combines and harmonises the psychic and material natures". Under that rule a person knows in the rational part something of whence he came and whither he is going. The irises and the single path are Smith's details; Waite does not interpret them.

**Meaning.** Economy, moderation, frugality, management, accommodation.

**Reversed.** Things connected with churches, religions, sects, the priesthood, sometimes even the priest who will marry the Querent; also disunion, unfortunate combinations, competing interests.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes samekh and the sign Sagittarius. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names the blending of unlike elements into a workable whole, and patience in the process. It suggests measure, adjustment and a steady course.

**Links.** XX, Judgement, which Waite compares to it as a card of eternal life. VI, The Lovers, another card with an angel and a union. XV, The Devil, the following card of bondage.

## XV — The Devil

**Image.** The Horned Goat of Mendes, with wings like a bat, stands on an altar. At the pit of the stomach is the sign of Mercury; a reversed pentagram is on the forehead. His right hand is upraised, the reverse of the Hierophant's benediction, and his left holds a great flaming torch inverted towards the earth. A ring in front of the altar carries two chains to the necks of a man and a woman (Waite).

**Symbols.** The pair are "analogous with those of the fifth card, as if Adam and Eve after the Fall". The chains are "the chain and fatality of the material life". The figures are tailed to signify the animal nature, "but there is human intelligence in the faces, and he who is exalted above them is not to be their master for ever", for he too is a bondsman, "sustained by the evil that is in him and blind to the liberty of service". Waite derides Lévi's claim that the figure is occult science and magic, and rejects the view that it signifies predestination in the divine world. What it signifies is "the Dweller on the Threshold without the Mystical Garden", when those who have eaten the forbidden fruit are driven out.

**Meaning.** Ravage, violence, vehemence, extraordinary efforts, force, fatality; that which is predestined but is not for this reason evil.

**Reversed.** Evil fatality, weakness, pettiness, blindness.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes ayin and the sign Capricorn. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names bondage that the person in it has helped to build, such as habit, dependency or a fixed way of seeing.

**Links.** V, The Hierophant, whose benediction it reverses. VI, The Lovers, the same pair before the Fall. VIII, Strength, where the beast is led by a chain of flowers.

## XVI — The Tower

**Image.** Waite's text gives no scene description for this card beyond the title; the drawing shows a tall tower on a crag struck by lightning at the crown, flames at the windows, and two figures falling from it.

**Symbols.** Waite finds the occult explanations of the card "meagre and mostly disconcerting", and says it is idle to indicate that it depicts ruin in all its aspects since this appears on the surface. He sees no warrant in Papus for taking it literally as the fall of Adam, but more in favour of the alternative that it signifies the materialisation of the spiritual word. He agrees with Grand Orient that it is the ruin of the House of Life, "when evil has prevailed therein", and above all the rending of "a House of Doctrine", understood as a House of Falsehood. It illustrates the old truth "except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it". The two persons who are the living sufferers are "the literal word made void" and "its false interpretation". In yet a deeper sense it may signify the end of a dispensation. Waite calls the Tower an intellectual counterpart to the fall into the material state shown by the Devil.

**Meaning.** Misery, distress, indigence, adversity, calamity, disgrace, deception, ruin. It is a card in particular of unforeseen catastrophe.

**Reversed.** According to one account, the same in a lesser degree; also oppression, imprisonment, tyranny.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes pe and the planet Mars. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names the collapse of a structure built on a false premise, usually sudden and often unwelcome. What follows may be clearer, since the collapse removes what was in the way.

**Links.** XV, The Devil, the fall into the material state to which the Tower responds. V, The Hierophant, the House of Doctrine in its outward form. XVII, The Star, the card that follows with calm after the ruin.

## XVII — The Star

**Image.** A great, radiant star of eight rays is surrounded by seven lesser stars, also of eight rays. The female figure in the foreground is entirely naked. Her left knee is on the land and her right foot upon the water, and she pours Water of Life from two great ewers, "irrigating sea and land". Behind her is rising ground and on the right a shrub or tree on which a bird alights (Waite).

**Symbols.** The figure "expresses eternal youth and beauty". The star is the *étoile flamboyante* of Masonic symbolism. What she communicates to the living scene is "the substance of the heavens and the elements"; the mottoes of the card are "Waters of Life freely" and "Gifts of the Spirit". Waite sets aside the summary that it is a card of hope, and the other readings as immortality and interior light, as tawdry. To most prepared minds the figure appears as "Truth unveiled, glorious in undying beauty", but she is "in reality the Great Mother in the Kabalistic Sephira Binah, which is supernal Understanding", who gives to the Sephiroth below as they can receive her. The bird is the one detail Waite does not interpret; later readers sometimes call it an ibis.

**Meaning.** Loss, theft, privation, abandonment; another reading says hope and bright prospects.

**Reversed.** Arrogance, haughtiness, impotence.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes tsadi and the sign Aquarius, in the arrangement that exchanges the Star and the Emperor. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** Modern use follows Waite's second reading: the card names hope, renewal and a quiet confidence after a hard period. It suggests that what is needed is available and freely given.

**Links.** II, The High Priestess, who shares the Binah attribution. XVI, The Tower, the preceding collapse. XVIII, The Moon, the following card of reflected light.

## XVIII — The Moon

**Image.** The moon is shown increasing on the side of mercy, to the right of the observer, with sixteen chief and sixteen secondary rays. Below it a dog and a wolf stand on either side of a path between two towers, and from the water at the foot of the card something rises toward the land (Waite).

**Symbols.** Waite says the card "represents life of the imagination apart from life of the spirit". The path between the towers is "the issue into the unknown". The dog and wolf are "the fears of the natural mind in the presence of that place of exit, when there is only reflected light to guide it". The intellectual light is a reflection, and beyond it lies the unknown mystery it cannot show forth. The light illuminates the animal nature, including "the nameless and hideous tendency which is lower than the savage beast", which strives to crawl from the abyss to the land but as a rule sinks back. The face of the mind directs a calm gaze on the unrest below; the dew of thought falls; and the message is "Peace, be still". The creature from the water is drawn as a crayfish in Smith's card; Waite does not name it.

**Meaning.** Hidden enemies, danger, calumny, darkness, terror, deception, occult forces, error.

**Reversed.** Instability, inconstancy, silence, lesser degrees of deception and error.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes qoph and the sign Pisces. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names uncertainty in which fears and half-seen things shape judgement. It describes the condition before clarity, and advises care about what is taken for fact.

**Links.** XVII, The Star, the preceding true light against this reflected one. XIX, The Sun, the direct light that follows. II, The High Priestess, the other lunar figure.

## XIX — The Sun

**Image.** A naked child is mounted on a white horse and displays a red standard (Waite). Smith's drawing places a wall and sunflowers behind the child, and the sun above.

**Symbols.** Waite calls this "the better symbolism connected with this card", the destiny of the Supernatural East and "the great and holy light which goes before the endless procession of humanity, coming out from the walled garden of the sensitive life and passing on the journey home". The card signifies "the transit from the manifest light of this world, represented by the glorious sun of earth, to the light of the world to come", which goes before aspiration and is typified by the heart of a child. The sun is that of consciousness in the spirit, "the direct as the antithesis of the reflected light" shown on the Moon. Humanity has become a little child in the sense of simplicity, and in that innocence signifies "the restored world". When the self-knowing spirit has dawned above the natural mind, that mind in its renewal leads forth the animal nature in perfect conformity.

**Meaning.** Material happiness, fortunate marriage, contentment.

**Reversed.** The same in a lesser sense.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes resh and the Sun. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names clear success, warmth and good conditions without much qualification. It describes a time when things are visible and straightforward, and a confidence that needs no defence.

**Links.** XVIII, The Moon, the reflected light it replaces. XX, Judgement, the next card of a new life. 0, The Fool, another young figure under a bright sun, with a wand and rose where the child has a standard.

## XX — Judgement

**Image.** A great angel, encompassed by clouds, blows a bannered trumpet on which the cross is displayed. The dead rise from their tombs, a woman on the right, a man on the left, and between them their child, whose back is turned. Waite notes that more than three are restored and that he made this variation "as illustrating the insufficiency of current explanations". All the figures are as one in wonder, adoration and ecstasy.

**Symbols.** Waite says the symbol is essentially invariable in all Tarot sets. The card "registers the accomplishment of the great work of transformation in answer to the summons of the Supernal — which summons is heard and answered from within". He asks what it is within us that sounds a trumpet, so that all that is lower in our nature rises in response "almost in a moment, almost in the twinkling of an eye". For those who see no further, the card can continue to depict the Last Judgement and the resurrection in the natural body; those with inward eyes may discover more. It has been called truly a card of eternal life, and for this reason he compares it with Temperance. The Golden Dawn titles the card The Last Judgement. Later readers name the angel Gabriel.

**Meaning.** Change of position, renewal, outcome. Another account specifies total loss through lawsuit.

**Reversed.** Weakness, pusillanimity, simplicity; also deliberation, decision, sentence.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes shin and the element fire. Waite did not print these on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names a call to answer for the past and to take up a changed life. It describes a decision or an awakening that closes one phase and opens another.

**Links.** XIV, Temperance, which Waite compares with it. XIII, Death, the ending it answers. XXI, The World, the completion that follows.

## XXI — The World

**Image.** Waite's text gives no description of the picture, and discusses meanings only. The drawing shows a dancing figure within a wreath, holding a wand in each hand, with four creatures in the corners.

**Symbols.** Waite calls this "the final message of the Major Trumps", unchanged and unchangeable in design. It represents "the perfection and end of the Cosmos, the secret which is within it, the rapture of the universe when it understands itself in God", and the state of the soul in the consciousness of Divine Vision. It is also the restored world when the law of manifestation has been carried to the highest natural perfection, but "perhaps more especially a story of the past, referring to that day when all was declared to be good, when the morning stars sang together and all the Sons of God shouted for joy". He rejects the claims that the figure is the Magus at the highest initiation, the absolute (which he calls ridiculous), or Truth, which belongs to the seventeenth card. Lastly it has been called the Crown of the Magi. The four creatures are those of the Wheel of Fortune and of Ezekiel's vision; Waite does not discuss them under this card.

**Meaning.** Assured success, recompense, voyage, route, emigration, flight, change of place.

**Reversed.** Inertia, fixity, stagnation, permanence.

**Correspondences.** The Golden Dawn attributes tav and the planet Saturn. These are later attributions, not printed on the card.

**In a reading.** The card names completion, the end of a cycle with its aims met. It also suggests movement to a wider field, since a finished thing leaves room for the next.

**Links.** 0, The Fool, who begins the sequence the World completes. X, Wheel of Fortune, whose four creatures reappear at its corners. XX, Judgement, the summons that precedes it.

## Ace of Wands

**Image.** Waite's description is one sentence: "A hand issuing from a cloud grasps a stout wand or club." In Smith's drawing the wand is in leaf, with loose leaves falling from it. Below lie a river, hills and a castle on a distant height.

**Symbols.** The hand from the cloud appears on all four aces and stands for a gift from above. Waite says nothing of the leaves, the castle or the landscape. The budding wand fits his remark, made under the Queen, that the wands of this suit "are always in leaf, as it is a suit of life and animation." Later readers take the falling leaves for seeds of new growth.

**Meaning.** "Creation, invention, enterprise, the powers which result in these; principle, beginning, source; birth, family, origin, and in a sense the virility which is behind them; the starting point of enterprises." Waite adds that another account gives money, fortune and inheritance.

**Reversed.** "Fall, decadence, ruin, perdition, to perish also a certain clouded joy."

**In a reading.** The card names the start of a project, drive or undertaking, before any form has been given to it. It points to energy and opportunity on offer, and to the need to take them up.

## Two of Wands

**Image.** "A tall man looks from a battlemented roof over sea and shore; he holds a globe in his right hand, while a staff in his left rests on the battlement; another is fixed in a ring. The Rose and Cross and Lily should be noticed on the left side." (Waite)

**Symbols.** Waite points to the Rose and Cross and the Lily but does not explain them. Elsewhere in his writing the rose and the lily stand for desire and purity, and the Rose and Cross is a Rosicrucian emblem. His one interpretation of the design is that of "a lord overlooking his dominion and alternately contemplating a globe". He compares the mood to the sadness of Alexander amid the grandeur of the world. The sea and the distant shore are Smith's setting.

**Meaning.** Waite says that "between the alternative readings there is no marriage possible". One reading gives riches, fortune and magnificence. The other gives physical suffering, disease, chagrin, sadness and mortification.

**Reversed.** "Surprise, wonder, enchantment, emotion, trouble, fear."

**In a reading.** The card names a person who has made one gain and is deciding what to do with it. Dominion is in hand, and the choice is between staying and venturing further. Waite's dual reading keeps both prosperity and discontent in view.

## Three of Wands

**Image.** "A calm, stately personage, with his back turned, looking from a cliff's edge at ships passing over the sea. Three staves are planted in the ground, and he leans slightly on one of them." (Waite)

**Symbols.** Waite reads the ships as the man's own, "bearing his merchandise". The turned back and the open sea give the card its sense of distance. The cliff, the staves and the figure's red cloak are Smith's drawing, and Waite assigns no meaning to them. Later readers link the three wands to a plan already made and the ships to its results at a distance.

**Meaning.** "He symbolizes established strength, enterprise, effort, trade, commerce, discovery." Waite adds that the card signifies able co-operation in business, "as if the successful merchant prince were looking from his side towards yours with a view to help you."

**Reversed.** "The end of troubles, suspension or cessation of adversity, toil and disappointment."

**In a reading.** The card names ventures that have been launched and are now out of the querent's hands. The task is to wait and watch, with the prospect of returns and of help from partners. It describes foresight and trade across a distance.

## Four of Wands

**Image.** "From the four great staves planted in the foreground there is a great garland suspended; two female figures uplift nosegays; at their side is a bridge over a moat, leading to an old manorial house." (Waite)

**Symbols.** Waite gives no symbolic reading of the details. The garland and nosegays belong to a harvest-home, which he names in his meaning, and the manor, moat and bridge mark a settled household. The four staves form a gateway or canopy. Later readers see in this a ceremony such as a wedding, but Waite does not say so.

**Meaning.** "They are for once almost on the surface — country life, haven of refuge, a species of domestic harvest-home, repose, concord, harmony, prosperity, peace, and the perfected work of these." The phrase "for once almost on the surface" suggests that Waite found the card more direct than most.

**Reversed.** "The meaning remains unaltered; it is prosperity, increase, felicity, beauty, embellishment."

**In a reading.** The card names a completed stage that is celebrated: a home, a stable arrangement, a milestone reached with others. It shows rest after work, and a place of safety that the work has built.

## Five of Wands

**Image.** "A posse of youths, who are brandishing staves, as if in sport or strife." (Waite) Smith draws five figures in everyday dress, each with a wand, in apparent disorder and with no clear sides.

**Symbols.** Waite calls it "mimic warfare", a sham fight. The number of staves and of figures is the only detail he gives. The lack of weapons other than wands, and the absence of any wound, support his reading as a contest and not a battle. The scene is open and uncontrolled, and Waite offers no further symbolism.

**Meaning.** "Imitation, as, for example, sham fight, but also the strenuous competition and struggle of the search after riches and fortune. In this sense it connects with the battle of life. Hence some attributions say that it is a card of gold, gain, opulence."

**Reversed.** "Litigation, disputes, trickery, contradiction."

**In a reading.** The card names rivalry among peers: a crowded field, competing aims, argument over direction. Nobody is hurt, and no one prevails. The energy is real, but it is spent as much on noise as on result.

## Six of Wands

**Image.** "A laurelled horseman bears one staff adorned with a laurel crown; footmen with staves are at his side." (Waite) In Smith's drawing the rider wears a laurel wreath and sits on a white horse. The foot soldiers walk beside him, and a crowd is gathered around.

**Symbols.** The laurel is the classical crown of victory. Waite says the card "has been so designed that it can cover several significations": a victor in triumph, "great news, such as might be carried in state by the King's courier", and "expectation crowned with its own desire, the crown of hope". The colour of the horse is Smith's choice, and Waite does not comment on it.

**Meaning.** The three significations above, as Waite gives them. He treats victory as the surface reading.

**Reversed.** "Apprehension, fear, as of a victorious enemy at the gate; treachery, disloyalty, as of gates being opened to the enemy; also indefinite delay."

**In a reading.** The card names public success and recognition, or good news that is on its way. It depends on a following and on the effort of others. The honour is shared, and it tends to be short-lived.

## Seven of Wands

**Image.** "A young man on a craggy eminence brandishing a staff; six other staves are raised towards him from below." (Waite)

**Symbols.** Waite reads the picture as a contest in which "six are attacking one, who has, however, the vantage position". The height is his advantage. Smith draws the man in mismatched shoes, and Waite does not mention it. The six attackers are shown only by their staves.

**Meaning.** "It is a card of valour." On the intellectual plane it signifies "discussion, wordy strife"; in business, "negotiations, war of trade, barter, competition". Waite adds that it is "further a card of success, for the combatant is on the top and his enemies may be unable to reach him."

**Reversed.** "Perplexity, embarrassments, anxiety. It is also a caution against indecision."

**In a reading.** The card names a position that must be defended against several challengers, with the advantage of holding the higher ground. It calls for resolution. The struggle may be one of argument or competition, and it is not yet over.

## Eight of Wands

**Image.** Waite writes that the card "represents motion through the immovable — a flight of wands through an open country; but they draw to the term of their course." Smith's drawing has no human figure. Eight wands slant across the air above a river and a green landscape.

**Symbols.** Waite says what the wands signify "is at hand; it may be even on the threshold". He adds "the arrows of love" to the meanings. The empty landscape is Smith's invention. The wands are all at the same angle, which gives the sense of a single movement.

**Meaning.** "Activity in undertakings, the path of such activity, swiftness, as that of an express messenger; great haste, great hope, speed towards an end which promises assured felicity; generally, that which is on the move; also the arrows of love."

**Reversed.** "Arrows of jealousy, internal dispute, stingings of conscience, quarrels; and domestic disputes for persons who are married."

**In a reading.** The card names events gathering speed: messages, travel, a decision that moves quickly towards its conclusion. The outcome is close, and little control remains once the flight has begun.

## Nine of Wands

**Image.** "The figure leans upon his staff and has an expectant look, as if awaiting an enemy. Behind are eight other staves — erect, in orderly disposition, like a palisade." (Waite) Smith's figure has a bandaged head and looks back over his shoulder.

**Symbols.** Waite gives the eight staves as a palisade, a defence that the figure has built. The bandage is not mentioned in his text and is a later talking point, usually read as a sign of earlier injury. Waite says only that the man's build "shews, that he may prove a formidable antagonist".

**Meaning.** "The card signifies strength in opposition. If attacked, the person will meet an onslaught boldly." With this main significance go "all its possible adjuncts — delay, suspension, adjournment."

**Reversed.** "Obstacles, adversity, calamity."

**In a reading.** The card names endurance after earlier conflict, with a defence built and an expected return of trouble. The figure is wary and wounded and still on his feet. It indicates reserves that have not run out, and a pause before the next test.

## Ten of Wands

**Image.** "A man oppressed by the weight of the ten staves which he is carrying." (Waite) In Smith's drawing the man is bent forward under a bundle of wands, and his face is hidden. A village or town lies ahead of him.

**Symbols.** Waite says the card has "many significances, and some of the readings cannot be harmonized". The bundle is the burden, and he notes that "the place which the figure is approaching may suffer from the rods that he carries." The village and the bent back are Smith's drawing, and Waite offers no further symbolism.

**Meaning.** "The chief meaning is oppression simply, but it is also fortune, gain, any kind of success, and then it is the oppression of these things." It also bears the senses of "false-seeming, disguise, perfidy". Waite sets aside the readings that tie it to honour and good faith. He adds that success is stultified if the Nine of Swords follows, and that in a lawsuit there will be certain loss.

**Reversed.** "Contrarieties, difficulties, intrigues, and their analogies."

**In a reading.** The card names an overload of commitments, often those taken on through success. The goal is near, and the weight is the cost of reaching it.

## Page of Wands

**Image.** Waite writes: "In a scene similar to the former, a young man stands in the act of proclamation. He is unknown but faithful, and his tidings are strange." Smith shows the Page holding a wand upright, in a desert with pyramids in the distance. His tunic carries a pattern of salamander-like figures.

**Symbols.** Waite describes only the act of proclamation and says the Page "has the chief qualities of his suit". The desert, the pyramids and the salamander pattern are Smith's drawing, and Waite does not interpret them. The salamander is the traditional creature of fire, and later readers connect it to the suit's element.

**Meaning.** "Dark young man, faithful, a lover, an envoy, a postman. Beside a man, he will bear favourable testimony concerning him. A dangerous rival, if followed by the Page of Cups. Has the chief qualities of his suit. He may signify family intelligence."

**Reversed.** "Anecdotes, announcements, evil news. Also indecision and the instability which accompanies it."

**In a reading.** The card names a messenger or a young, eager person, and news that carries an invitation to act. It can also name an early enthusiasm that has yet to be tested.

## Knight of Wands

**Image.** "He is shewn as if upon a journey, armed with a short wand, and although mailed is not on a warlike errand. He is passing mounds or pyramids. The motion of the horse is a key to the character of its rider, and suggests the precipitate mood, or things connected therewith." (Waite) Smith draws the horse rearing, and the Knight wears a surcoat with salamander figures.

**Symbols.** Waite gives the horse's motion as the sign of a hasty temper. The mounds or pyramids lie in the background. The salamander design is Smith's, with the usual association to fire.

**Meaning.** "Departure, absence, flight, emigration. A dark young man, friendly. Change of residence."

**Reversed.** "Rupture, division, interruption, discord."

**In a reading.** The card names movement and a change of place, undertaken with more speed than care. As a person it describes an impulsive, spirited traveller. It suggests an abrupt start, and the risk lies in leaving before the ground is prepared.

## Queen of Wands

**Image.** Waite gives no picture description of this card. He says only that the wands of the suit "are always in leaf, as it is a suit of life and animation", and that "emotionally and otherwise, the Queen's personality corresponds to that of the King, but is more magnetic." Smith shows her enthroned, holding a wand in one hand and a sunflower in the other. A black cat sits at her feet.

**Symbols.** The sunflower, the black cat and the lions carved on the throne are Smith's drawing, and Waite does not interpret them. Later readers take the sunflower for warmth and the cat for independence. The lion appears in Waite's text on the King.

**Meaning.** "A dark woman, countrywoman, friendly, chaste, loving, honourable. If the card beside her signifies a man, she is well disposed towards him; if a woman, she is interested in the Querent. Also, love of money, or a certain success in business."

**Reversed.** "Good, economical, obliging, serviceable." Waite adds that in certain positions it signifies "opposition, jealousy, even deceit and infidelity."

**In a reading.** The card names a warm, confident person with presence, or those qualities in the querent. Energy is directed at others and at practical affairs.

## King of Wands

**Image.** Waite describes the nature to which the card is attributed: "dark, ardent, lithe, animated, impassioned, noble. The King uplifts a flowering wand, and wears, like his three correspondences in the remaining suits, what is called a cap of maintenance beneath his crown. He connects with the symbol of the lion, which is emblazoned on the back of his throne."

**Symbols.** The cap of maintenance is a ceremonial cap worn under a crown, and Waite says all four Kings have it. The lion is the heraldic emblem of royal courage. The flowering wand matches the leafing wands of the suit. The salamander on his cloak is Smith's drawing, and Waite does not mention it.

**Meaning.** "Dark man, friendly, countryman, generally married, honest and conscientious. The card always signifies honesty, and may mean news concerning an unexpected heritage to fall in before very long."

**Reversed.** "Good, but severe; austere, yet tolerant."

**In a reading.** The card names a seasoned, decisive leader, or the capacity to lead with candour and vision. It describes authority built on experience in enterprise.

## Ace of Cups

**Image.** A hand emerges from a cloud and holds a cup in its palm. Four streams pour from the cup into the waters below, where water-lilies float. A dove descends toward the cup carrying in its bill a Host marked with a cross, and drops of water fall on every side.

**Symbols.** Waite calls the card "an intimation of that which may lie behind the Lesser Arcana". The hand from the cloud is the gift from above that opens each suit. The dove and the Host give the image a Eucharistic cast: the Wafer is placed in the Cup. The four streams are often read as the four rivers of Eden or the four suits, though Waite does not say so. Water is the element the tradition assigns to Cups, and the lilies belong to the same watery setting. The falling drops are Waite's "dew of water".

**Meaning.** House of the true heart, joy, content, abode, nourishment, abundance, fertility; Holy Table, felicity hereof.

**Reversed.** House of the false heart, mutation, instability, revolution.

**In a reading.** The card names an offering of feeling that is whole and freely given, whether love, contentment or a place where the querent is fed. Later readings extend it to the start of an emotional or creative chapter.

## Two of Cups

**Image.** A youth and a maiden pledge one another, each holding a cup. Above the cups rises the caduceus of Hermes, and between its wings appears a lion's head.

**Symbols.** Waite says the winged caduceus with the lion's head is a variant of a sign found in a few old examples of this card. He adds that "some curious emblematical meanings are attached to it, but they do not concern us in this place", and he gives no interpretation. The caduceus is the wand of Hermes, and the lion's head is usually read, in later accounts, as passion or strength joined to the union. The pledge itself is the plain subject of the picture. Waite gives no symbolism for the figures' clothing or the landscape.

**Meaning.** Love, passion, friendship, affinity, union, concord, sympathy, the interrelation of the sexes, and, as a suggestion apart from all offices of divination, "that desire which is not in Nature, but by which Nature is sanctified".

**Reversed.** Waite gives no reversed meaning for this card in the Pictorial Key.

**In a reading.** The card names a bond between two parties, freely entered and mutual, whether romantic, friendly or between partners in a common undertaking. It speaks of agreement more than of attraction alone.

## Three of Cups

**Image.** Maidens in a garden-ground raise their cups, as if pledging one another.

**Symbols.** Waite gives only this brief description and no commentary on the symbols. The garden and the raised cups are the whole of what he records. Later readings take the three women as friendship, community or a shared celebration, and the plenty of the garden as the fruit of the occasion. These are later readings. The three-figure grouping, the garlands and the harvest produce in Smith's drawing carry no explanation in Waite's text.

**Meaning.** The conclusion of any matter in plenty, perfection and merriment; happy issue, victory, fulfilment, solace, healing.

**Reversed.** Expedition, dispatch, achievement, end. Waite adds that it signifies also "the side of excess in physical enjoyment, and the pleasures of the senses".

**In a reading.** The card names a completion marked with company: a task finished, a recovery, a gathering held to mark it. Reversed, it points to indulgence, or to a matter hurried to its end.

## Four of Cups

**Image.** A young man sits under a tree and contemplates three cups set on the grass before him. An arm issuing from a cloud offers him a fourth cup. His expression is one of discontent with his surroundings.

**Symbols.** Waite's description is brief. The three cups already in front of him stand for what he has had; the fourth, held out from the cloud, is the gift of heaven or fortune that he does not take up. Waite likens it to "a fairy gift", and says the wastrel "sees no consolation therein". The tree and the seated pose are Smith's setting, and Waite does not interpret them.

**Meaning.** Weariness, disgust, aversion, imaginary vexations, as if the wine of this world had caused satiety only. Waite adds that it is also "a card of blended pleasure".

**Reversed.** Novelty, presage, new instruction, new relations.

**In a reading.** The card names boredom or withdrawal amid comfort, and an offer that goes unnoticed because attention is turned inward. The reversed card suggests that the mood has lifted and something new is being admitted.

## Five of Cups

**Image.** A dark, cloaked figure looks sideways at three prone cups. Two other cups stand upright behind him. A bridge in the background leads to a small keep or holding.

**Symbols.** Waite lists the spilled cups, the two that remain, and the bridge and keep, and he gives no allegory for them. His reading is arithmetical: "three have been taken, but two are left". Later accounts add that the spilled liquid and the cloak signal grief, and that the bridge and house suggest a way home. Those are later readings.

**Meaning.** A card of loss, but something remains over. It is a card of inheritance, patrimony and transmission, but not corresponding to expectations. With some interpreters it is a card of marriage, but not without bitterness or frustration.

**Reversed.** News, alliances, affinity, consanguinity, ancestry, return, false projects.

**In a reading.** The card names a loss that holds the querent's attention while what is left goes unregarded. It is often read as mourning that has begun to turn toward what survives.

## Six of Cups

**Image.** Children in an old garden, their cups filled with flowers.

**Symbols.** Waite describes the picture in one line and gives no commentary on the objects. The cups hold flowers where other cards of the suit show wine or water. Smith's drawing also shows a house and a figure in the background, which Waite does not mention. The gifts exchanged between the children are a later reading of the image as kindness or innocence.

**Meaning.** A card of the past and of memories, looking back, as on childhood; happiness, enjoyment, but coming rather from the past; things that have vanished. Waite notes another reading that reverses this, giving new relations, new knowledge, new environment, and then the children are disporting in an unfamiliar precinct.

**Reversed.** The future, renewal, that which will come to pass presently.

**In a reading.** The card names memory, nostalgia and old ties, and sometimes a person or place from the querent's early life. Waite's own note that the readings are inverted in some systems means the card can face forward as well as back.

## Seven of Cups

**Image.** Waite's entry describes "strange chalices of vision", and adds that the images are more especially those of the fantastic spirit.

**Symbols.** Waite does not itemise the contents of the cups. In Smith's drawing a figure seen from behind faces seven cups floating in clouds, each holding a different object. The objects are Smith's invention, and Waite gives no list or meaning for them. Later readings treat them as choices, temptations or fantasies. Waite's own phrases are "fairy favours" and "things seen in the glass of contemplation".

**Meaning.** Fairy favours, images of reflection, sentiment, imagination, things seen in the glass of contemplation; some attainment in these degrees, but nothing permanent or substantial is suggested.

**Reversed.** Desire, will, determination, project.

**In a reading.** The card names a surplus of possibilities, many of them imagined rather than real, and the difficulty of choosing among them. Reversed, it marks the point at which wishing gives way to a decision.

## Eight of Cups

**Image.** A man of dejected aspect is deserting the cups of his felicity, enterprise, undertaking or previous concern.

**Symbols.** Waite says the card "speaks for itself on the surface", and he supplies no further symbolism. Smith's drawing shows the cups stacked in the foreground, a figure walking away with a staff, and hills and water beyond. Waite does not explain these details. The moon in the sky is not mentioned by him, and any lunar reading is a later one.

**Meaning.** The card speaks for itself on the surface, but other readings are entirely antithetical, giving joy, mildness, timidity, honour, modesty. In practice, it is usually found that the card shows the decline of a matter, or that a matter which has been thought to be important is really of slight consequence, either for good or evil.

**Reversed.** Great joy, happiness, feasting.

**In a reading.** The card names a departure from something that was once valued, whether a job, a relationship or an aim. Waite's note that the matter may prove slight means the leaving can be a relief as well as a loss.

## Nine of Cups

**Image.** A goodly personage has feasted to his heart's content. Abundant refreshment of wine is on the arched counter behind him, which seems to indicate that the future is also assured.

**Symbols.** Waite says the picture "offers the material side only, but there are other aspects", and he does not name them. The man seated before the curved shelf of cups is the figure of satisfied appetite. In Smith's drawing nine cups stand in an arc behind him. Popular later usage calls this the wish card, a reading Waite does not give.

**Meaning.** Concord, contentment, physical bien-être; also victory, success, advantage; satisfaction for the Querent or person for whom the consultation is made.

**Reversed.** Truth, loyalty, liberty; but the readings vary and include mistakes, imperfections, etc.

**In a reading.** The card names comfort and a wish fulfilled, with the plain pleasure of having enough. Waite's hint that the picture shows only the material side suggests the contentment may be bodily more than spiritual.

## Ten of Cups

**Image.** A rainbow shows cups in the sky. A man and a woman, evidently husband and wife, contemplate them in wonder and ecstasy. His right arm is about her, and his left is raised upward; she raises her right arm. Two children dance near them and have not observed the prodigy, but are happy after their own manner. A home-scene lies beyond.

**Symbols.** The cups in the rainbow are the apparition; the family below is the human setting. Waite says the children are unaware of the sign. The home in the distance is part of the scene he describes, and he does not interpret it. Rainbow imagery generally recalls a covenant, though Waite does not draw the link.

**Meaning.** Contentment, repose of the entire heart; the perfection of that state; also perfection of human love and friendship. If with several picture-cards, a person who is taking charge of the Querent's interests; also the town, village or country inhabited by the Querent.

**Reversed.** Repose of the false heart, indignation, violence.

**In a reading.** The card names settled happiness in a household or community, and the sense of belonging that accompanies it. The reversed card marks a calm that is only apparent.

## Page of Cups

**Image.** A fair, pleasing, somewhat effeminate page, of studious and intent aspect, contemplates a fish rising from a cup to look at him.

**Symbols.** Waite explains the fish directly: "It is the pictures of the mind taking form." The cup is a vessel of imagination, and the fish that rises from it is an image arriving unbidden. He gives no meaning for the page's costume or the sea behind him, which Smith adds.

**Meaning.** Fair young man, one impelled to render service and with whom the Querent will be connected; a studious youth; news, message; application, reflection, meditation; also these things directed to business.

**Reversed.** Taste, inclination, attachment, seduction, deception, artifice.

**In a reading.** The card names a young or gentle person who brings a message or a fresh idea, and the first stir of an imaginative or emotional impulse. It often marks a piece of news that asks for reflection rather than action.

## Knight of Cups

**Image.** Graceful but not warlike, the knight rides quietly, wearing a winged helmet.

**Symbols.** Waite says the winged helmet refers to "those higher graces of the imagination which sometimes characterize this card". He calls the knight a dreamer, "but the images of the side of sense haunt him in his vision". In Smith's drawing he holds out a cup, and the horse walks, which fits the quiet riding Waite describes. The stream and landscape are not interpreted by Waite.

**Meaning.** Arrival, approach, sometimes that of a messenger; advances, proposition, demeanour, invitation, incitement.

**Reversed.** Trickery, artifice, subtlety, swindling, duplicity, fraud.

**In a reading.** The card names an offer or invitation, often romantic or creative, delivered with courtesy. The reversed card cautions that the charm may cover less than it promises.

## Queen of Cups

**Image.** Beautiful, fair and dreamy, as one who sees visions in a cup. Waite adds that this is only one of her aspects: she sees, but she also acts, and her activity feeds her dream.

**Symbols.** The cup is the instrument of her vision. Waite's brief description gives her no other attributes, and he does not interpret her throne, her dress or the shore on which she sits. Smith's cup is closed and ornate, which later readers take to mean that the visions are private. That is a later reading.

**Meaning.** Good, fair woman; honest, devoted woman, who will do service to the Querent; loving intelligence, and hence the gift of vision; success, happiness, pleasure; also wisdom, virtue; a perfect spouse and a good mother.

**Reversed.** The accounts vary: good woman; otherwise, distinguished woman but one not to be trusted; perverse woman; vice, dishonour, depravity.

**In a reading.** The card names a caring, perceptive person, or the same qualities in the querent: attention to feeling joined to practical help. Waite's note that she acts as well as dreams separates her from simple passivity.

## King of Cups

**Image.** He holds a short sceptre in his left hand and a great cup in his right. His throne is set upon the sea; on one side a ship is riding, and on the other a dolphin is leaping.

**Symbols.** Waite says the suit of Cups "naturally refers to water, which appears in all the court cards". The sea beneath the throne makes the king ruler of the emotional element, and the ship and dolphin are its inhabitants. Waite gives no further meaning for them. Later readings take the king's steadiness amid the waves as mastery of feeling, which is not Waite's wording.

**Meaning.** Fair man, man of business, law, or divinity; responsible, disposed to oblige the Querent; also equity, art and science, including those who profess science, law and art; creative intelligence.

**Reversed.** Dishonest, double-dealing man; roguery, exaction, injustice, vice, scandal, pillage, considerable loss.

**In a reading.** The card names a mature person, usually in a profession, who combines feeling with judgment and can be relied on for counsel. Reversed, it marks someone who uses position for gain.

## Ace of Swords

**Image.** Waite describes "a hand issues from a cloud, grasping a sword, the point of which is encircled by a crown." On Smith's card the hand comes from a cloud at the left and holds the sword upright, with an olive branch and a palm hanging from the crown, and bare mountains lie far below.

**Symbols.** The cloud and the hand are the usual device of the four aces, a gift from above the visible world. Waite says only that "the crown may carry a much higher significance than comes usually within the sphere of fortune-telling," and does not explain it further. The sword is the emblem of the suit, which Waite calls a suit of trouble rather than of benefit. The palm and olive branches that hang from the crown on Smith's drawing are not mentioned by Waite; later readers take them as victory and peace.

**Meaning.** Triumph, the excessive degree in everything, conquest, triumph of force. Waite calls it "a card of great force, in love as well as in hatred."

**Reversed.** The same, but the results are disastrous. Another account, Waite adds, gives conception, childbirth, augmentation and multiplicity.

**In a reading.** A later reading takes the card as the start of a matter decided by clear thought: a verdict, a diagnosis, a plan with an edge to it. Because Waite stresses excess, the force it names can cut either way.

## Two of Swords

**Image.** Waite gives one line: "a hoodwinked female figure balances two swords upon her shoulders." Smith seats her on a stone bench with her arms crossed over her chest, a calm sea behind her and a crescent moon in the sky.

**Symbols.** Waite gives no account of the bandage, the sea or the moon, and they should be taken as Smith's setting. The blindfold and the crossed blades suggest a balance held by refusing to look, and the later tradition reads the card as a stalemate or a decision put off. Waite instead stresses that the favourable meanings must be "considered in a qualified manner, as Swords generally are not symbolical of beneficent forces in human affairs."

**Meaning.** Conformity and the equipoise which it suggests, courage, friendship, concord in a state of arms. Another reading gives tenderness, affection, intimacy.

**Reversed.** Imposture, falsehood, duplicity, disloyalty.

**In a reading.** The card names a standoff in which both sides, or both options, are kept level for the time being. The peace is armed and may not last.

## Three of Swords

**Image.** Waite's description is "three swords piercing a heart; cloud and rain behind." Smith draws a red heart held up by three blades, under a grey sky streaked with rain.

**Symbols.** Waite thinks the design needs no commentary: it is "too simple and obvious to call for specific enumeration." The heart, the swords and the rain carry their ordinary sense of grief, and no esoteric attribution is given for them in the Key.

**Meaning.** Removal, absence, delay, division, rupture, dispersion, "and all that the design signifies naturally."

**Reversed.** Mental alienation, error, loss, distraction, disorder, confusion.

**In a reading.** The card names a separation or a wound inflicted by words or events, and the sorrow that follows. Later readers often apply it to heartbreak, a sense Waite's list leaves implicit.

## Four of Swords

**Image.** Waite describes "the effigy of a knight in the attitude of prayer, at full length upon his tomb." Smith shows a stone figure with hands joined, three swords hung on the wall above and one laid beneath the effigy.

**Symbols.** Waite says the meanings of tomb and coffin "have suggested the design," so the knight on the monument comes from the divinatory sense of the card and the rest of the picture is Smith's. A stained-glass window in her drawing shows a figure giving or receiving something, and no explanation of it appears in the Key. Later readers connect the image with rest after conflict.

**Meaning.** Vigilance, retreat, solitude, hermit's repose, exile, tomb and coffin.

**Reversed.** Wise administration, circumspection, economy, avarice, precaution, testament.

**In a reading.** The card names a pause enforced or chosen, a withdrawal to recover after struggle. Waite's reversed meanings turn toward careful management of what remains.

## Five of Swords

**Image.** In Waite's words, "a disdainful man looks after two retreating and dejected figures. Their swords lie upon the ground. He carries two others on his left shoulder, and a third sword is in his right hand, point to earth. He is the master in possession of the field." Smith sets the scene on a shore under a ragged sky.

**Symbols.** Waite offers no symbolism beyond the picture of defeat itself. The victor holds three swords, the defeated two have left theirs behind, and the figures walk away toward the water. The stormy sky and the shoreline are Smith's additions and carry no stated meaning.

**Meaning.** Degradation, destruction, revocation, infamy, dishonour, loss, with the variants and analogues of these.

**Reversed.** The same; burial and obsequies.

**In a reading.** The card names a contest won in a way that costs the winner something, or a loss that comes with humiliation. Later readers add the question of what the victory was worth.

## Six of Swords

**Image.** Waite writes of "a ferryman carrying passengers in his punt to the further shore. The course is smooth, and seeing that the freight is light, it may be noted that the work is not beyond his strength." Smith shows a cloaked woman and a child sitting in the boat, six swords standing upright in its bow, and a rougher water behind them than before.

**Symbols.** Waite gives only the ferryman, the punt and the shore. The upright swords as cargo, the bowed passengers and the change from choppy to smooth water are Smith's drawing, and the usual later reading is of passage away from trouble.

**Meaning.** Journey by water, route, way, envoy, commissionary, expedient.

**Reversed.** Declaration, confession, publicity; one account says that it is a proposal of love.

**In a reading.** The card names a transition, often a move or a journey, made with sorrow carried along. The work is not heavy, in Waite's phrase, and the direction is away from the worst.

## Seven of Swords

**Image.** Waite describes "a man in the act of carrying away five swords rapidly; the two others of the card remain stuck in the ground. A camp is close at hand." Smith draws him on tiptoe, glancing back, with tents behind him.

**Symbols.** Waite attaches no symbolism to the swords or the camp. He admits the design "is uncertain in its import, because the significations are widely at variance with each other," and his list bears this out, running from hope to quarrel. The popular label of theft is a later reading of the figure's furtive stance.

**Meaning.** Design, attempt, wish, hope, confidence; also quarrelling, a plan that may fail, annoyance.

**Reversed.** Good advice, counsel, instruction, slander, babbling.

**In a reading.** The card names a scheme pursued alone and by stealth, with some chance that it fails. The two swords left behind are usually taken as the price of the attempt.

## Eight of Swords

**Image.** Waite gives "a woman, bound and hoodwinked, with the swords of the card about her. Yet it is rather a card of temporary durance than of irretrievable bondage." In Smith's drawing the swords stand in the ground around the figure, and a castle stands on a rock in the distance, with water at her feet.

**Symbols.** Waite stresses the temporary nature of the confinement. The loose binding, the shallow water and the distant castle are in Smith's picture and unexplained in the Key; later readers see in them a restraint that could be undone.

**Meaning.** Bad news, violent chagrin, crisis, censure, power in trammels, conflict, calumny; also sickness.

**Reversed.** Disquiet, difficulty, opposition, accident, treachery; what is unforeseen; fatality.

**In a reading.** The card names a sense of being hemmed in, by circumstance or by judgment. Waite reads the constraint as passing.

## Nine of Swords

**Image.** Waite has "one seated on her couch in lamentation, with the swords over her. She is as one who knows no sorrow which is like unto hers. It is a card of utter desolation." Smith shows her sitting up in bed with her face in her hands and nine swords hung horizontally on the black wall above.

**Symbols.** Waite gives no symbolism beyond the grief itself. The quilt, which carries roses and astrological signs, and the carved figures on the bedstead are Smith's embellishments, and he does not interpret them.

**Meaning.** Death, failure, miscarriage, delay, deception, disappointment, despair.

**Reversed.** Imprisonment, suspicion, doubt, reasonable fear, shame.

**In a reading.** The card names anguish, often at night, and the worry that feeds on itself. Waite's reversed list moves toward fear and doubt, which he counts as reasonable.

## Ten of Swords

**Image.** Waite describes "a prostrate figure, pierced by all the swords belonging to the card." Smith draws a man face down on dark ground, ten blades along his back, with black sky above and a pale band of light at the horizon.

**Symbols.** Waite adds nothing beyond the design: "whatsoever is intimated by the design." The dawn on the horizon and the calm water are Smith's, and later readers take them as the end of the worst. Waite himself warns that "it is not especially a card of violent death."

**Meaning.** Whatsoever is intimated by the design; also pain, affliction, tears, sadness, desolation.

**Reversed.** Advantage, profit, success, favour, but none of these are permanent; also power and authority.

**In a reading.** The card names a collapse that is complete, after which nothing further can be taken. Waite's reversal is a gain of short duration.

## Page of Swords

**Image.** Waite writes: "A lithe, active figure holds a sword upright in both hands, while in the act of swift walking. He is passing over rugged land, and about his way the clouds are collocated wildly. He is alert and lithe, looking this way and that, as if an expected enemy might appear at any moment." In Smith's drawing the wind bends the trees and birds fly in the sky.

**Symbols.** Waite's account is of vigilance. The wind, the birds and the rough ground belong to the picture, and he assigns them no further meaning.

**Meaning.** Authority, overseeing, secret service, vigilance, spying, examination, and the qualities thereto belonging.

**Reversed.** The more evil side of these qualities; what is unforeseen, unprepared state; sickness is also intimated.

**In a reading.** The card names a watchful, inquiring person, or a stretch of scrutiny and gathering of facts. Later readers often take it as a young or newly alert mind.

## Knight of Swords

**Image.** Waite writes: "He is riding in full course, as if scattering his enemies. In the design he is really a prototypical hero of romantic chivalry. He might almost be Galahad, whose sword is swift and sure because he is clean of heart." Smith shows a rider charging with a raised sword, his horse at full stretch, the trees bent and the clouds torn by wind.

**Symbols.** Waite's one explicit comparison is to Galahad, the knight of the Grail. The butterflies and birds on the rider's gear and the wind belong to Smith's design and are not discussed in the Key.

**Meaning.** Skill, bravery, capacity, defence, address, enmity, wrath, war, destruction, opposition, resistance, ruin. Waite adds that the card signifies death only "in its proximity to other cards of fatality."

**Reversed.** Imprudence, incapacity, extravagance.

**In a reading.** The card names speed and decisiveness, valuable in a fight and costly when the aim is wrong. Reversed, the same energy lacks judgment.

## Queen of Swords

**Image.** Waite notes that her right hand raises the sword vertically with the hilt resting on an arm of her royal chair, and the left hand is extended. "Her countenance is severe but chastened; it suggests familiarity with sorrow. It does not represent mercy, and, her sword notwithstanding, she is scarcely a symbol of power." Smith places her high on a cliff with the clouds around her and a bird in the sky.

**Symbols.** The butterflies and cherub carved on her throne are not discussed by Waite, nor is the bird. Later readers take her as a figure of clear sight bought by loss.

**Meaning.** Widowhood, female sadness and embarrassment, absence, sterility, mourning, privation, separation.

**Reversed.** Malice, bigotry, artifice, prudery, bale, deceit.

**In a reading.** The card names a person or a manner shaped by grief and without sentiment. Later readers stress her directness and independence, which Waite does not.

## King of Swords

**Image.** Waite says: "He sits in judgment, holding the unsheathed sign of his suit. He recalls, of course, the conventional symbol of justice in the Trumps Major, and he may represent this virtue, but he is rather the power of life and death, in virtue of his office." Smith seats him frontally on a throne, a sword upright in his right hand, with clouds and a few birds behind him.

**Symbols.** The comparison with Justice, card XI, is Waite's own. Butterflies and crescent moons carved on the throne appear in Smith's drawing and are unexplained in the Key.

**Meaning.** Whatsoever arises out of the idea of judgment and all its connexions: power, command, authority, militant intelligence, law, offices of the crown.

**Reversed.** Cruelty, perversity, barbarity, perfidy, evil intention.

**In a reading.** The card names someone with authority to decide, such as a judge, a lawyer or a superior, or the exercise of reasoned judgment. Reversed, the same authority is used without mercy.

## Ace of Pentacles

**Image.** "A hand — issuing, as usual, from a cloud — holds up a pentacle" (Waite). Smith places the hand above a garden with a flowering arch and a path leading to distant mountains.

**Symbols.** Waite gives only the hand and the pentacle. As in the other three aces, the hand from a cloud marks a gift offered from beyond the figures of the suit. The pentacle, a coin engraved with a pentagram, stands for the suit's element, earth. Waite says the pentagram typifies "the correspondence of the four elements in human nature and that by which they may be governed." The garden, arch and mountains are Smith's additions, and Waite does not interpret them. Later readings take the path as an invitation to enter a settled, fruitful life.

**Meaning.** Perfect contentment, felicity, ecstasy; also speedy intelligence; gold.

**Reversed.** The evil side of wealth, bad intelligence; also great riches. Waite adds that the card shows prosperity and comfortable material conditions in either position, but whether these benefit the possessor depends on whether it is reversed.

**In a reading.** The card names a material beginning: an offer, a payment, a secure footing, a first real asset. It concerns resources that can be used, not yet resources that have grown.

## Two of Pentacles

**Image.** "A young man, in the act of dancing, has a pentacle in either hand, and they are joined by that endless cord which is like the number 8 reversed" (Waite). Smith adds a tall hat and two ships riding large waves behind him.

**Symbols.** The cord forms a lemniscate, the figure-eight lying on its side, which Waite elsewhere uses as a sign of endless continuation. He does not describe the ships or the sea, which are Smith's invention. Later readings take the waves and ships as the swings of fortune that the dancer must balance, and the two coins as two demands on his attention.

**Meaning.** On the one hand a card of gaiety and recreation, which is the subject of the design; but it is read also as news and messages in writing, as obstacles, agitation, trouble, embroilment.

**Reversed.** Enforced gaiety, simulated enjoyment, literal sense, handwriting, composition, letters of exchange.

**In a reading.** The card names the handling of two matters at once, usually money or commitments, with a light touch and some risk of dropping one. Waite's own list pairs ease with agitation, and the card is read for either.

## Three of Pentacles

**Image.** "A sculptor at his work in a monastery" (Waite). Smith shows a craftsman on a bench working at a carved arch, while two figures, one in a monk's habit, stand by with a plan in hand.

**Symbols.** Waite points to the Eight of Pentacles: the apprentice there "has received his reward and is now at work in earnest." The three pentacles are carved into the arch above the worker. Waite gives no further interpretation of the plan or the onlookers. Later readings treat the scene as collaboration among craftsman, designer and patron.

**Meaning.** Métier, trade, skilled labour; usually, however, regarded as a card of nobility, aristocracy, renown, glory.

**Reversed.** Mediocrity, in work and otherwise, puerility, pettiness, weakness.

**In a reading.** The card names competence put to work on a lasting structure, with the approval of those who commission or inspect it. It is read for skill that has been recognised, and for projects that need more than one pair of hands.

## Four of Pentacles

**Image.** "A crowned figure, having a pentacle over his crown, clasps another with hands and arms; two pentacles are under his feet. He holds to that which he has" (Waite). Smith seats him before a city, in a dark cloak.

**Symbols.** Waite names the figure's posture and nothing more: the coins are held on the head, at the chest and under the feet, so that the man is in contact with all four. The city behind him is Smith's setting, and Waite does not interpret it. Later readings take the arrangement as a picture of possessiveness, with the body closed around its property.

**Meaning.** The surety of possessions, cleaving to that which one has, gift, legacy, inheritance.

**Reversed.** Suspense, delay, opposition.

**In a reading.** The card names security and the wish to keep it: savings, property, an inheritance held on to. Whether this reads as prudence or as miserliness depends on the surrounding cards; Waite's wording, "he holds to that which he has," allows either.

## Five of Pentacles

**Image.** "Two mendicants in a snow-storm pass a lighted casement" (Waite). In Smith's card one is lame, on crutches, and the other, barefoot, goes bent beside him; the lit window is a church's stained glass, with five pentacles in its pattern.

**Symbols.** Waite describes only the beggars, the snow and the window. The church window and the crutches are Smith's details. Later readings take the window as shelter that the pair fail to notice or cannot enter.

**Meaning.** The card foretells material trouble above all, whether in the form illustrated, that is, destitution, or otherwise. For some cartomancists it is a card of love and lovers, wife, husband, friend, mistress; also concordance, affinities. Waite adds that these alternatives cannot be harmonized.

**Reversed.** Disorder, chaos, ruin, discord, profligacy.

**In a reading.** The card names want: of money, work or support, and often a sense of being shut out. Its reading is hardship, but the lit window leaves open the possibility of help nearby.

## Six of Pentacles

**Image.** "A person in the guise of a merchant weighs money in a pair of scales and distributes it to the needy and distressed. It is a testimony to his own success in life, as well as to his goodness of heart" (Waite). Two beggars kneel at his feet.

**Symbols.** The scales and the coins are the main symbols; Waite gives no further interpretation. The unequal positions of giver and recipients are part of the design. Later readings take the scales as a sign of fair dealing in giving, and note that the card can be read from either side of the exchange.

**Meaning.** Presents, gifts, gratification; another account says attention, vigilance, now is the accepted time, present prosperity, etc.

**Reversed.** Desire, cupidity, envy, jealousy, illusion.

**In a reading.** The card names an exchange of resources between those who have and those who need: a loan, a donation, a wage, a favour. Which party the questioner stands for is decided by the other cards.

## Seven of Pentacles

**Image.** "A young man, leaning on his staff, looks intently at seven pentacles attached to a clump of greenery on his right; one would say that these were his treasures and that his heart was there" (Waite).

**Symbols.** The coins hang on the bush like fruit, so that the design joins wealth with growth. The staff suggests a pause in work, and Waite's remark about the heart places the young man's attention on the pentacles. Waite gives no further symbolism. Later readings take the card as a pause to assess what long effort has produced.

**Meaning.** Waite calls the meanings "exceedingly contradictory": in the main a card of money, business, barter; but one reading gives altercation, quarrels, and another innocence, ingenuity, purgation.

**Reversed.** Cause for anxiety regarding money which it may be proposed to lend.

**In a reading.** The card names a pause between effort and result, with the question whether the yield justifies the labour. Waite's contradictory list shows that older readers did not agree on it; the pictured pause is the safest ground.

## Eight of Pentacles

**Image.** "An artist in stone at his work, which he exhibits in the form of trophies" (Waite). Smith shows a craftsman at a bench striking a pentacle with hammer and chisel, with six finished coins displayed on a post and a seventh at his feet; a town stands in the distance.

**Symbols.** The row of finished pentacles is Waite's "trophies". Waite's note on the Three of Pentacles describes the figure here as an apprentice or amateur who is preparing for the work of the Three. The distant town is Smith's setting, without comment from Waite.

**Meaning.** Work, employment, commission, craftsmanship, skill in craft and business, perhaps in the preparatory stage.

**Reversed.** Voided ambition, vanity, cupidity, exaction, usury. It may also signify the possession of skill, in the sense of the ingenious mind turned to cunning and intrigue.

**In a reading.** The card names diligent practice and the building of skill through repetition. It concerns work still in progress, valued for the craft itself.

## Nine of Pentacles

**Image.** "A woman, with a bird upon her wrist, stands amidst a great abundance of grapevines in the garden of a manorial house. It is a wide domain, suggesting plenty in all things. Possibly it is her own possession and testifies to material well-being" (Waite). The bird is a hooded falcon.

**Symbols.** Waite names the bird, the vines and the domain. The hood on the falcon and the snail on the ground are not in his text; they come from Smith's drawing. Later readings take the hooded bird as a trained and controlled instinct and the snail as slow progress.

**Meaning.** Prudence, safety, success, accomplishment, certitude, discernment.

**Reversed.** Roguery, deception, voided project, bad faith.

**In a reading.** The card names self-sufficiency earned over time: a comfortable home, independent means, and the discipline that produced them. The figure is alone, and later readers connect the card with solitude as well as comfort.

## Ten of Pentacles

**Image.** "A man and woman beneath an archway which gives entrance to a house and domain. They are accompanied by a child, who looks curiously at two dogs accosting an ancient personage seated in the foreground. The child's hand is on one of them" (Waite).

**Symbols.** The pentacles are arranged over the scene in the pattern of the Tree of Life, a detail that Waite does not mention in the picture description. The old man, the couple and the child cover three generations, and the arch and house stand for the family's holding. Waite offers no further reading of the dogs. Later readings take the design as a picture of inheritance and continuity.

**Meaning.** Gain, riches; family matters, archives, extraction, the abode of a family.

**Reversed.** Chance, fatality, loss, robbery, games of hazard; sometimes gift, dowry, pension.

**In a reading.** The card names wealth held across generations: property, family business, a settled household. It is read for long-term security and the obligations that come with it.

## Page of Pentacles

**Image.** "A youthful figure, looking intently at the pentacle which hovers over his raised hands. He moves slowly, insensible of that which is about him" (Waite). Smith sets him in a green field with a ploughed strip and a tree in blossom.

**Symbols.** The hovering pentacle is the subject of his attention. Waite's phrase about his being "insensible of that which is about him" describes concentration. The field and trees are Smith's setting, without Waite's comment. Later readings take them as promise that the study will bear fruit.

**Meaning.** Application, study, scholarship, reflection; another reading says news, messages and the bringer thereof; also rule, management.

**Reversed.** Prodigality, dissipation, liberality, luxury; unfavourable news.

**In a reading.** The card names a student or beginner in practical matters, and the patient study of a subject or a skill. It may also stand for a message about money or work.

## Knight of Pentacles

**Image.** "He rides a slow, enduring, heavy horse, to which his own aspect corresponds. He exhibits his symbol, but does not look therein" (Waite). Smith shows the knight in dark armour on a stationary black horse, before a ploughed field.

**Symbols.** The heavy horse matches the rider's temperament, as Waite says. The knight holds the pentacle but does not look at it, in contrast to the Page, who attends to nothing else. The ploughed field and the oak leaves on the helmet and the horse's bridle are Smith's details; Waite does not interpret them. Later readings take the field as a sign of patient labour.

**Meaning.** Utility, serviceableness, interest, responsibility, rectitude, all on the normal and external plane.

**Reversed.** Inertia, idleness, repose of that kind, stagnation; also placidity, discouragement, carelessness.

**In a reading.** The card names reliability and steady progress without display. It is read for a person or an approach that finishes what it begins, slowly. Reversed, the same steadiness turns to inertia.

## Queen of Pentacles

**Image.** "The face suggests that of a dark woman, whose qualities might be summed up in the idea of greatness of soul; she has also the serious cast of intelligence; she contemplates her symbol and may see worlds therein" (Waite). Smith seats her on a throne in a garden, with a rabbit at her feet.

**Symbols.** The pentacle in her lap is the object of her contemplation. Waite does not describe the surroundings. The flowering garden, the roses and the rabbit are Smith's additions, and Waite does not interpret them. Later readings take them as abundance, fertility and domestic comfort. The goat's head carved on the throne is likewise Smith's, though the King's throne carries the bull's head that Waite notes.

**Meaning.** Opulence, generosity, magnificence, security, liberty.

**Reversed.** Evil, suspicion, suspense, fear, mistrust.

**In a reading.** The card names practical generosity: a person or an attitude that is at home with resources and uses them to look after others. It is read for security, hospitality and comfort.

## King of Pentacles

**Image.** Waite says that "the figure calls for no special description; the face is rather dark, suggesting also courage, but somewhat lethargic in tendency." Smith shows him seated on a throne decorated with bulls' heads, in a robe embroidered with grapes, with a castle at his side and a garden of vines.

**Symbols.** "The bull's head should be noted as a recurrent symbol on the throne." Waite says the suit's sign is "represented throughout as engraved or blazoned with the pentagram, typifying the correspondence of the four elements in human nature and that by which they may be governed." He adds that old packs showed coins, money or deniers, and that he did not invent the change to pentacles; the divinatory meanings do not mainly concern money.

**Meaning.** Valour, realizing intelligence, business and normal intellectual aptitude, sometimes mathematical gifts and attainments of this kind; success in these paths.

**Reversed.** Vice, weakness, ugliness, perversity, corruption, peril.

**In a reading.** The card names mastery of practical affairs: business sense, command of resources, and the capacity to make a venture succeed. It is read for the established, successful person, and for the quality of steadiness that goes with success.
