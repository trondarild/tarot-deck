# Pessimistic philosophy deck: research and proposed structure

This note covers the fourth item in `todo.md`. It sets out the structures in the pessimist tradition that a deck could draw on, then proposes a card list for the major series and a suit structure. The symbolism and illustration files will build on the card list, so that list is the part to settle first.

Source gathering was delegated to a research agent with limited web access: it worked mostly from search summaries, Wikipedia, the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* and *Philosophy Now*, and read no primary text in full. Points marked "unverified" need a check against an edition before they are quoted.

## Design premise

The Zen deck could take its major series from one staged text, the ox-herding pictures. The pessimist tradition has no single text of that kind. It has several short sequences (Hartmann's three illusions, Schopenhauer's path from affirmation to denial of the will), a fourfold scheme (Zapffe's mechanisms), and a stock of recurring images. The proposal below builds the major series as an argument in three movements, each card taken from a named author and text, and takes the suits from Zapffe.

Joshua Foa Dienstag's *Pessimism: Philosophy, Ethic, Spirit* (Princeton, 2006) treats pessimism as a tradition with a positive ethic, not a mood of despair, and links it to the shift from cyclical to linear time, with pessimism as the counterpart of the idea of progress. That framing suits a deck meant to be read: the cards describe a view of the world and the dispositions it recommends.

## Candidate structures

### Sequences

- **Hartmann's three stages of illusion.** Eduard von Hartmann, *Philosophie des Unbewussten* (1869). Happiness is sought first in this life, then in a transcendent afterlife, then in the future of the world-process, that is, in progress. Each illusion is exhausted in turn. Hartmann paired the stages with antiquity, the Middle Ages and modernity, and with childhood, youth and adulthood. He rejected individual suicide and proposed instead that an enlightened humanity might collectively will the end of the world-process. Britannica describes a separate triad about the Unconscious itself; the two should not be conflated.
- **Schopenhauer's path to denial of the will.** *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung* (1818, dated 1819; enlarged 1844). Book IV moves from affirmation of the will and egoism, through justice and compassion (*Mitleid*), to asceticism and the denial of the will (§68). Elsewhere in the same work: the pendulum between pain and boredom (§57) and Ixion's wheel, stilled in aesthetic contemplation (§38). Section numbers are from memory and unverified.
- **Mainländer's *Philosophie der Erlösung*** (vol. I 1876; vol. II 1886). A primal unity, God, shattered into the world; the world is the process of that God's dying, and a will to death underlies the will to live. No staged ethical sequence could be confirmed. Mainländer hanged himself in 1876, shortly after the first volume appeared.
- **Ecclesiastes.** Probably composed between about 450 and 180 BCE. Its motifs are *hevel* (vapour, breath; rendered "vanity"), "under the sun", the question of what profit a man has from his toil (1:3), and the paired times of chapter 3. The Vulgate's *vanitas vanitatum* (1:2) gave the vanitas genre its name.
- **The wisdom of Silenus.** Captured by Midas, Silenus says it is best never to be born, and second best to die soon. Sources: Aristotle's *Eudemus*, preserved in Plutarch; Theognis 425–428; Sophocles, *Oedipus at Colonus* 1224–1227; Nietzsche, *The Birth of Tragedy* §3 (1872).

### Leopardi's *Operette morali*

Leopardi (1798–1837) wrote 24 prose dialogues and essays between 1824 and 1832; the order varied between the 1827, 1834, 1835 and 1845 editions. The "Dialogue between Nature and an Icelander" (1824) is the best-known: an Icelander who has fled Nature across the world meets her as a giantess seated against a mountain in inner Africa, and she tells him the world was not made for humans. Critics read it as Leopardi's turn from historical to cosmic pessimism. His last major poem, *La ginestra* (1836), takes the broom flower growing on the lava of Vesuvius as an emblem of dignity before an indifferent nature, and calls for human solidarity against it.

### Zapffe's four mechanisms

Peter Wessel Zapffe (1899–1990), "Den sidste Messias" (*Janus*, 1933), a short version of *Om det tragiske* (1941). Human consciousness exceeds what survival requires, like antlers grown too heavy for the deer that carries them, and people survive by damping it. Zapffe names four mechanisms:

1. **Isolation:** dismissing disturbing thought and feeling from consciousness.
2. **Anchoring:** fixing points in, or building walls around, the flow of consciousness (God, nation, family, career).
3. **Distraction:** holding attention within safe bounds by a constant supply of impressions.
4. **Sublimation:** turning the pain into something else, chiefly art. Zapffe treats it as the rarest, and has been read as counting his own essay as an instance.

The essay ends with the last Messiah, who tells humanity to be infertile and let the earth be silent.

### Other sources

- **Bahnsen's real dialectic.** Contradiction is basic to reality and never resolves; acting on one value violates another. Humour is the response.
- **Cioran:** insomnia, lucidity, the "inconvenience" of being born (*De l'inconvénient d'être né*, 1973).
- **Pessoa / Bernardo Soares,** *The Book of Disquiet:* the clerk's tedium as a form of inner life.
- **Benatar,** *Better Never to Have Been* (2006): the asymmetry between the absence of pain (good even if no one enjoys it) and the absence of pleasure (bad only if someone is deprived).
- **Ligotti,** *The Conspiracy against the Human Race* (2010): Zapffe's mechanisms as a general evasion. **Thacker:** the world-without-us.
- **Camus,** *Le Mythe de Sisyphe* (1942): Sisyphus as the absurd hero. Camus argues against pessimist conclusions, which makes him useful as a response card.
- **Older parallels:** Hegesias of Cyrene (fl. c. 290 BCE), whose lectures were reportedly banned because listeners killed themselves; Job's curse on the day of his birth (3:3); Schopenhauer's reading of the Upanishads and Buddhism.

### Recurring images

The pendulum, Ixion's wheel, Sisyphus's stone, Schopenhauer's porcupines (*Parerga* II §396, 1851), the giant deer, the archer by the waterhole from Zapffe's opening parable, the skull and hourglass of vanitas, the Icelander and the giantess, the broom on the lava, the shattered God, the unborn.

## Existing decks and art traditions

The search found no pessimism- or nihilism-themed tarot or oracle deck. The search was shallow, so this should be checked before the deck is described as original. The art traditions that pair with the subject are vanitas still life (Dutch, c. 1585–1730), the danse macabre (Holbein's woodcuts, 1538), Goya's *Caprichos* and Black Paintings, Max Klinger's etching cycles, Alfred Kubin, and Odilon Redon's *Noirs*.

## Proposal

### Major series: 16 cards in three movements

**0 — The Unborn.** Unnumbered. The wisdom of Silenus, with Theognis and Benatar: the state before existence, judged the best. Like Mu in the Zen deck it stands outside the sequence, and the last card leads back to it.

**Awakening and disillusion (I–IV).**

| # | Card | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | The Giant Deer | Zapffe, "The Last Messiah": consciousness grown beyond need |
| II | The First Illusion: Happiness Here | Hartmann, stage one |
| III | The Second Illusion: Happiness Hereafter | Hartmann, stage two |
| IV | The Third Illusion: Progress | Hartmann, stage three |

**The world as it is (V–X).**

| # | Card | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V | Vanity | Ecclesiastes 1–3: *hevel*, labour under the sun |
| VI | The Pendulum | Schopenhauer, WWR I §57: pain and boredom |
| VII | Nature and the Icelander | Leopardi, *Operette morali* (1824): an indifferent nature |
| VIII | The Porcupines | Schopenhauer, *Parerga* II §396: the right distance between people |
| IX | The Shattered God | Mainländer, *Philosophie der Erlösung* (1876) |
| X | The Insomniac | Cioran: lucidity without rest |

**Responses (XI–XV).**

| # | Card | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| XI | The Stone | Camus, *Le Mythe de Sisyphe* (1942): revolt |
| XII | Compassion | Schopenhauer, WWR IV and *On the Basis of Morality* (1840) |
| XIII | The Broom | Leopardi, *La ginestra* (1836): solidarity |
| XIV | The Stilled Wheel | Schopenhauer, WWR §§38, 68: contemplation and denial of the will |
| XV | The Last Messiah | Zapffe (1933): the call to let the earth be silent |

The order reads as one argument. Consciousness outgrows its use (I), each place where happiness was sought fails (II–IV), the world is described without consolation (V–X), and five responses follow, from defiance to the call for extinction. XV leads back to 0. The responses are deliberately plural: the tradition does not agree on what follows from pessimism, and the deck should not choose for it.

### Minor series: four suits from Zapffe

- **Suits:** Isolation, Anchoring, Distraction, Sublimation.
- **Pips:** 1–10 per suit, showing the mechanism at increasing strength or cost.
- **Court cards:** The four ages of life, set out in the subsection below.

### Minor series: how it supports the major cards (proposal, 2026-10-10)

- **Pips as what is evaded:** Zapffe's four mechanisms are the everyday ways people keep the insight of the major cards at bay. The suit names the mechanism, and pip n names the truth of major card n that it holds off. Anchoring 3 is anchoring in faith in an afterlife (the second illusion, card III). Distraction 6 is keeping busy so as not to feel the pendulum (card VI). Isolation 7 is not thinking about an indifferent nature (card VII). This mirrors the Zen deck's scheme in reverse: there the minor cards are the path lived in each domain, and here they are the path avoided in each mechanism.
- **Court cards as the four ages of life:** Schopenhauer wrote on the periods of life in the *Aphorisms on the Wisdom of Life* (*Parerga and Paralipomena* I), and Hartmann tied his illusions to childhood, youth and adulthood. Page is childhood, Knight is youth, Queen is adulthood, and King is old age. Each court shows how its mechanism looks at that age. This keeps the four-court structure of the tarot and adds the old age that Hartmann's three illusions leave out.
- **Sublimation as the bridge:** Zapffe treats sublimation as the rarest mechanism and the only productive one. Its suit can lead toward the response cards (XI–XV), especially XIII The Broom and XIV The Stilled Wheel. The other three suits stay with evasion.
- **Suit colours from the deck's palette:** Isolation is slate (cold, distance). Anchoring is caput mortuum (blood, kin, nation). Distraction is bone (the candlelight of vanitas pleasures). Sublimation is verdigris (the patina of time).
- **Count:** 4 suits of 10 pips and 4 courts give 56 minor cards and 72 cards in all.
- **Cards without a pip counterpart:** Card 0 and the responses (XI–XV) stand outside the numbered run. They are the question and its answers, while the minor cards are situations.

### Handling of suicide

Several sources concern suicide: Mainländer's death, Hegesias, Hartmann's and Schopenhauer's arguments against it, and Cioran's view of it as a consolation. The cards should present the ideas without depicting suicide or treating it as a recommendation. Mainländer's card takes its image from his metaphysics, the shattered God, not from his death.

### Decisions (2026-10-10)

1. The 16-card list stands, with Camus among the responses.
2. The minor series is deferred; symbolism and illustration work covers the major cards only.
3. The illustrations use etching and aquatint after Goya's *Caprichos* and Klinger, hand-tinted in a four-colour watercolour palette. The palette below was approved on the same date.

### Proposed palette

Hand-coloured etching has a long history (Rowlandson, Blake, early botanical plates): the etched line carries the drawing and thin watercolour washes are laid over it. The line here is warm black ink on off-white laid paper, which is not counted in the palette. The four washes are muted earth pigments, each with an association in the tradition:

| Colour | Pigment | Approx. hex | Association |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bone | yellow ochre, diluted | `#D8C59A` | skulls and candlelight in vanitas still life; Leopardi's broom flower |
| Slate | Payne's grey | `#5E6B78` | sky, sea and stone; Leopardi's indifferent Nature |
| Caput mortuum | iron-oxide violet-brown | `#5A2E2E` | the pigment's name means "death's head"; blood and dried flowers |
| Verdigris | copper green, greyed | `#6F8C7A` | corrosion, moss and decay; the slow work of time |

Each card uses one colour as its dominant wash, and at most one other in a small area. Most of the paper stays white or carries only the grey of the aquatint. If the minor series is built later, the four colours could mark Zapffe's four suits.
