# Zen deck: symbolism of the major cards

The major series has eighteen cards in two strands, laid out in the proposal section of `zen-buddhism-research.md`. The Way (I–X) follows Kuoan Shiyuan's ten ox-herding pictures, which describe one practitioner's path from restless seeking to a return among ordinary people. Card X leads back to card I, so the strand is read as a cycle in which each return finds the seeker at a different depth. The Transmission (XI–XVII) follows how the teaching passes from person to person, using the lineage legends of Chan and Zen from Mahākāśyapa to Linji. Where the stories are later inventions, the entry says so once and then treats them as the tradition does, as teaching stories whose value does not depend on the event.

Card 0, Mu, has no number. It stands before both strands, like the Fool, as the question that opens the gate and is never closed by an answer. The other cards can be read as stages on either side of it. Each entry below gives the source, the scene, the symbols, the teaching, a reading, a characteristic pitfall (which replaces a reversed meaning) and links to other cards. Translations of Chinese and Japanese terms are the author's unless attributed.

## 0 — Mu (無)

**Source.** *Mumonkan* (*Wumenguan*, The Gateless Gate), case 1, compiled by Wumen Huikai and published in 1228. The exchange belongs to the Tang master Zhaozhou Congshen (Jp. Jōshū), and is recorded in earlier collections. Wumen's commentary and verse are his own.

**Scene.** A monk asks Zhaozhou whether a dog has Buddha-nature. Zhaozhou answers "Mu", meaning no, or nothing. Orthodox doctrine holds that all beings have Buddha-nature, so the answer contradicts the teaching the monk expected. Wumen's commentary tells the student to pass the barrier with the whole body and mind, to avoid reading Mu as nothingness or as a yes-or-no, and to carry it like a hot iron ball that cannot be swallowed or spat out.

**Symbols.** The gate is the barrier of the patriarchs, and it has no gate in the sense of a door: the only way through is the question itself. The dog is an ordinary creature that the monk's doctrine has already classified. The syllable Mu is a negation with nothing behind it, and it is used as the object of sustained attention in Rinzai practice. The card is unnumbered because it belongs to no sequence.

**Teaching.** Mu is the head phrase (*wato*, 話頭) on which attention is gathered until the "great doubt" (*daigi*, 大疑) forms. The tradition treats the first koan as the entrance to the whole curriculum. The case turns on *shūjō* (衆生) and *busshō* (佛性), sentient beings and Buddha-nature, and on the habit of settling such questions by concept.

**In a reading.** A question that cannot be answered in the terms it was asked. The card marks a beginning before any plan exists and invites sustained attention to the question instead of a quick resolution. It can also name a refusal to be classified.

**Pitfall.** Treating Mu as a technique for producing a state, or as a clever denial. Wumen warns against both the nihilist reading (a void) and the reading that sets being against non-being. The opposite error is to collect the answer as a piece of knowledge.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the seeker who has not yet heard the question as his own. VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: Mu is where the emptiness of that card is first met. XV — Ordinary Mind: Zhaozhou carries the same plain manner into his own answer to Nanquan.

## I — Searching for the Ox (尋牛)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan (Jp. Kakuan), *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 1; 12th century, Song China. The paintings most often reproduced are attributed to Tenshō Shūbun (1414–1463). Kuoan wrote the prose preface and verse for each picture.

**Scene.** A herdsman goes alone into wilderness, through grass, rivers and mountain paths, looking for an ox he has lost. Kuoan's preface says the ox was never lost: the man has estranged himself from his own nature, and desire and fear unsettle him. In the verse he is exhausted, with a long road ahead, and hears only cicadas in the maples. The picture shows no ox.

**Symbols.** The ox stands for true nature or mind, the herdsman for the practitioner. The wilderness is the world of pursuit, and the missing ox is the thing sought. Its absence from the picture is the point: nothing in the scene yet shows what is being looked for. The cicadas mark late summer and a noise that goes on regardless.

**Teaching.** The doctrine is that Buddha-nature (*busshō*) is present from the start, so the search starts from a false premise that is nevertheless the only way in. The Chinese title of the stage, *xún niú*, means seeking the ox. The restlessness is a form of *kleśa*, affliction, which Kuoan names as desire and fear.

**In a reading.** A sense of lack with no object, or a search whose object cannot yet be named. The card does not predict success. It names the disposition from which a path begins, and it asks what is being sought and whether it is elsewhere.

**Pitfall.** Equating the search with the result: more seeking, more teachers, more books, with each new method a way of staying lost. Another is to take the preface's reassurance as a reason not to seek at all.

**Links.** 0 — Mu: the question that sends the seeker out. II — Seeing the Traces: the next stage, in which texts give the first signs. X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the cycle returns here, and the figure who returns can search without the old distress.

## II — Seeing the Traces (見跡)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 2; 12th century.

**Scene.** The herdsman finds hoofprints beside a stream and under the trees. In the preface, scripture and teaching have shown him the traces: he sees that all things share one substance and that the world reflects the self, but he cannot yet tell right from wrong. The verse says the animal may have wandered far, yet its scent rises to the sky and cannot be hidden.

**Symbols.** The footprints are the teachings, sutras and the words of teachers, which point to the ox without being the ox. The stream and trees are the same landscape as in the first picture, now carrying evidence. The herdsman bends toward the ground and looks at marks, which is the posture of study.

**Teaching.** The stage corresponds to learning and doctrine, *kyōgaku* (教學) in Japanese usage, and to the first understanding of non-duality at the level of concept. Zen holds that this understanding is real but insufficient. The preface's admission that the seeker cannot yet tell right from wrong marks the gap between knowing a doctrine and acting from it.

**In a reading.** Early evidence that something is there: a book, a teacher, a remark that rings true. The card suggests following the evidence while remembering that signs are not the thing they indicate. It can name a period of study.

**Pitfall.** Mistaking the traces for the ox: collecting doctrine and becoming learned without a first-hand experience. Zen's slogan of a transmission "not founded on words and letters" targets this stage.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the same seeker, now with something to follow. III — Seeing the Ox: the traces lead to a direct sight. XI — The Flower and the Smile: the tradition's own account of a teaching that goes beyond scripture.

## III — Seeing the Ox (見牛)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 3; 12th century.

**Scene.** The herdsman sees the ox, standing alone in a spring landscape. A nightingale sings on a branch, the willows are green and the sun is warm, and the ox has nowhere to hide. The preface says he now hears the way, the senses are in harmony, and the ox's presence pervades everything like salt dissolved in water. When he looks rightly, he finds it is himself.

**Symbols.** The ox appears for the first time, often partly hidden, as in a first glimpse. The birdsong shows the sense of hearing opened, and the willows and sun show a world made vivid. Salt in water is the preface's image for something present everywhere and visible nowhere as a separate object.

**Teaching.** This is the first *kenshō* (見性), literally seeing into one's nature, in the Japanese tradition's terms. The preface places the entry through the senses and states the identity of seer and seen. Rinzai teaching treats kenshō as a beginning that needs long cultivation.

**In a reading.** A sudden clarity: a moment when something becomes obvious. The card says the glimpse is real and gives no guarantee that it will last. It invites the reader to keep what was seen without building a story around it.

**Pitfall.** Taking the first glimpse for the whole of the path, announcing it, or ranking oneself by it. Zen teachers speak of "stinking of Zen" (*zen kusai*, 禅臭い), the air that clings to those who display their attainment.

**Links.** II — Seeing the Traces: the study that preceded the sight. IV — Catching the Ox: the glimpse has to be taken hold of. VII — The Ox Forgotten: the ox seen here is the one that later disappears.

## IV — Catching the Ox (得牛)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 4; 12th century.

**Scene.** The herdsman has the ox, but it is wild and resists, longing for its old pasture. In the verse he has seized it with all his strength and its will is fierce; it struts up the plateau, then vanishes into a misty pass. The preface says full mastery needs a firm use of the whip.

**Symbols.** The rope appears in hand for the first time, and the preface calls for the whip. The rope is attention, the whip is effort and discipline, and the ox's resistance is the habit-energy of the old mind. The mist in the pass shows how quickly the experience of the third picture can slip away.

**Teaching.** The stage describes the work of stabilising an insight against habitual patterns: *karma* and the traces of past conditioning (Skt. *vāsanā*). In Rinzai practice it corresponds to the long koan work after the first kenshō. The preface treats effort here as necessary, not as a sign of error.

**In a reading.** A period of struggle to hold on to something seen: a decision, a resolution, a new way of working. The card asks for steady effort and some force, and it warns that the old habits return quickly.

**Pitfall.** Holding too hard. The force that catches the ox can harden into a fixation that prevents its calming. The opposite error is giving up when the insight proves unstable.

**Links.** III — Seeing the Ox: the sight that must now be held. V — Herding the Ox: the struggle softens into routine. XVI — The Wild Fox: the consequences of mistaken freedom from cause and effect.

## V — Herding the Ox (牧牛)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 5; 12th century.

**Scene.** The herdsman leads the ox with a nose-string and whip, and the ox walks beside him. The preface says that one thought arises after another in an unending chain; clarity makes them true and confusion makes them false. The verse warns that if he slackens whip and tether, the ox will wander into the dust. Tended properly, it becomes docile and follows without a chain.

**Symbols.** The nose-string is mindfulness, and the whip is the correction that follows a lapse. The ox walks on, still tethered, and the grass is the ordinary world of sense objects. The change from the fourth picture is that the ox now cooperates.

**Teaching.** The picture shows *shugyō* (修行), practice after awakening, and the Zen teaching that realisation must be matured. The preface's remark about the chain of thought refers to the arising of mental activity (*nen*, 念) and to the work of discernment. Dōgen's idea that practice and realisation are one is relevant to the later stages.

**In a reading.** Disciplined, steady, unglamorous work. The card names a stretch in which progress comes from regularity, and it favours routine over breakthrough.

**Pitfall.** Taking discipline as the goal: formal rigidity, the "stinking" posture of one who guards his purity. The preface's own condition for release is that the tether eventually becomes unnecessary.

**Links.** IV — Catching the Ox: the same animal, now broken to the lead. VI — Riding the Ox Home: the tether is loosened. XV — Ordinary Mind: the later form of discipline that no longer looks like discipline.

## VI — Riding the Ox Home (騎牛歸家)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 6; 12th century. The Chinese title is literally "riding the ox, returning home."

**Scene.** The struggle is over and the herdsman sits on the ox's back, playing a flute on the way home through the evening mist. The preface says gain and loss no longer concern him, and he hums rustic tunes and ignores calls to turn back. The verse describes a joy that cannot be put into words.

**Symbols.** The flute and the ride are ease, and the ox is now a mount instead of an opponent. The evening mist is the dusk of effort, and home is the original nature. The herdsman's eyes are fixed ahead. In some paintings the whip lies unused.

**Teaching.** The stage describes harmony between practitioner and mind, with the dualism of striver and struggled-against beginning to dissolve. The preface's indifference to gain and loss corresponds to the Buddhist ideal of equanimity (*upekṣā*).

**In a reading.** Ease after effort, with practice and life in agreement. The card suggests the work has been absorbed and invites the reader to enjoy it without watching it.

**Pitfall.** Contentment, the attachment to a pleasant state of mind. The herdsman is still riding, and the ox is still there: the dualism remains, and the music can become the new object of desire.

**Links.** V — Herding the Ox: the discipline that made this ease possible. VII — The Ox Forgotten: the animal is dropped once home is reached. X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the same lightness, extended to others.

## VII — The Ox Forgotten, Leaving the Man Alone (忘牛存人)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 7; 12th century.

**Scene.** The herdsman has reached home and the ox is gone. He sits at ease in the thatched hut, and the whip and rope lie idle. The preface says the ox was a symbol: once the hare is caught, the snare is forgotten, and gold is separated from dross like the moon coming out of clouds. In the verse, he sleeps late into the day.

**Symbols.** The empty field and the idle tools show that the means is no longer needed. The snare and the hare come from the *Zhuangzi*, which Kuoan's preface echoes. The rising moon stands for clarity coming out of obscuring cloud. The herdsman remains, which is the stage's limit.

**Teaching.** The doctrine is *upāya* (方便, *hōben*), skilful means, and the principle that teachings are a raft to be left on the shore. The means is dropped but the self who used it persists, so the stage still contains a subject. The problem of the self remains.

**In a reading.** The tool has done its work and can be put down: a method, a role, a belief. The card suggests that a long effort has ended in a plain satisfaction and invites asking what remains once the scaffolding goes.

**Pitfall.** Pride in having let go. A person who has dropped the ox may rest on that and refuse the next step, where the person also drops.

**Links.** VI — Riding the Ox Home: the animal that has just been left behind. VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight: the self follows the ox. XIII — Pacifying the Mind: the mind sought and not found.

## VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone Out of Sight (人牛俱忘)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 8; 12th century. The picture is an empty circle (*ensō*, 圓相). The earlier Puming series ends at an empty circle, whereas Kuoan continues past it.

**Scene.** Whip, rope, ox and man have all disappeared. The preface says confusion has cleared and only serenity remains, without even the idea of holiness; where there is no duality, not even a thousand eyes could find a gap, and a holiness that birds would honour with flowers is an embarrassment. In the verse, no one can survey the vastness of heaven, and a snowflake cannot fall on a blazing furnace.

**Symbols.** In Kuoan's picture the circle is a blank; this deck shows it as an empty clearing, with the ground and a few trees and no one in it. It stands for emptiness (*śūnyatā*, *kū*, 空), wholeness and the completion of a movement, and in Zen painting the brush circle is a signature of the artist's state at the moment of drawing. The furnace and snowflake show a mind in which nothing lodges.

**Teaching.** The doctrine is the dropping of subject and object together (*jingyū gubō*), the emptiness of both self and means. The Heart Sutra's "form is emptiness" belongs to this stage. Dongshan's Five Ranks give a parallel in the first rank, the relative within the absolute, where particulars are absorbed into emptiness.

**In a reading.** A clearing: a phase in which roles, goals and the self that held them lose their grip. The card can mean a real relief, a pause before a new form, or a state with no content that is not yet a state of understanding.

**Pitfall.** Attachment to emptiness. Hakuin, among other teachers, warned against the "cave of the black mountain" (*kokuzan kikutsu*, 黒山鬼窟), the dead quiet in which a practitioner sits as if awakened, with nothing in his life changed. The picture is the limit of the Puming series, and Kuoan's continuation exists to prevent a stop here.

**Links.** VII — The Ox Forgotten: the step before, where the self still remains. IX — Returning to the Source: the world reappears. 0 — Mu: the blank that opened the whole sequence.

## IX — Returning to the Source (返本還源)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 9; 12th century.

**Scene.** The herdsman sits in his hut, or is absent, while water flows and flowers bloom. In the preface he has been pure from the beginning and untouched by defilement; he watches things grow and change from a stillness that does not move, and he does not identify with their illusory transformations. The verse says that returning to the origin is itself a false step, and that it would be better to stay at home; the streams flow of their own accord, and the flowers are red, for no one in particular.

**Symbols.** The river and the flowers represent the phenomenal world, seen with nothing added. Green mountains and blue water appear in the preface as things as they are. Some versions of the picture show only landscape, with no person.

**Teaching.** The stage corresponds to the saying, attributed to Qingyuan Weixin, that before practice mountains are mountains, during practice they are not, and after realisation they are again. The Chinese terms for source and origin (*ben*, *yuan*) refer to the original ground, and the doctrine in play is the second half of the Heart Sutra's formula, "emptiness is form" (*kū soku ze shiki*, 空即是色), together with the Mahāyāna principle that *saṃsāra* and *nirvāṇa* are not two.

**In a reading.** Seeing the situation as it is, without commentary: clear perception after a period of confusion. The card suggests that nothing needs adding and invites the reader to look at what is already present.

**Pitfall.** Quietism: remaining in the hut and letting the world run on without taking part. The verse's own irony, that "returning" is already a step too many, warns against making a project of this stage as well.

**Links.** VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone: the emptiness out of which the world re-emerges. X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: the clear seer goes out. XV — Ordinary Mind: the same recovery of the plain world, in a master's words.

## X — Entering the Market with Open Hands (入鄽垂手)

**Source.** Kuoan Shiyuan, *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*, picture 10; 12th century. The figure is conventionally read as Budai (Jp. Hotei), the laughing monk of 10th-century China, though Kuoan's text does not name him.

**Scene.** The herdsman, barefoot and bare-chested, goes into the marketplace carrying a gourd and leaning on a staff. The preface says his gate is closed and even the wise cannot see into him; he mingles with drunkards and butchers, and they become Buddhas. In the verse his face and chest are smeared with mud and ashes, he smiles broadly, and he makes dead trees bloom without using supernatural powers.

**Symbols.** The market is the ordinary world of trade and appetite. The gourd and staff are the travelling monk's equipment, and the bare feet show that he has no rank. The open hands (*suishu*, 垂手, literally hands hanging down) give without grasping. Budai's sack and belly come from his legend.

**Teaching.** The stage corresponds to the bodhisattva vow to work for the awakening of all beings, and to *upāya*. The idea is that realisation shows in conduct among others, with no outward sign of distinction. The tradition calls the work after awakening *gogo no shugyō* (悟後の修行).

**In a reading.** Returning to ordinary life with whatever has been learned, and being useful there without announcing it. The card may point to teaching, caring for others, or joining a community. It also marks the close of a cycle, and the next card is the first.

**Pitfall.** A show of humility, or a missionary stance that sets the helper above the helped. The preface says the figure's goodness is invisible, which excludes the performance of it.

**Links.** I — Searching for the Ox: the cycle returns, and the seeker begins again. IX — Returning to the Source: the clear sight that makes this possible. XVII — The Shout: another form of acting for others, much less gentle.

## XI — The Flower and the Smile (拈花微笑)

**Source.** *Mumonkan* case 6, Wumen Huikai, 1228. The Flower Sermon is a legend. It is first attested in 1036, in a Chinese Chan text, and no Indian source contains it. Scholars treat it as a later Chan invention that grounds the claim of a transmission outside the scriptures in the Buddha himself.

**Scene.** On Vulture Peak, the Buddha holds up a flower to his assembly and says nothing. The monks are puzzled, and only Mahākāśyapa smiles. The Buddha then says that he holds the true dharma eye, the wondrous mind of nirvana, and entrusts it to Mahākāśyapa. Wumen's commentary jokes that Gautama passed dog's meat off as mutton, and asks what would have happened to the transmission if everyone had smiled, or no one.

**Symbols.** The flower is the teaching in its simplest form, with no argument or text. The smile is recognition without words. The assembly's silence is the audience that failed to see. Mahākāśyapa, the disciple foremost in ascetic practice, is counted as the first patriarch of the lineage.

**Teaching.** The case grounds the Chan formula of "a special transmission outside the scriptures" (教外別傳, *kyōge betsuden*) and the idea of mind-to-mind transmission (*ishin denshin*, 以心傳心). The doctrinal point is that the dharma is not stored in the words of the sutras.

**In a reading.** A recognition that passes between two people without explanation, or the beginning of a line of inheritance. The card can also name the experience of being the only one in a room to understand, and what follows from it.

**Pitfall.** The cult of the wordless: treating silence as superior to speech, or using claims of inexpressible understanding to avoid examination. Wumen's joke addresses this, and the later Chan literature is full of words.

**Links.** II — Seeing the Traces: scripture as traces, set against transmission outside it. XII — Facing the Wall: the legend of the next patriarch in the line. XV — Ordinary Mind: the later form of mind-to-mind transmission, in everyday speech.

## XII — Facing the Wall (面壁)

**Source.** *Blue Cliff Record* case 1, with verse by Xuedou Chongxian and commentary by Yuanwu Keqin, dated 1125. The nine years of wall-gazing at Shaolin are reported in later Chan histories such as the *Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall* (952). Bodhidharma is a semi-legendary figure, and much of his biography was added after the 6th century. The four-line verse "a special transmission outside the scriptures, not founded on words and letters, pointing directly at the mind, seeing one's nature and becoming a Buddha" is attributed to him but first appears as a single verse in the *Zuting shiyuan* of 1108; the attribution is later.

**Scene.** Emperor Wu of Liang, a patron of Buddhism, asks Bodhidharma what the highest meaning of the holy truths is. The answer is that there is vast emptiness and nothing holy. Asked who it is that stands before him, Bodhidharma says he does not know. The emperor fails to understand, and Bodhidharma crosses the Yangtze and goes north to the kingdom of Wei. Zhigong tells the emperor that the man was Avalokiteśvara, and that no one could bring him back. In the legend, Bodhidharma then sits facing a wall at Shaolin for nine years.

**Symbols.** The wall is the opaque surface on which attention rests, the barrier and the support. The crossing of the river marks the departure from a patron who thought merit could be counted. The emperor is the believer who has built temples and expects credit.

**Teaching.** The case teaches the emptiness of holy things (*kakunen musshō*, 廓然無聖) and the refusal of merit as a reward. The wall-gazing is the origin of seated practice (*zazen*) in the lineage story, which later Zen retained.

**In a reading.** A refusal to explain, deliberate withdrawal, or sustained effort without an audience. The card names solitude in the service of a long task.

**Pitfall.** Withdrawal as a pose, or the stubbornness that mistakes obstinacy for depth. The emperor's error, expecting reward for good works, has a counterpart in the practitioner who expects recognition for sitting.

**Links.** XI — The Flower and the Smile: the previous link in the legendary line. XIII — Pacifying the Mind: the disciple who arrives at the wall. XIV — No Mirror, No Stand: the later patriarch who also denies the holy.

## XIII — Pacifying the Mind (安心)

**Source.** *Mumonkan* case 41, Wumen Huikai, 1228. The story of Huike's arm appears in earlier Chan histories in several variants and is generally regarded as legend.

**Scene.** Bodhidharma sits facing a wall. Huike stands in the snow, and in some versions cuts off his arm to prove his sincerity. He says that his mind is not at peace and asks Bodhidharma to pacify it. Bodhidharma says, "Bring me your mind, and I will pacify it." Huike answers that he has searched for it and cannot find it. Bodhidharma says, "Then it is already pacified." Wumen's commentary is critical of the whole performance, saying that the old foreigner caused trouble and the disciple made it worse by mutilating himself.

**Symbols.** The snow is the test of the seeker's resolve. The severed arm is the sacrifice offered, which in the commentary is excessive. The mind that cannot be found is the central image: the object of the search is not an object. Bodhidharma's turned back is the teaching that offers no reassurance.

**Teaching.** The doctrine is the emptiness of the mind as a thing (*shin fukatoku*, 心不可得), a theme from the *Diamond Sutra*: past, present and future mind cannot be grasped. Peace of mind (*anjin*, 安心) comes from looking directly at the troubled mind and finding no one there to be troubled.

**In a reading.** Anxiety brought to a teacher, a friend or oneself, and the discovery that looking at it dissolves its solid form. The card can name the moment a persistent worry loses its object.

**Pitfall.** Treating the exchange as a trick and "not finding the mind" as a technique for dispelling distress. The other error is the arm: dramatic sacrifice as a substitute for looking.

**Links.** XII — Facing the Wall: the master who does not turn around. VII — The Ox Forgotten: the mind sought and the means abandoned. XIV — No Mirror, No Stand: a later denial of a mind to be cleaned.

## XIV — No Mirror, No Stand (本來無一物)

**Source.** *Platform Sutra* of the Sixth Patriarch, verse contest between Shenxiu and Huineng. The text is attributed to Fahai, a disciple of Huineng, but its authorship is disputed: the oldest manuscripts from Dunhuang date to the 9th century and derive from an edition of about 780, the text went through later editions (1056, 1291), and some scholars see a hand from the Oxhead school. The verse contest may be a later story. In the Dunhuang version Huineng's verse differs from the later and more famous wording of "originally there is not a thing", which is the version the card title follows.

**Scene.** The Fifth Patriarch Hongren asks his monks to write a verse showing their understanding; the author of the best will inherit robe and dharma. Shenxiu, the senior monk, writes anonymously on the corridor wall that the body is a bodhi tree and the mind a bright mirror on a stand, to be wiped constantly so that no dust settles. Huineng, an illiterate lay worker in the threshing room, has the verse read to him and dictates a reply: there is no bodhi tree and no mirror stand, and since there is nothing, dust has nowhere to land. Hongren secretly approves, publicly rubs out the verse, and later transmits the robe to Huineng by night.

**Symbols.** The mirror is the mind in its clear state. The dust is delusion and the cleaning is gradual practice. The tree is the body as the site of awakening. Huineng's reply removes mirror, stand and tree. The robe is the sign of transmission, and the night handover marks a lineage in danger.

**Teaching.** The two verses are the textbook pair for the "gradual" and "sudden" approaches (*jian* and *dun*), a reading of the Southern school, though modern scholars including John McRae argue Shenxiu's verse was not understood as gradualism in its own time. The doctrinal core is emptiness (*śūnyatā*) and the lack of an inherent self (*anātman*).

**In a reading.** Dropping a project of self-improvement, or recognising that the problem imagined as dirt on a surface does not exist. It can also name a talent from an unexpected quarter.

**Pitfall.** Taking Huineng's verse as permission to give up practice and cleaning, which is the objection raised against it in later centuries. A verse proving that there is nothing to clean can itself become something to cling to.

**Links.** XIII — Pacifying the Mind: the mind that cannot be found. VIII — The Ox and the Man Both Gone: the same emptiness in a different image. XII — Facing the Wall: the lineage's earlier denial of holiness.

## XV — Ordinary Mind (平常心)

**Source.** *Mumonkan* case 19, Wumen Huikai, 1228. The exchange is between the Tang masters Nanquan Puyuan (Jp. Nansen) and his student Zhaozhou Congshen (Jp. Jōshū), before Zhaozhou became a teacher in his own right. The dialogue is a Chan record and has no particular claim to be a verbatim report.

**Scene.** Zhaozhou asks Nanquan what the Way is. Nanquan answers that ordinary mind is the Way. Zhaozhou asks whether he should direct himself toward it, and Nanquan says that if you try to direct yourself toward it, you turn away from it. Zhaozhou asks how he can know the Way without directing himself, and Nanquan says that the Way belongs neither to knowing nor to not knowing: knowing is delusion, and not knowing is blankness. If one truly reaches the Way beyond doubt, it is like vast space, open and empty, so how could there be right and wrong in it? At these words Zhaozhou is awakened. Wumen remarks that Zhaozhou still needed thirty years to digest it.

**Symbols.** The case has no props, and the card shows an ordinary setting on an unmarked day. Vast space stands for a mind without a centre from which to judge. The thirty years in Wumen's commentary mark the time that realisation takes to become habit.

**Teaching.** *Heijōshin* (平常心) is the mind of ordinary daily life, not a special state. The case rejects both the effort to produce a state and the complacency of having none. The teaching joins the Mahāyāna principle of non-duality with the Chinese preference for the everyday.

**In a reading.** Attention to what is already in front of you: an ordinary meal, a task, a day. The card recommends dropping the project of becoming special, and it may mark the end of a search that was itself the obstacle.

**Pitfall.** Using "ordinary" as an excuse for carelessness or laziness, treating any behaviour as the Way. Nanquan's refusal of both knowing and not knowing leaves no resting place for this reading either.

**Links.** 0 — Mu: the same Zhaozhou, first as the student and then as the master of the dog. IX — Returning to the Source: the world as it is, seen after emptiness. XVI — The Wild Fox: ordinary mind still stands under cause and effect.

## XVI — The Wild Fox (野狐)

**Source.** *Mumonkan* case 2, Wumen Huikai, 1228. The setting is the monastery of the Tang master Baizhang Huaihai (Jp. Hyakujō); the story is a teaching narrative with no independent corroboration.

**Scene.** An old man who attends Baizhang's lectures tells him that he is not human. In the time of a former Buddha he was the abbot of the same mountain. Asked whether an enlightened person falls under the law of cause and effect, he answered that such a person does not, and for this he has been reborn as a fox for five hundred lives. He asks Baizhang the same question, and Baizhang replies that an enlightened person does not obscure cause and effect. The old man is freed at these words and asks for a monk's funeral. Baizhang holds the funeral, and after the meal leads the monks to a cave, where he finds the body of a fox. Huangbo then asks what would have happened had the old man answered correctly each time. Baizhang tells him to come closer, Huangbo slaps him, and Baizhang laughs. Wumen's commentary asks why a wrong answer produced a fox and a right answer released one, and says that anyone with the one eye will see.

**Symbols.** The fox is the cunning mind that has slipped into a doctrine and made it a licence. The cave is the place where the former self is buried. The funeral for an animal shows that the tradition honours the practitioner whatever his form. The slap and the laugh are an exchange between teacher and student.

**Teaching.** The case concerns *inga* (因果), cause and effect, and the Zen claim that awakening does not suspend karma. The old abbot's answer was "does not fall into" cause and effect (*furaku inga*, 不落因果); Baizhang's is "does not obscure" it (*fumai inga*, 不昧因果). Translators render 昧 as "obscure", "ignore" or "be blind to".

**In a reading.** A loophole used once too often: the belief that insight, status or intention exempts one from the consequences of one's acts. The card can also name a cost that has been carried a long time and is finally being released.

**Pitfall.** The error it depicts: treating realisation as freedom from consequences. The opposite error is the fatalism that lets cause and effect crush any possibility of change.

**Links.** IV — Catching the Ox: the wildness of the unmastered mind. XIV — No Mirror, No Stand: emptiness stated without the reminder this card supplies. XV — Ordinary Mind: the ordinary life in which cause and effect operate.

## XVII — The Shout (喝)

**Source.** *Linji lu* (*Record of Linji*), the passage known as the Four Shouts; Linji Yixuan (Jp. Rinzai, died 866). The standard text was compiled by Yuanjue Zongyan and printed in 1120, about 250 years after Linji's death, and Albert Welter has argued that later editors shaped it, so the passage is the tradition's record and not a transcript. Blue Cliff Record case 10 (Muzhou and a shouting monk, 1125) is a related case about an imitated shout.

**Scene.** In Burton Watson's translation, Linji asks a monk whether he understands the shout. At times, he says, it is like the precious sword of the Diamond King, at times like a golden-haired lion crouching on the ground, at times like the search pole and the shadow grass, and at times it does not work like a shout at all. When the monk hesitates to answer, Linji shouts. In the Blue Cliff case the old master Muzhou asks a monk where he has come from, and the monk shouts. Muzhou says he has been shouted at once. The monk shouts again, and Muzhou asks what comes after three or four shouts. The monk has no answer, and Muzhou strikes him and calls him a thieving phony. The Four Shouts passage was not checked against a printed translation; the account above rests on secondary reports of Watson's rendering.

**Symbols.** The shout (*katsu*, 喝) is a sound with no meaning, so it carries no concept. The sword cuts through, the lion presides, the pole and grass probe whether a visitor is genuine, and the fourth shout is the one that lacks the form of a shout. The strike that follows is a teacher's reply to a gesture that was imitated and not lived.

**Teaching.** Linji's teaching centres on the "true person of no rank" (*mui no shinnin*, 無位真人) who is already present in the body, and on the demand to depend on no external authority: "if you meet the Buddha, kill the Buddha" is the familiar form. The shout is the practice of cutting off conceptual replies. The fourfold structure is a way of testing students in encounter dialogue (*mondō*).

**In a reading.** A direct interruption, at the right or the wrong moment: a plain refusal of the story a person is telling. The card may name a candid word that breaks a cycle, or the reader's own need to be cut off from borrowed authority.

**Pitfall.** Imitation. The monk in case 10 shouts as a performance, and the teacher answers a copy with a stick. Loudness and aggression are not a Zen method. The later tradition warns against empty shouting and against the habit of dismissing others' views.

**Links.** XV — Ordinary Mind: the quiet pole of the same tradition. X — Entering the Market with Open Hands: gentle help, set against the sharp version here. 0 — Mu: another syllable that cuts rather than explains.
